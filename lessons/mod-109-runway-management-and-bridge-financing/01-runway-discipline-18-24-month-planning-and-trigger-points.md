# Runway Discipline — 18-24 Month Planning, Monthly Re-Forecast, and the 12 / 9 / 6-Month Trigger Points

## Why this matters

At `startup-foundations` (level 10) the founder learned the four-question runway slice: what is our cash balance, what is our monthly burn, when do we run out, are we default alive or default dead. That is enough to keep a company from missing payroll. It is not enough to keep the same company from *arriving at the fundraise conversation with no time left*. The founder-numbers slice is a status check. The CFO-grade discipline is a **planning frame** with pre-committed trigger points that force conversations at the exact moment they are still cheap.

The gap between "we have nine months of runway" and "we ran out because we didn't act on that number at month nine" is where most of the reasons companies die live. The CFO's job is to close that gap by installing a **standing runway conversation** with the CEO — anchored on the model, refreshed monthly against actuals, and equipped with pre-agreed triggers that no longer require re-litigating "is it time to worry" every month.

## The 18-24 month planning frame

**Why 18-24 months.** Every priced-round fundraise process takes 6-9 months end-to-end (pipeline construction, first meetings, deep diligence, term sheet, definitive documents, close). Any responsible plan therefore needs to leave enough time to *raise*, not just *survive*. A company that opens its Series-B raise with 12 months of runway is in the middle of the market; a company that opens with 9 months is in the "you need to raise this quarter" band that lead investors treat as leverage; a company that opens with 6 months is priced against distress. 18-24 months of forward planning is the industry-standard frame because 18 months lets you close a raise before you cross into the 9-month leverage band, and 24 months lets you actively decide *not* to raise if the market or the KPI signal is bad.

**The three planning documents.** The 18-24 month frame is not one spreadsheet. It is three linked artifacts, all owned by the CFO and all updated on the same monthly cadence:

- **The **base-case operating model** — the driver-based three-statement model from [mod-103](../mod-103-three-statement-model-and-driver-based-forecasting/), with the current-year budget locked and the following 12 months rolling. This is the "plan of record" that the board approves and that the finance team executes against.
- **The **scenario / sensitivity heatmap** — the two-variable analysis over the two levers that dominate the cash-out date (usually net-new-ARR growth × gross-margin, or hiring pace × payback period), producing the range of plausible runway outcomes and the specific KPI thresholds at which the base-case breaks.
- **The **capital plan** — the memo describing how the next raise will be structured: target round size, target milestone bar, target investor pool, backup instruments (bridge, venture debt, RBF), and the trigger points at which each backup activates. This is the document the CFO writes for the CEO and the board that translates "we have 18 months of runway" into "here is what we do at month N."

A company that has all three of these current every month is planning at CFO grade. A company that only has the first is planning at founder grade and will discover the missing pieces the first time the model diverges from the actuals.

## The monthly re-forecast — actuals vs. plan

The discipline that keeps the planning frame honest is the **monthly re-forecast against actuals**. On a defined cadence — typically the finance team closes the books by business day 5, and the re-forecast pack lands on the CEO's and lead investors' desks by business day 10 — the CFO produces:

- **Actuals vs. plan for the month** — revenue, gross margin, opex by category, EBITDA, cash burn, ending cash, headcount. Every line reconciled to the general ledger; every variance over a threshold (typically ±10% of budget or ±$X absolute) annotated.
- **Actuals vs. plan year-to-date** — the running variance and the trend of the variance. A single month of unfavourable variance is noise; three consecutive months of the same-signed variance is a signal.
- **The re-forecast** — the updated forward view given what has just happened. If the last three months showed 15% below-plan net-new-ARR, the re-forecast rolls that trend forward and shows the new cash-out date, the new default-alive / default-dead classification, and the new distance to each trigger point.
- **KPI dashboard reconciled to the model** — the same KPIs the board sees quarterly (ARR, net-new-ARR, NRR, gross margin, burn multiple, magic number, headcount, CAC, CAC payback) refreshed off the same actuals used for the re-forecast, so that the operating KPI story and the finance story are the same story.

The critical property of the re-forecast is that the forward view is anchored on **actuals**, not on the base-plan aspiration. It is common for early-stage founders to keep re-plugging the aspirational plan every month and being surprised when the runway shortens; the CFO's discipline is to roll actuals forward with the current run-rate and to force the conversation about "how do we get back on plan" or "we're not going to, and here's the consequence" every month.

The re-forecast produces one number that matters above all others: the **updated cash-out date**. Every trigger below is defined as a distance from that date. That is the number the CEO checks first when opening the re-forecast pack.

## The trigger points — 12 / 9 / 6 months out

Pre-agreeing what happens at each trigger is the CFO's leverage. Once triggers are set at the board level (or with the lead investor and the CEO, at least), the CFO does not have to re-litigate whether the next conversation is warranted; the calendar and the cash-out date have already decided.

**12 months out — scenario-plan activation warning.** When the re-forecast shows the cash-out date is 12 months forward or less, the CFO's next monthly pack must include an **activated scenario plan**: two or three concrete alternative operating plans covering the runway-extension menu. Typical scenarios: (a) hold the plan and raise on schedule; (b) throttle hiring and G&A to extend runway by 3-6 months at the cost of a slower revenue ramp; (c) begin exploring bridge and venture-debt options as insurance. This is *not* the moment to activate any of the alternatives; it is the moment to have them documented and ready. The pack goes to the CEO, and — in a well-governed company — to the board or the lead investor as a heads-up.

The signal the 12-month trigger sends is deliberate: the CFO is saying "we are still on plan, but we are close enough that the plans have to be real, not sketches." Founders sometimes push back on this ("we're fine, we'll raise") — the CFO's job is to keep the memo on the record so that the trigger fires anyway. In practice, most companies that miss their runway do so because they treated the 12-month warning as premature.

**9 months out — narrowing to two or three concrete plans.** When the re-forecast shows the cash-out date is 9 months forward or less, the scenarios narrow. One is committed to as the primary path (usually: "open the raise now"). One is developed in detail as the plan B (usually: "insider-led bridge from the existing lead") with a rough term sheet drafted and an informal conversation opened with the relevant existing investor. One or two others are held as further contingencies (venture debt, headcount reduction, RBF).

The CFO produces a **narrowing memo** that goes to the CEO and the board. Key contents: (a) which plan is being pursued as primary; (b) what the specific decision points are on the plan B path (when the bridge conversation opens, what the trigger for closing it is, what dilution range the founder has pre-agreed to accept); (c) what specific KPI or funding milestone has to happen by when in order to hold the primary path. The board receives this at the regular quarterly meeting if the cadence lines up, or as an interim board memo if not.

The 9-month trigger is where most of the meaningful choices actually get made. At 12 months there is still full optionality; at 6 months the market is already treating the company as distressed. 9 months is the last band in which the CFO is negotiating from strength.

**6 months out — plan-B activation.** When the re-forecast shows the cash-out date is 6 months forward or less, the plan B activates. The insider bridge conversation moves to actual term-sheet drafting. The venture-debt facility is drawn or the standing bank line is closed. Headcount actions, if part of the plan, are executed. The primary fundraise can continue in parallel, but the plan-B closing becomes the operating anchor.

A 6-month trigger that is *not* accompanied by a plan-B activation is a governance failure. The CFO who sits on a 6-month cash-out with no capital plan closing behind it has almost always misread the runway or been unable to convince the CEO of the reality; either way, the escalation is to the board.

**3 months out — emergency.** Below 3 months, the company is operating under distress. Every option has re-priced against the company. Venture-debt lenders will withdraw, RBF providers will re-underwrite, and the insider bridge will come at terms materially worse than the equivalent conversation would have been three months earlier. This is not a designed trigger — it is the failure state. A CFO who lets the company arrive here without a specific and pre-agreed distressed-financing path (typically: an emergency insider recap with pay-to-play, per chapter 3) has run out of the tools this module provides. ## The CFO-CEO runway conversation

The founder-alone version of the runway conversation is a monthly check-in against the cash-out date, usually done by the founder to themselves in a spreadsheet. The CFO-CEO version is different. It is a **standing** conversation with defined participants, a defined agenda, and defined outputs.

**Cadence.** Monthly, timed to arrive on the same day the re-forecast pack is delivered. Typically 45-60 minutes. If a board meeting is imminent, extend to 90 minutes and treat as the board-meeting prep. Missing the meeting because "everything is fine" is the failure mode; the meeting exists precisely so that "everything is fine" is *stated*, not assumed.

**Participants.** CEO, CFO. Optionally COO / CRO if the KPI variance is on their functions and needs joint discussion. Do not include the board here — the board conversation happens quarterly on the same content, but the internal conversation needs to be honest without external observation.

**Agenda.**
- Actuals vs. plan for the closed month, top three variances by dollar and by narrative.
- Updated cash-out date and distance to each trigger.
- Any trigger that has fired this month or is on track to fire in the next 60-90 days.
- The one specific decision or non-decision the CFO is escalating to the CEO this month.
- The one specific ask the CEO has of the CFO before next month.

**Outputs.**
- The re-forecast pack as-of the meeting (already produced).
- A short memo (half a page) capturing decisions and asks, filed in the standard board-adjacent memo directory.
- Any needed updates to the capital plan document.

The critical property of the standing conversation is that it is a **honest-signal** discipline. The CFO's job is to bring the number, name the trend, and force the choice; the CEO's job is to decide. Both jobs fail if the conversation devolves into narrative comfort. ## Common CFO-side failure modes

- **Modelling on aspiration, not on actuals.** The re-forecast that keeps re-plugging the plan-of-record every month always shows plenty of runway. The one that rolls actuals forward shows the truth. Discipline: use actuals for the trailing three months as the baseline; only revert to plan when a specific event (e.g., a large new deal that closes) justifies it.
- **Missing a re-forecast month "because nothing changed."** Even in flat months, the re-forecast is the discipline that keeps the trigger clock accurate. The month you skip is invariably the month a variance would have surfaced.
- **Letting trigger points slide "because we're close to closing."** The bridge conversation that "will close next month" for four consecutive months is a signal, not an update. The CFO's discipline is to fire the trigger anyway and let the plan-B activation continue in parallel with the "any-day-now" primary raise.
- **Not documenting the trigger commitments upstream.** Triggers that live in the CFO's head are not triggers. Triggers that are agreed with the CEO, memorialised in the capital-plan memo, and reviewed at the last board meeting are triggers. If the CEO disagrees when the trigger fires, the pre-commitment is what lets the CFO hold the line.
- **Treating the 12-month warning as premature.** The most common failure mode. By the time the "warning" feels warranted, the company is already inside the 9-month leverage band. Fire the 12-month trigger on the calendar, not on the feel.

## What good looks like

A CFO or fractional CFO who has this material installed:

- Maintains a driver-based operating model, a scenario / sensitivity heatmap, and a capital plan memo — all current within the last calendar month.
- Delivers a monthly re-forecast pack to the CEO and lead investor by business day 10 of the following month.
- Has documented 12 / 9 / 6-month triggers agreed with the CEO and the board, and does not require re-litigation when a trigger fires.
- Runs a standing 45-60 minute monthly CFO-CEO runway conversation with defined agenda and short-memo outputs.
- Opens the plan-B capital-instrument conversations (bridge lead, venture-debt lender, RBF provider) at the 9-month trigger, not at the 6-month one.
- Escalates to the board when the CEO is not acting on a fired trigger; does not sit on the number.

## Summary

- The founder-numbers slice from Foundations is a status check; the CFO discipline is a planning frame with pre-committed triggers.
- The 18-24 month frame is anchored on three linked documents: the operating model, the scenario / sensitivity heatmap, and the capital plan memo — all refreshed on the same monthly cadence.
- The monthly re-forecast against actuals is the discipline that keeps the frame honest. The forward view rolls actuals, not aspiration. The output is one number that matters most: the updated cash-out date.
- The 12 / 9 / 6-month triggers force pre-agreed conversations at the exact moment they are still cheap. 12 = scenario warning; 9 = narrowing to primary and plan B; 6 = plan-B activation. Below 3 months is the failure state, not a designed trigger.
- The standing monthly CFO-CEO runway conversation is where the number becomes a decision. It is a defined-participants, defined-agenda, defined-output meeting, not a periodic catch-up.
