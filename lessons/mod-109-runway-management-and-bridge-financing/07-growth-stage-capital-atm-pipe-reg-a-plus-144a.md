# Growth-Stage Capital Instruments — ATM, PIPE, Reg A+, and Rule 144A

## Why this matters

By the time a company is a late-stage growth or newly-public entity, the capital menu expands well past traditional preferred-stock rounds and venture debt. **At-the-market (ATM) offerings**, **private investments in public equity (PIPEs)**, **Regulation A+ Tier-2 "mini-IPO" offerings**, and **Rule 144A private placements to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs)** join the choice set. Each is a distinct US-securities-law instrument with a specific regulatory pathway, a specific investor base, and a specific fit range. A pre-IPO or newly-public CFO needs to know all four well enough to evaluate them against a traditional Series-C or crossover round and to defend the choice to the board.

None of these instruments belongs at seed or Series-A. All of them become relevant at the late-Series-C / crossover / pre-IPO / newly-public transition where the company is preparing for or has crossed into the public-markets universe. The specific transaction execution work — running the ATM programme, closing the PIPE, filing the Reg A+ Form 1-A — is often significantly delegated to bankers, counsel, and specialised advisors, but the **selection decision** and the **structural understanding** live with the CFO.

## The four instruments — quick orientation

**ATM offering.** A post-IPO company files a shelf registration and periodically sells small blocks of newly-issued stock into the public market at prevailing prices, via a distribution agent (typically an investment bank). Used to raise incremental capital without a discrete secondary offering.

**PIPE (private investment in public equity).** A discrete negotiated sale of equity (common, preferred convertible, or convertible debt) by a public company to one or more accredited or institutional investors, in a private placement exempt from registration under Reg D. Also used by pre-IPO companies immediately prior to a SPAC merger or as a "de-SPAC PIPE" to anchor the transaction.

**Reg A+ Tier 2 "mini-IPO."** A public offering under Regulation A+ Tier 2 (formerly "Reg A" as reformed by the JOBS Act's Title IV), permitting up to $75M in offering proceeds in a 12-month period, with SEC review of a Form 1-A rather than the more onerous Form S-1 registration. Used as an intermediate public-markets entry point below a full IPO.

**Rule 144A private placement.** A resale exemption under Rule 144A allowing sales of restricted securities to qualified institutional buyers (QIBs — sophisticated institutions with $100M+ in securities under management). Used by pre-IPO companies to raise capital privately from institutional investors on IPO-style terms, and by public companies to issue convertible debt privately to institutions.

## ATM offerings in depth

**Mechanics.** The public company files (or has already effective) a **shelf registration statement (Form S-3)** covering a defined aggregate dollar amount of securities. The company enters an **equity distribution agreement** with one or more distribution agents (typically the company's investment-banking coverage broker). Under the agreement, the company can instruct the agent from time to time to sell defined amounts of newly-issued common stock into the market at prevailing prices ("at the market"). Sales settle through the ordinary secondary market; the company receives net proceeds less the agent's commission (typically 1-3% of gross proceeds).

**S-3 eligibility.** To file a shelf, the issuer must be S-3 eligible — broadly, a public company with at least 12 months of Exchange Act reporting history, meeting the minimum public float threshold ($75M for "primary" issuances by non-well-known seasoned issuers; separate rules for WKSIs). A newly-IPO'd company can file its first S-3 12 months after listing.

**When to use.**

- The company wants to raise incremental capital opportunistically as the stock price permits, without a discrete secondary offering that would signal a specific capital need.
- The company can absorb the dilution incrementally, avoiding the concentrated share overhang of a large marketed follow-on.
- The market price is at or above a level the company would accept for capital.
- The company has enough public float and daily trading volume to absorb the ATM sales without pressuring the price.

**Advantages.** No underwriter discount (only agent commission, which is much smaller). Timing flexibility (sell when the price supports it, pause when it does not). No specific-transaction disclosure trigger (although the ATM programme itself is disclosed at establishment).

**Disadvantages.** Dilution is real and cumulative; multiple ATM tranches can add up to a large aggregate issuance without the market-facing "we are doing a follow-on" moment. Insider-trading restrictions apply (the CFO cannot sell during blackout windows or when in possession of MNPI). Best-execution rules and Regulation M restrictions apply to the sales. The programme requires operational infrastructure (agent monitoring, sales instructions, disclosure controls) that a small newly-public company may not have.

**Board and disclosure.** Board authorises the shelf and the equity distribution agreement. Each sale under the programme is disclosed in the company's periodic reports (10-Q, 10-K). Establishment of a significant ATM programme is often disclosed by 8-K. Some issuers pre-disclose an anticipated aggregate ATM tranche in earnings calls to reduce surprise.

## PIPEs in depth

**Mechanics.** The company negotiates a discrete equity sale to one or more accredited investors under a Reg D exemption (typically Rule 506(b) or 506(c) — see [mod-105 chapter 6](../mod-105-convertible-instruments/06-reg-d-exemptions-504-506b-506c-and-form-d.md)). The security may be common stock (often at a discount to the current public market price), convertible preferred, or convertible debt. Definitive documents include a securities purchase agreement, a registration rights agreement (committing the company to register the resale of the PIPE-purchased shares under an S-3 or S-1 within a defined period), and any specific investor rights (board observer, information rights, standstill).

**Public-company PIPE.** For a listed public company, the PIPE typically prices at a discount to the current market price (5-25% is common), reflecting the illiquidity between purchase and effectiveness of the resale registration. The transaction is disclosed under 8-K within four business days.

**De-SPAC PIPE.** Historically a common feature of SPAC (special-purpose acquisition company) transactions: institutional investors commit to buy shares of the SPAC (or the combined post-merger company) at the merger close, anchoring the deal against redemption risk from SPAC public shareholders. De-SPAC PIPE volume was substantial in 2020-2021, contracted sharply in 2022-2023 as the SPAC-de-SPAC market cooled, and has continued at reduced levels since.

**Pre-IPO / late-stage PIPE.** A late-stage private company can raise a "crossover" round from a mix of traditional VCs, mutual-fund crossover investors (Fidelity, T. Rowe Price, Wellington, Baillie Gifford), and hedge funds, structured similarly to a public-company PIPE but at pre-IPO pricing. This crossover investor base often stays with the company through the IPO and provides an IPO-book anchor.

**Common features.**

- **Registration rights.** The PIPE investors expect the company to file a resale registration statement within a defined period post-close (typically 30-90 days), and to keep it effective until the shares can be freely traded under Rule 144.
- **Standstill.** Frequently the PIPE investors agree to a standstill (no additional purchases, no proxy contest, no strategic-position building) for a defined period.
- **Information rights.** Institutional PIPE investors sometimes negotiate information rights and rarely board observer seats.
- **Anti-dilution or price-protection.** Some PIPEs (particularly convertible-security PIPEs) include price adjustment or reset mechanics — the CFO must model these carefully, especially in a stressed market.

**When to use.**

- The company needs a discrete, large capital infusion that ATM sales cannot deliver in the required window.
- The company can identify institutional investors with genuine long-hold appetite (mutual funds, crossover funds, well-known sector specialists) that will provide reputational anchor as well as capital.
- The market price is depressed relative to the company's own view but a discount-priced PIPE is achievable.
- The board is comfortable disclosing the discrete capital raise (8-K disclosure with size, price, and use of proceeds).

**Disadvantages.** Immediate 8-K disclosure of the transaction and its price signals to the market that the company raised at a discount. Registration-rights obligations create ongoing compliance work. Convertible-security PIPEs with reset mechanics can be dilutive well past the initially-priced amount if the stock declines.

## Reg A+ Tier 2 in depth

**Mechanics.** The company files a **Form 1-A** with the SEC. The filing includes an offering circular (simplified version of a Form S-1 prospectus) and audited financial statements (typically two years of audited financials, less onerous than the S-1 requirement of three years for larger issuers). SEC staff review the Form 1-A and provide comments; the company responds; upon SEC qualification, the offering can proceed.

**Tier 2 specifics.**

- Maximum offering size: **$75M** in a 12-month period (raised from $50M in March 2021).
- Ongoing reporting: annual audited financials on Form 1-K, semi-annual reports on Form 1-SA, current reports on Form 1-U — a lighter-touch analogue to the 10-K / 10-Q / 8-K regime.
- No state-securities (blue-sky) registration required — pre-empted by federal law.
- Testing-the-waters permitted before and after Form 1-A filing.
- Investment limits for non-accredited investors: no more than 10% of the greater of annual income or net worth.

**When to use.**

- The company wants a public-markets entry that is less onerous than a full S-1 IPO — smaller size ($75M cap), lighter ongoing reporting, no blue-sky compliance.
- The company can market to a retail-and-institutional mixed base and has an authentic story that will resonate with retail (consumer brand, community-based product, mission-aligned company).
- The company can sustain the ongoing 1-K / 1-SA / 1-U reporting cadence.

**Disadvantages.** $75M cap limits usefulness at the largest scales. Retail-heavy shareholder base can be less strategic than institutional. Ongoing reporting requirements are still substantial. Reg A+ market has been used more by consumer / mission-aligned companies than by growth-tech; a tech CFO evaluating Reg A+ needs to weigh whether the retail-heavy investor mix fits.

**Historical usage.** Reg A+ Tier 2 was reformed in the JOBS Act (2015) and expanded to $75M in 2021. Notable users include a handful of consumer, real-estate, and community-oriented issuers; the volume has never approached that of traditional S-1 IPOs.

## Rule 144A in depth

**Mechanics.** Rule 144A is a Securities Act safe harbour for resale of restricted securities to **qualified institutional buyers (QIBs)** — institutions that own and invest on a discretionary basis at least $100M in securities of non-affiliated issuers ($10M for registered broker-dealers, in most cases). Under Rule 144A, a broker-dealer can purchase securities from an issuer (typically in a Reg D private placement) and then resell them to QIBs without registration.

**Common uses.**

- **Convertible-debt issuances by public companies.** A public company issues convertible notes in a Rule 144A placement — bankers underwrite and sell to QIB accounts. Common for growth-stage public companies that want convertible-debt financing without the Rule 144 six-month resale hold.
- **Pre-IPO institutional private placements.** A late-stage private company raises from institutional investors under Reg D, with a 144A-compliant resale market for the securities (typically via an unregistered "144A trading" venue).
- **International offerings.** Rule 144A is frequently combined with Regulation S (offshore-offerings exemption) for cross-border issuances — the "144A / Reg S" structure permits sales to US QIBs and to non-US persons offshore in one transaction.

**When to use.**

- The issuer wants institutional capital and can accept the QIB-only resale market.
- The security is convertible debt or preferred where an institutional-only market is efficient.
- The issuer wants the flexibility to sell to institutions without registering the securities for public trading.

**Disadvantages.** QIB-only market restricts the resale universe. Not appropriate for retail-directed offerings. Requires banker underwriting and QIB placement — the transaction execution cost is closer to a public offering than to a friends-and-family placement.

## The comparison — the CFO's evaluation memo

For a growth-stage company evaluating these instruments against a traditional Series-C or crossover round, the CFO's memo produces a comparison across the following dimensions:

- **Size.** Traditional Series-C: $30-100M+. ATM (post-IPO only): incremental, cumulative up to shelf capacity. PIPE: $10-200M+ typical. Reg A+ Tier 2: up to $75M in 12 months. 144A: highly variable.
- **Cost.** Series-C: no explicit cost, dilution priced against valuation. ATM: 1-3% agent commission. PIPE: no explicit cost but priced at discount to current market. Reg A+: SEC-review costs, banker fees (often smaller than IPO), qualification legal work. 144A: banker fees comparable to public offering (often 3-7%).
- **Regulatory friction.** Series-C: low (private, Reg D). ATM: modest (shelf maintenance, periodic disclosure). PIPE: modest (8-K disclosure, registration-rights obligations). Reg A+: high (Form 1-A review, ongoing 1-K/1-SA/1-U). 144A: modest (Reg D placement, private).
- **Investor base.** Series-C: traditional VC. ATM: retail plus institutional in secondary market. PIPE: institutional (public), sometimes retail (some crossover PIPEs). Reg A+: mixed retail-plus-institutional. 144A: institutional QIB only.
- **Signalling.** Series-C: standard pricing signal. ATM: incremental, easier to absorb. PIPE: discrete signal, discount reveals current market willingness. Reg A+: signals mission / retail orientation. 144A: signals institutional-quality access.
- **Time to close.** Series-C: 3-9 months. ATM: post-establishment, real-time. PIPE: 4-8 weeks. Reg A+: 3-6 months for SEC qualification, then rolling. 144A: 4-8 weeks after banker engagement.
- **Prerequisites.** Series-C: none. ATM: 12+ months as reporting company, S-3 eligibility. PIPE (public): public listing. Reg A+: no listing required. 144A: institutional-quality diligence package.

The choice depends on the specific situation. A newly-public company with a stable share price and modest incremental capital need — ATM. A pre-IPO late-stage private company wanting a crossover anchor before the IPO — PIPE (crossover round). A consumer or community-oriented company wanting a public-markets entry that includes its customers as shareholders — Reg A+ Tier 2. A growth-stage public company issuing convertibles to institutional investors — 144A. A private company continuing with traditional venture financing — Series-C.

## Where these instruments interact with each other

Most late-stage companies do not use these instruments in isolation. Typical stacking patterns:

- **Late-stage private → PIPE crossover → S-1 IPO.** Company raises a crossover round from mutual funds and hedge funds structured similarly to a public PIPE (though technically private under Reg D), then IPOs on the S-1 12-18 months later. The crossover investors form the IPO-book anchor.
- **S-1 IPO → post-IPO ATM.** Company IPOs on the S-1, becomes public, files a shelf 12+ months later, and uses ATM offerings to raise incremental capital opportunistically over subsequent years.
- **S-1 IPO → convertible 144A + capped-call.** Public company raises convertible debt in a 144A placement, hedges the dilution with a capped-call transaction with the underwriters. Common financing pattern for growth tech.
- **Post-IPO ATM + occasional discrete PIPE.** Larger public companies use ATM for incremental capital and occasional discrete PIPEs when a strategic anchor investor is available or when a large discrete raise is required.

## What the CFO produces before recommending one of these instruments

- **The instrument-selection memo.** 2-3 pages. Bottom-line recommendation. Comparison table across the dimensions above (size, cost, friction, base, signal, time, prerequisites). Specific board question the CFO is asking the board to decide.
- **The illustrative model.** Illustrates the transaction: proceeds, cost, resulting cash position, resulting cap-table impact, dilution and per-share metrics (for public companies, EPS impact if material). Multiple scenarios if the instrument has variability (ATM tranches at different prices, PIPE at different discounts).
- **The regulatory-and-execution checklist.** What SEC filings are triggered. What board and shareholder approvals are needed. What ongoing reporting the instrument creates. Timeline to close.
- **The banker / advisor slate.** Which investment bank(s) will run the process, which counsel will draft, what fees are being negotiated.
- **The disclosure plan.** How the transaction will be disclosed (8-K timing and content), how it will be communicated on the next earnings call or investor update, how it will interact with any planned strategic disclosures.

## Common failure modes

- **ATM tranches that cumulatively erode retail confidence.** Even individually-small ATM sales add up. The CFO who does not proactively communicate the ATM programme to the market can find retail selling on the pattern.
- **PIPEs with dilutive reset mechanics.** A PIPE with a "make-whole" reset if the stock declines can be catastrophically dilutive in a bad market. Model it explicitly and understand what a 30-50% stock decline does to the effective dilution.
- **Reg A+ used as a substitute for IPO by companies not suited to a retail-shareholder base.** The retail-heavy Reg A+ base can be less patient than institutional and can pressure the company to hit near-term milestones the company is not ready for.
- **144A convertibles priced without a capped-call hedge.** The dilution on conversion can be material. The capped-call hedge is a cost but reduces the effective dilution.
- **Confusing ATM proceeds with "no dilution."** ATM sales are dilutive by definition. The word "at the market" refers to pricing, not to dilution absence.
- **Treating any of these as "less rigorous" than a traditional IPO.** Each has real securities-law compliance, real disclosure obligations, and real litigation risk. Cutting corners on regulatory work is not the way to save cost.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Knows the four instruments cold — size, cost, friction, investor base, signalling, timing, prerequisites — and can construct the comparison memo in a week.
- Chooses the instrument for the specific situation and defends the choice against the alternatives.
- Works with tier-1 banking and securities counsel on the transaction execution; does not treat any of these as a DIY exercise.
- Models the dilution and disclosure impact accurately, including tail scenarios (ATM cumulative drift, PIPE reset, 144A convertible conversion).
- Coordinates the transaction with the company's broader disclosure and investor-relations plan.
- Recognises the boundary — the M&A and IPO **transaction execution** work lives in [`startup-exit-curriculum`](https://github.com/ai-startup-curriculum/startup-exit-curriculum); this module owns the **selection decision** and the **structural understanding** that anchor the CFO's participation.

## Summary

- Four growth-stage capital instruments join the CFO's menu at the late-Series-C / crossover / pre-IPO / newly-public transition: ATM offerings, PIPEs, Reg A+ Tier 2 offerings, and Rule 144A placements.
- **ATM offerings** — post-IPO shelf-registered incremental sales via a distribution agent; opportunistic, small commission, cumulative dilution, requires S-3 eligibility.
- **PIPEs** — discrete negotiated private placements into public (or pre-IPO) companies; institutional anchor, 8-K disclosure, registration-rights obligations, sometimes with dilutive reset mechanics.
- **Reg A+ Tier 2** — up to $75M public offering with lighter SEC review (Form 1-A) and lighter ongoing reporting (1-K / 1-SA / 1-U); retail-plus-institutional base; suits consumer / mission-aligned issuers more than growth tech.
- **Rule 144A** — private placement into QIBs (institutions with $100M+ under management); common for public-company convertible debt and for pre-IPO institutional rounds; often paired with Reg S for cross-border.
- The CFO's job is the **selection decision** and the **structural understanding** — the execution work lives with bankers, counsel, and (for M&A/IPO transactions themselves) `startup-exit-curriculum`.
- Deliverables: instrument-selection memo with dimension-by-dimension comparison, illustrative model, regulatory-and-execution checklist, banker / advisor slate, disclosure plan.

Chapter 8 closes the module with the discipline that runs above all these instrument selections — the "default alive" cash-planning frame layered on top of the runway plan, and the founder-and-CFO communication practice that anchors the whole ongoing-company capital narrative and hands off to `startup-exit-curriculum` when the capital-management path resolves into a sale, IPO, or wind-down.
