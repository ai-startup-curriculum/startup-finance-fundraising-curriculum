# Exercise 07 — Default-Alive Cash-Plan Authoring

**Estimated time:** ~2.5 hours
**Prerequisites:** Chapter 8 (Default Alive Cash Planning and Founder Communication). Prior familiarity with chapter 1 (runway discipline and the monthly re-forecast) — this exercise assumes the runway model and re-forecast pack already exist and layers the default-alive discipline on top.

## Problem statement

For each of three specified companies at different stages and trajectories, produce the CFO's default-alive-or-default-dead classification with the specific best-case-KPI assumptions that support the classification, name the plan-B path on the same page as the classification, and author the tiered founder-communication artifacts (CEO-CFO monthly-meeting output, quarterly board deck excerpt, monthly investor-update excerpt, quarterly team all-hands script). The point of the drill is to build the muscle for the affirmative declaration chapter 8 requires — no soft classifications, no drift — and to produce the tiered communication set that makes the same underlying reality reach three different audiences without contradicting itself.

## The three companies

Diagnose and produce artifacts for each of the following.

### Company X — Series-A SaaS, on plan, default alive with runway

- ARR $6M growing 8% MoM (steady, on plan since Series-A close 10 months ago).
- NRR 122%, gross margin 79%, CAC payback 15 months.
- Cash on hand $9M, burn $650K/month. On the current trajectory (revenue growth continues, hiring plan proceeds as approved) the company is projected to reach cash-flow break-even in month 26 at ARR ~$18M, with $1M of cash remaining at that crossover. Series-B planned for month 15-18 targeting $50-70M at $250-350M post-money.
- Best-case KPI assumptions: continued 7-9% MoM ARR growth, NRR held above 115%, gross margin held above 77%, hiring plan executed on schedule with a mid-year re-plan check.

### Company Y — Series-B B2B, off plan, default dead

- ARR $14M, ARR growth slowed to 3% MoM over trailing four months (from 6% MoM at Series-B close 20 months ago).
- NRR 108% (weakened from 118% at Series-B), gross margin 71%, CAC payback 26 months.
- Cash on hand $8M, burn $1.3M/month. On the current trajectory the company reaches month-18 with $0 remaining and has not reached break-even. Series-C not on the pipeline in the base case; the CFO estimates 25% probability of closing a flat-to-down Series-C in the next 8 months. Insider bridge from the Series-B lead is available in principle but has not been negotiated.
- Best-case KPI assumptions: even if ARR growth reaccelerates to 5% MoM and CAC payback improves to 22 months, the company still does not reach break-even before cash-out on the current plan.

### Company Z — Growth-stage vertical SaaS, mixed signals, default alive but tight

- ARR $32M growing 4% MoM (on plan; the plan is deliberately more measured than at Series-B).
- NRR 118%, gross margin 75%, CAC payback 20 months.
- Cash on hand $18M, burn $1.6M/month. On the current trajectory the company reaches cash-flow break-even in month 22 at ARR ~$46M, but with only 2 months of cash remaining at crossover. Series-C planned for month 14-18 targeting $75-100M at $400-500M post-money to fund an acquisition and international expansion; if the Series-C does not close, the company can continue and reach break-even but with limited buffer.
- Best-case KPI assumptions: 3.5-4.5% MoM ARR growth, NRR held above 115%, gross margin held above 74%, hiring plan proceeds but is throttleable on a defined trigger.

## Requirements

Produce a single deliverable pack containing the following, with per-company artifact sets.

### Part A — the default-alive classification with best-case KPI assumptions

1. **Classification memo (half a page per company, 3 memos).** For each of Companies X, Y, Z:
   - State the classification: **default alive with runway**, **default alive but tight**, or **default dead**. Use the chapter 8 formal definitions.
   - State the specific best-case KPI assumptions under which the classification holds.
   - State the cross-over month (where cumulative cash flow first goes positive) and the cash remaining at cross-over — or, if default dead, the cash-out month and the specific gap between cash-out and break-even in dollars and in months of additional runway needed.
   - State whether the classification is stable (a modest shock does not flip it) or fragile (a modest shock flips it). Name one specific plausible shock that would flip the classification (a customer loss, a hiring delay, a market downturn).

### Part B — the plan-B path on the same page as the classification

2. **Plan-B path (attached to each classification memo).** For each company, the plan-B path attached to the classification:
   - **Company X (default alive with runway).** Plan A: raise the Series-B on the planned timeline. Plan B: if the Series-B slips or comes at unfavourable terms, the company can slow hiring by 20% to extend runway by 3-6 months; venture debt is available as an insurance option to extend by 6-9 months. Name the specific triggers for each plan-B activation.
   - **Company Y (default dead).** Plan A: close the insider bridge from the Series-B lead in the next 8 weeks (specific: $6-8M SAFE at the Series-B post-money-adjusted cap, extending runway to month 14). Plan B: if the insider bridge does not close, activate a cost-reduction plan (30-40% headcount reduction) extending runway by 8-12 months while pursuing a Series-C or a strategic sale. Plan C: hand off to `startup-exit-curriculum` (sale process) if neither closes.
   - **Company Z (default alive but tight).** Plan A: close the Series-C on the planned timeline. Plan B: if the Series-C slips or is downsized, the acquisition is deferred and the company reverts to organic-plan operating with the tight-but-alive trajectory. Plan C: venture debt as an incremental insurance option to add 6-9 months of runway if the trajectory softens.
   - Name specific dates (or milestone triggers) for each plan-B activation. The dates should be consistent with the chapter 1 trigger discipline (12 / 9 / 6-month cash-out distances).

### Part C — the CEO-CFO monthly-meeting output

3. **Monthly-meeting minutes template (one per company).** A short half-page template capturing:
   - Date and participants (CEO, CFO, sometimes COO / CRO).
   - Classification confirmation for the month (default alive / default alive with runway / default alive but tight / default dead).
   - Any change in classification since the prior month, with the specific driver.
   - Top three variances vs. plan and their default-alive implications.
   - Any trigger that has fired or is projected to fire in the next 60-90 days.
   - The one specific decision or non-decision the CFO is escalating to the CEO this month.
   - The one specific ask the CEO has of the CFO before next month.
   - For Company Y specifically: also includes a section on the bridge-conversation status.
4. **A sample completed set of monthly minutes for a plausible month** for each company — Company X's minutes look calm and repetitive (which is exactly what the discipline requires in stable months); Company Y's minutes are tense and specific; Company Z's minutes track the Series-C readiness against a defined milestone list.

### Part D — the quarterly board-deck excerpt

5. **Quarterly board-deck runway page (one per company).** Half a page (one slide). Includes:
   - Cash on hand, monthly burn, cash-out date, distance to each trigger.
   - The default-alive classification with the best-case-KPI assumptions.
   - The plan-A / plan-B / plan-C paths with named triggers and dates.
   - The KPI dashboard tied to the classification (ARR, NRR, gross margin, burn, headcount, CAC payback).
   - The board question being asked: for Company X, "affirm the classification"; for Company Y, "authorise the bridge conversation and the fallback cost-reduction plan"; for Company Z, "affirm the Series-C target and the plan-B deferral of the acquisition."

### Part E — the monthly investor-update excerpt

6. **Monthly investor-update runway section (one per company).** A paragraph. Framed for the broader existing-investor list (beyond the board). For Company X: "runway of X months on the current plan; on schedule for the Series-B raise this half." For Company Y: "cash on hand of $X; addressing runway with an insider-led bridge conversation in progress; will share terms once negotiated." For Company Z: "runway of X months; Series-C process opening this quarter; well-positioned for a strong outcome on current trajectory."
   - Note: the language is deliberately calibrated — Company Y's "insider-led bridge conversation in progress" is honest without saying "default dead" (which would spook non-board investors). The CFO's judgement on this framing is what the exercise is training.

### Part F — the team-facing quarterly all-hands script

7. **Team all-hands runway segment (one per company).** A short script (100-200 words per company) the founder would use in the quarterly all-hands. The messages differ by company:
   - Company X: brief, confident, forward-looking. "We are on plan, well-funded, and preparing for the Series-B this half."
   - Company Y: honest, focused, does not use the words "default dead" (which would trigger a departure spiral). Frame as "we are raising this quarter, we're focused on the deals we need to close, we're preparing for both a fast close and a slower one."
   - Company Z: measured, positioned as choice-making rather than distress. "The Series-C process is opening this quarter to fund the acquisition and international expansion. If we close it strong, we'll accelerate; if it takes longer, we'll continue the disciplined plan we're on today."
8. **Manager-briefing talking points (one per company).** A short set of talking points line managers can use to answer team questions consistently. For Company Y specifically, this includes managing the "should I look elsewhere" conversation that will happen in some 1:1s.

### Part G — the consistency check

9. **Consistency-check memo (one per company).** A short paragraph confirming that the CEO-CFO monthly meeting output, the board deck, the investor update, and the team all-hands script all tell the same underlying story with audience-appropriate framing. The chapter 8 discipline is that all four artifacts are consistent; the exercise output should demonstrate that.

## Starter guidance

- **Company X is the easy case pedagogically but the most important operationally.** The discipline of running the monthly meeting and confirming default-alive-with-runway explicitly *in stable months* is what makes the discipline stick when the trajectory changes. Do not skip Company X because it is easy.
- **Company Y is the hard case.** The classification memo has to say "default dead" and mean it. The plan-B path has to be specific — not "we'll figure it out." The team-facing communication has to be honest without being panic-inducing. All three artifacts have to hold together.
- **Company Z is the ambiguous case.** "Default alive but tight" is a classification that requires the CFO to walk both the "we're alive on the current plan" story and the "but we're closer to the edge than default-alive-with-runway" story. The board wants the classification affirmed alongside the plan-B options; the team wants confidence without complacency.
- **The best-case KPI assumptions are the CFO's honest baseline.** Do not use "if everything went perfectly" — use "if the current base-case forecast holds." Chapter 8 is specific: on best-case operating KPIs, does the math work out?
- **The plan-B path must live on the same page as the classification.** A classification without a plan B is signalling without action. The board memo, the investor update, and the team script all need the plan-B reference in a form appropriate to the audience.
- **The tiered communication is where CFOs most often fail.** The board hears one story, the investors hear another, the team hears a third — and the three don't reconcile. When an investor talks to a team member or a board director talks to an employee, the inconsistency lands as a trust break. The consistency-check memo is the discipline that makes sure this does not happen.
- **The team-facing communication is the hardest to get right.** Under-communicating creates surprise when reality lands; over-communicating creates a departure spiral. The middle path (Company Y's script) requires care. Read the chapter 8 discussion carefully and calibrate to the specific company.
- **The manager-briefing talking points are the operational lever for the team communication.** The founder gives the all-hands script; the managers repeat it in 1:1s. If the managers are not briefed, they either invent their own version (inconsistent) or say nothing (silent, which reads as concealment).
- **The specific date discipline in the plan-B path is chapter 1's discipline layered in.** The 12 / 9 / 6-month triggers from chapter 1 anchor when each plan-B option activates.

## Acceptance criteria

- **Each of the three companies has a classification memo** with a specific best-case-KPI assumption set and a stability-vs-fragility assessment.
- **Each classification is paired with a specific plan-B path** with named triggers and dates.
- **Each company has a CEO-CFO monthly-meeting minutes template plus one sample completed set.**
- **Each company has a quarterly board-deck runway page** with the classification, the plan-B paths, and the specific board question.
- **Each company has a monthly investor-update runway section** with audience-appropriate framing.
- **Each company has a team-facing all-hands script plus manager-briefing talking points.**
- **Each company has a consistency-check memo confirming the four communication tiers reconcile.**
- **Company Y's artifacts include the "default dead" declaration explicitly** in the internal artifacts (classification memo, CEO-CFO minutes, board deck) but frame the team-facing and investor-update artifacts in the chapter-8-recommended middle-path language.

- Three CEO-CFO monthly-meeting templates plus completed sample sets (Markdown or PDF).
- Three quarterly board-deck runway pages (Markdown or slide export).
- Three monthly investor-update runway sections (Markdown).

## Extensions (optional)

- **Trajectory-change simulation.** For Company X, assume that in month 5 the ARR growth drops from 8% MoM to 4% MoM for three consecutive months. Rerun the classification, the plan-B path, and the four-tier communication. Show the *change* memo the CFO delivers to the CEO explaining what has flipped.
- **Bridge-close simulation for Company Y.** Assume the insider bridge closes six weeks after Plan A activation. Produce the post-bridge classification, plan-B, and communication artifacts, showing the trajectory now runs to month 14 and needs the Series-C conversation to be re-opened by then.
- **Handoff simulation for Company Y.** Assume the insider bridge does not close, the cost-reduction plan is activated and cuts headcount by 35%, and 6 weeks later the CFO concludes that the surviving-plan trajectory is not viable and the sale-process handoff to `startup-exit-curriculum` is warranted. Produce: the CFO's transition memo to the board; the specific banker-engagement question the CEO is now facing; the founder-communication practice at handoff per chapter 8 (WARN Act notice planning, retention design, team all-hands script for the handoff moment).
- **Series-C-slip simulation for Company Z.** Assume the Series-C slips by 6 months and downsizes from $75-100M to $30-40M. Rerun the classification, the plan-B path (acquisition deferred; incremental venture-debt draw layered in), and the four-tier communication.
- **Longitudinal artifact set.** Produce the six-month set of monthly minutes for Company Y, showing how the classification evolves (default dead in month 1; default dead with bridge-conversation-active in month 2; default alive but tight post-bridge close in month 3; and so on). The artifact set should show the discipline compounding.
- **Multi-company CFO comparison.** Assume the same CFO runs all three companies as a fractional CFO. Produce the CFO's own artifact — a one-page cross-company runway summary — that the CFO uses to keep the three classifications distinct and prevents the discipline from any one company from bleeding into another.
