# Pre-Revenue Anchor Methods — Berkus, Payne Scorecard, and Risk-Factor Summation

## Why this matters

A pre-revenue company cannot be valued by anything that looks like a valuation. There is no revenue to multiply, no cash flow to discount, and no comparable transaction in which an identical asset was priced in an arm's-length market. What exists is a founder, a founding team, an idea, some early product work, and a claim about a future market. None of those are financial statements. Any number produced from them is a **bracket** — a defensible range within which the round should price — not a **price**.

Angel investors and pre-seed VCs have converged on three methods for producing that bracket. Each was published between the mid-1990s and the mid-2000s, each was designed for exactly this situation, and each is still in active use by real angel groups because nothing better has replaced them. A finance leader supporting a founder through an angel or pre-seed round needs to be able to run each of them, understand what each is anchored to, and produce a triangulated bracket that the founder can defend inside an angel-group screen or a pre-seed partner meeting.

The three methods:

- **The Berkus Method** — Dave Berkus's five-component addition, with each component contributing 0 to $500K to a maximum ceiling of roughly $2.5M pre-money (see the "vintage" caveat below).
- **The Payne Scorecard Method** — Bill Payne's weighted comparison of the target company against a regional average pre-money for comparable early-stage rounds.
- **Risk-factor summation** — a checklist adjustment (typically 12 factors) that starts from a regional pre-revenue average and adds or subtracts a discrete dollar amount per factor.

Each method is a heuristic. Each has a specific perspective it was built from — Berkus from a Southern California angel investor's checkbook decision, Payne from the Kauffman-supported angel-group training curriculum, risk-factor summation from the Ohio TechAngel Fund's methodology cited in Payne's own writing. All three are documented in Bill Payne's *The Definitive Guide to Raising Money from Angels* and in the Angel Capital Association's practitioner materials (see [`resources.md`](resources.md)).

## What "pre-revenue" actually means for valuation

Pre-revenue in this chapter means a company that has not yet reached the point at which revenue can support a defensible forward projection. Practically that covers three overlapping categories:

- **Idea stage / pre-product.** No product in market, possibly no company incorporated, sometimes just a founding team with a deck.
- **Pre-launch / pre-first-customer.** Product exists (or is close), no paying customers, no revenue to model.
- **Post-launch / first-cheque revenue.** A handful of customers, revenue exists in a P&L, but the sample is far too small and the churn behaviour far too unknown for a forward projection to survive scrutiny.

All three sit inside the pre-revenue-anchor-methods regime. The transition out of that regime happens when the company has enough cohort data that a revenue-multiple or DCF approach becomes defensible — typically post-Series-A, sometimes late seed for capital-efficient companies with fast unit-economics validation. Until then, the anchor methods are the tools.

The transition matters for a specific reason: if the founder uses a revenue-multiple pitch in a pre-revenue round ("we'll be at $10M ARR in year three, so at 10× the company is worth $100M today"), the pitch reads as unserious to any experienced investor. A defensible pre-revenue conversation is the anchor-method bracket plus the VC method (chapter 2) plus a market-conditions read (chapters 6-7). It is not a forward-revenue multiple.

## The Berkus Method

**Origin.** Dave Berkus, an angel investor active in the Los Angeles / Pasadena area since the late 1980s, formalised the method in the mid-1990s. Berkus's own retrospective is on his blog at [berkonomics.com](https://berkonomics.com/) (see [`resources.md`](resources.md)). He also inflation-adjusts the ceiling periodically in his own writing, but the "classic" ceiling most angel groups still use is $2.5M pre-money.

**Structure.** Berkus divides the value of a pre-revenue startup into five discrete "value drivers." Each is scored from $0 to $500,000 by the angel making the investment. The five components sum to the Berkus pre-money valuation, with the natural ceiling at 5 × $500K = **$2.5M pre-money**.

The five components in the classic formulation:

| Component | What it measures |
|---|---|
| Sound idea (basic value, product risk) | Is the underlying idea sound and defensible? Is there a real problem being solved? |
| Prototype (reduces technology risk) | Does a working prototype exist that demonstrates the technology works? |
| Quality management team (reduces execution risk) | Is there a credible founding team with domain and execution experience? |
| Strategic relationships (reduces market risk) | Are there real partnerships, LOIs, or channel arrangements that de-risk go-to-market? |
| Product rollout or sales (reduces production risk) | Has the product actually been in the hands of paying customers? |

Each of the five is a **risk-reduction** framing. The idea is that the pre-revenue company is a bag of risks — technology, execution, market, production — and each of the five components represents progress against one of those risks. A company that has all five well-covered gets close to the $2.5M ceiling. A company that has only one or two of the five gets a fraction of it.

**Worked example.**

- Target company: an early-stage SaaS company with two technical co-founders, a working alpha prototype, no paying customers, no strategic partnerships yet, and a "sound" identified problem.
- Sound idea: **$400K** (the problem is real and the technical approach is credible).
- Prototype: **$450K** (working alpha, functional but not scaled).
- Team: **$300K** (technical co-founders with domain experience but no prior startup exit).
- Strategic relationships: **$0** (none yet).
- Product rollout / customers: **$0** (no paying customers).
- **Berkus pre-money = $1.15M.**

**The right framing.** The Berkus number is not a *price*; it is Berkus's own explicit language that the five components represent "reasonable amounts of value" contributed by each area of progress. Two angels applying Berkus to the same company will produce different numbers because the per-component score is subjective. Berkus's stated goal in publishing the method was to give the investor a structured checklist rather than a defensible market clearing price. The founder using Berkus in the round conversation should present it the same way — as a structured checklist that the investor can push back on component-by-component, not as a spreadsheet-computed pre-money.

**When Berkus is useful.**

- The angel-group screen where the investor has to defend a valuation to a partnership of other angels. Berkus's five components are legible to a non-quantitative audience.
- The very-early round (idea to prototype) where none of the revenue-based methods apply at all.
- Companies where the "risk-reduction" framing lines up with how the investor is actually diagnosing the deal — where the investor is fundamentally asking "what has been de-risked?" and the founder needs to answer in those terms.

**When Berkus is a poor fit.**

- Companies whose pre-money valuation the market is already pricing higher than $2.5M (which today is a large fraction of pre-seed rounds — see [chapter 7](07-carta-state-of-private-markets.md) on Carta's pre-seed data). The ceiling is anchored to a 1990s angel-cheque environment; in a market where pre-seed rounds routinely close above $5M pre-money, the Berkus ceiling is a floor at best.
- Companies pitching to institutional VCs whose partnerships underwrite off target-return math (chapter 2), not off value-driver checklists.

**How to think about the "ceiling has been inflation-adjusted" debate.** Berkus himself has publicly discussed inflating the per-component cap to $600K or $1M in his own writing, which would move the ceiling to $3M or $5M. Different angel groups use different vintages. What matters for the founder using the method is to **name the vintage you are using** ("Berkus at $500K per component, $2.5M ceiling — the 1996 formulation," or "Berkus at $1M per component, $5M ceiling — Berkus's later inflation-adjusted formulation"), rather than treating the numbers as fixed for all time.

## The Payne Scorecard Method

**Origin.** Bill Payne, a serial angel investor and past chair of the Angel Capital Association, published the scorecard method in the 2000s. It is documented in *The Definitive Guide to Raising Money from Angels* (Payne, 2011) and in Payne's own writing at [billpayne.com](http://billpayne.com/) (see [`resources.md`](resources.md)). The Kauffman Foundation's angel-investor education programme incorporated the scorecard into its standard curriculum.

**Structure.** The scorecard starts from an **industry- and region-average pre-money valuation** for comparable early-stage rounds. That baseline number is not something you invent — you pull it from a recent angel-round data source (regional angel-group reports, Angel Capital Association HALO Report, PitchBook data for pre-seed rounds in your geography and vertical, or CB Insights data for the same).

You then rate the target company against that regional average across a set of weighted factors. Payne's canonical factors, with the weights he used in his own scorecard, are:

| Factor | Weight |
|---|---|
| Strength of the management team | 0-30% |
| Size of the opportunity | 0-25% |
| Product / technology | 0-15% |
| Competitive environment | 0-10% |
| Marketing / sales channels / partnerships | 0-10% |
| Need for additional investment | 0-5% |
| Other (customer feedback, etc.) | 0-5% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

For each factor, you rate the target company against the regional-average benchmark: 100% means "the target is at the regional average on this factor"; a rating below 100% means the target is below-average; a rating above 100% means the target is above-average. Payne's own writing gives 50%-200% as the reasonable range for individual factors.

You then compute a weighted multiplier by summing the (weight × rating) across all factors. The Payne pre-money is the regional-average pre-money × the weighted multiplier.

**Worked example.**

Assume the regional-average pre-money for a comparable early-stage SaaS round in the target's geography is **$4.0M** (this is a plug — you look it up from a real dataset). The founder-CFO rates the target as follows:

| Factor | Weight | Rating | Contribution (weight × rating) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Management team | 30% | 125% (strong technical team with domain expertise, one prior exit) | 0.375 |
| Opportunity size | 25% | 150% (large TAM, clearly-articulated wedge) | 0.375 |
| Product / technology | 15% | 100% (product works, no unusual technical moat) | 0.150 |
| Competitive environment | 10% | 75% (crowded space with well-funded incumbents) | 0.075 |
| Sales channels / partnerships | 10% | 80% (few partnerships to date) | 0.080 |
| Need for additional investment | 5% | 100% (round-size and follow-on need are typical) | 0.050 |
| Other | 5% | 100% | 0.050 |
| **Weighted multiplier** | 100% | | **1.155** |

Payne pre-money = $4.0M × 1.155 = **$4.62M**.

**The right framing.** The scorecard's core anchor is the regional-average pre-money. That number is the load-bearing input; the multiplier is a modest adjustment around it (typically 0.5x-2.0x, sometimes wider). The primary work the CFO does when running Payne is not the multiplier — it is defending the choice of regional-average baseline. If the baseline is $4M, the answer is a bracket around $4M. If the baseline is $8M (because the market has moved), the answer is a bracket around $8M. Using a stale or wrong baseline is the single biggest failure mode of the method.

**When Payne is useful.**

- Companies pitching in a specific geography where a regional angel-average pre-money is available and legible to the investors in the room. Payne is especially aligned with US angel-group screens in California, Boston, Texas, and other well-mapped angel-investor geographies.
- Founders who want to defend a specific number that sits above or below the regional average with an explicit factor-by-factor rationale.

**When Payne is a poor fit.**

- Geographies where recent angel-round data is not published or is too thin to establish a regional average.
- Companies whose vertical does not fit the "comparable early-stage" definition the regional-average was built from (e.g. deep-tech, hardware, biotech applying a SaaS-benchmarked scorecard).
- Rapidly moving markets where the regional average from six months ago is materially different from the current-market pre-money. Payne inherits every problem of the underlying comp set.

## Risk-factor summation

**Origin.** The risk-factor summation method is generally attributed to the Ohio TechAngel Fund and cited by Bill Payne as a companion method to the scorecard. Documentation is in Payne's *Definitive Guide* and in the Angel Capital Association educational materials.

**Structure.** Start from the regional-average pre-money (same baseline as the Payne scorecard). Then adjust that baseline up or down by a fixed dollar increment for each of a set of risk factors — typically 12, though some published variants use 10 or 11. The classical 12-factor set:

1. Management risk
2. Stage of business
3. Legislation / political risk
4. Manufacturing risk
5. Sales and marketing risk
6. Funding / capital-raising risk
7. Competition risk
8. Technology risk
9. Litigation risk
10. International risk
11. Reputation risk
12. Potential lucrative exit

For each factor, the investor rates the company on a five-level scale:

| Rating | Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Very low risk (+2) | +$500,000 |
| Low risk (+1) | +$250,000 |
| Normal (0) | $0 |
| High risk (-1) | -$250,000 |
| Very high risk (-2) | -$500,000 |

Sum the twelve adjustments, add to the baseline. The result is the risk-factor pre-money.

**Worked example.**

Baseline (same regional average as the Payne example): $4.0M. The rater's twelve-factor scoring:

| Factor | Rating | Adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Management | +1 (strong team, some domain) | +$250K |
| Stage | -1 (very early, product not fully de-risked) | -$250K |
| Legislation / political | 0 | $0 |
| Manufacturing | 0 (software, low manufacturing risk) | $0 |
| Sales / marketing | -1 (early GTM, no repeatable playbook) | -$250K |
| Funding | 0 | $0 |
| Competition | -1 (well-funded competitors) | -$250K |
| Technology | 0 (works, no unusual moat) | $0 |
| Litigation | 0 | $0 |
| International | -1 (US-only, non-trivial to internationalise) | -$250K |
| Reputation | +1 (founders have positive reputation in domain) | +$250K |
| Exit | +1 (attractive strategic-acquirer landscape) | +$250K |
| **Total adjustment** | | **-$250K** |

Risk-factor pre-money = $4.0M - $250K = **$3.75M**.

**The right framing.** Risk-factor summation is a companion to Payne rather than a substitute. Both start from the same regional-average baseline. Payne applies a weighted-multiplier adjustment; risk-factor summation applies a discrete-dollar adjustment. Where the two disagree materially on the same target, the disagreement itself is informative — it usually means the baseline is wrong or one of the two adjustment mechanisms is picking up a factor the other missed.

**When risk-factor summation is useful.**

- As a **second lens** on the Payne output. If Payne says $4.62M and risk-factor summation says $3.75M, the bracket is $3.75M-$4.62M and the CFO can defend a specific point in that bracket with a factor-by-factor argument.
- For teams that find the 12-factor checklist more legible than the weighted-percentage scorecard.
- In geographies where the risk-factor tradition has held on inside the local angel community (some Midwest US angel groups still run it as the primary method).

**When risk-factor summation is a poor fit.** Same failure modes as Payne — a stale or inappropriate baseline, an ill-matched geography, or a market moving fast enough that the baseline is out of date.

## Triangulating a bracket, not a price

The reason the three anchor methods exist together is that no one of them produces a defensible **price**. What all three together produce is a **bracket** — a range within which the round should price, with a defensible argument for a specific point in the range.

The mechanical triangulation:

1. Run all three methods.
2. Note the three outputs.
3. Identify the range spanned by the outputs.
4. Identify the point inside the range you want to defend, and the argument for defending it.
5. Cross-check the range against **market conditions** (chapters 6-7) — the median pre-seed / seed pre-money in the current-quarter Fenwick / WSGR / PitchBook or Carta report, filtered to the target's geography and vertical.
6. Cross-check the range against the **VC method** (chapter 2) — what pre-money does the target investor's target return, target exit value, and dilution ladder produce?
7. If the anchor-method range and the market-conditions range and the VC-method range all overlap, you have a defensible price. If they diverge materially, name the divergence and the argument for the point you're defending.

**Worked triangulation on the running example.**

- Berkus: **$1.15M**.
- Payne: **$4.62M**.
- Risk-factor summation: **$3.75M**.
- Bracket: **$1.15M-$4.62M**.
- Reason for the wide bracket: Berkus is ceilinged at $2.5M by design (the vintage we ran); the other two methods anchor to a $4M regional baseline. The Berkus output tells us the risk-reduction picture is not fully mature (which we already knew — no paying customers, no partnerships). The Payne and risk-factor outputs tell us the market for comparable rounds is transacting around $3.75M-$4.62M.
- Defensible point: Somewhere around **$3.5M-$4M pre-money**, on the argument that the market pricing is real (the Payne / risk-factor anchor) but the specific target is slightly below-average on customer-and-partnership traction (Berkus is telling us that; risk-factor summation partially confirms it).

A founder walking into an angel-group screen with that triangulation — three methods, an explicit bracket, an explicit defensible point, and a market-conditions cross-check — is doing meaningfully better than a founder who has just quoted a single number.

## Why these methods are all "bracket, not price"

Each of the three methods has a designed-in subjectivity:

- **Berkus** requires the investor to score five component values from $0-$500K on subjective criteria. Different investors will produce different scores.
- **Payne** requires the investor to rate the target across seven factors on a 50%-200% scale against a regional baseline. Different investors will produce different ratings, and the baseline itself is a plug.
- **Risk-factor summation** requires the investor to rate twelve factors on a five-level scale. Different investors will produce different ratings.

None of the three has a market-clearing mechanism. None of the three produces a number that an unrelated party would arrive at independently. That is the essential difference between an anchor method and a public-comp method (chapter 3) or a VC target-return calculation (chapter 2), each of which has a specific external anchor that constrains the answer.

The founder's job with these methods is not to argue "this is what the company is worth." It is to argue "this is the range the company is worth, and here is where inside that range we are proposing to price, and here is the argument for that specific point." The investor's job is to push back on the specific components and land the negotiation somewhere both can defend.

## Common founder traps

- **Presenting a single anchor-method output as the price.** "Our Berkus is $2.4M so we're raising at $2.4M pre" cedes the range and invites the investor to argue the score down.
- **Using a stale regional average.** Payne and risk-factor summation both anchor to a baseline that has to be updated at least yearly, and more often when the market moves fast. Using a 2021 baseline in a 2024 market — in either direction — is a defensibility failure.
- **Mismatching the method to the vertical.** Applying a US SaaS regional average to a European hardware company produces nonsense. Match the baseline to the specific comparable set — geography, vertical, stage.
- **Skipping the market-conditions cross-check.** The three anchor methods will not tell you the market has moved 30% down over the past two quarters. Chapters 6-7 will. Not cross-checking is how a founder produces a bracket that the market will not clear.
- **Skipping the VC method cross-check.** If the target investor's fund model requires a 25x return and the anchor-method pre-money puts them at a 5x path to a defensible exit, the round will not close at that price regardless of what the anchor methods say. Chapter 2 explains why.
- **Assuming Berkus's $2.5M ceiling is normative.** In many markets today the ceiling is a floor. Use the vintage you name explicitly.
- **Confusing the anchor methods with 409A methodology.** The anchor methods produce a **negotiated pre-money for a preferred-stock issuance**. 409A produces a **tax-basis common-stock fair-market value for equity compensation**. These are different numbers with different methodologies. [mod-104 chapter 5](../mod-104-cap-tables-and-equity-compensation/05-409a-methodology-fmv-and-refresh-cadence.md) covers the 409A methodology; do not confuse them.

## What good looks like

A finance leader supporting a pre-revenue round:

- Runs all three anchor methods against the target company.
- Names the vintage of Berkus being used and defends the choice.
- Pulls the regional-average baseline from a specific, dated, real data source (regional angel report, HALO Report, PitchBook, or CB Insights extract), and cites it.
- Produces the triangulated bracket and identifies the defensible point in the bracket.
- Cross-checks against the current-quarter market-conditions data (chapters 6-7).
- Cross-checks against the VC target-return math (chapter 2).
- Prepares to defend the specific price with a factor-by-factor argument the investor can push back on rather than a single number they can argue against wholesale.

## Summary

- Pre-revenue startups cannot be valued with revenue-based methods. Three anchor methods — Berkus, Payne Scorecard, and risk-factor summation — produce a defensible **bracket** within which the round should price.
- **Berkus** is a five-component addition, each component 0-$500K, ceiling around $2.5M pre-money in the classic formulation. It is a risk-reduction checklist.
- **Payne Scorecard** starts from a regional-average pre-money and applies a weighted-multiplier adjustment across seven factors. The baseline is the load-bearing input.
- **Risk-factor summation** starts from the same regional-average baseline and applies a discrete-dollar adjustment across twelve factors. It is a companion lens to Payne, not a substitute.
- All three are subjective by design and produce a range, not a price. Triangulating the three outputs and cross-checking against market-conditions data (chapters 6-7) and the VC-method calculation (chapter 2) produces the defensible price.
- The common failure modes are treating an anchor number as a price, using a stale or mismatched baseline, and skipping the market-conditions and VC-method cross-checks.

Chapter 2 turns to the VC method — how a VC prices a seed round starting from their fund's target return and working backwards to today's pre-money — which is the essential cross-check on any anchor-method bracket for a round where an institutional VC is likely to be the lead investor.
