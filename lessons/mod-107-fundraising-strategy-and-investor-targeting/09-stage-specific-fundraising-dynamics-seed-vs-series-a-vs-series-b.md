# Stage-Specific Fundraising Dynamics — Seed vs. Series-A vs. Series-B

## Why this matters

The mechanics of chapters 1-8 — round sizing, target list, funnel, deck, data room, DocSend signal, close choreography, CFO / CEO split — are common to every early-stage priced-round raise. The **motion** those mechanics execute is not. Seed rounds, Series-A rounds, and Series-B rounds are structurally different. The specific way an investor underwrites a seed is not the specific way that same investor underwrites a Series-A, and neither resembles a Series-B. The pitch that closes a seed will not close a Series-A. The data room that closes a Series-A is materially under-built for a Series-B. The founder who runs a Series-A the way they ran a seed will not clear a Series-A term sheet.

A useful short-hand for the difference: **seed sells the founder, Series-A sells the traction, Series-B sells the operating model.** At each stage a different set of things is being underwritten, a different level of proof is expected, and a different fundraising motion is rewarded. Recognising which stage the current round is — not by the label on the term sheet but by the substance of what is being sold — and matching the motion to it, is one of the load-bearing skills of a CFO running raises across stages.

This chapter walks the three canonical stages, what each is really underwriting, the specific proof-artifacts each expects, the fundraising motion that fits each, and the diagnostic patterns for spotting a raise that is running the wrong motion for its stage.

## The three underwriting frames

### Seed — underwriting the founder and the insight

At seed, there is typically not enough traction to underwrite the traction. The company has 6-24 months of operating history, a small user or customer base (or none), an early product, and a small team. What the fund is underwriting is a bet on the founder, the specific insight the founder is executing on, and the founder's ability to move the company from the current state to a Series-A-worthy business in 18-24 months.

The **specific things being underwritten** at seed:

- **The founder-market fit.** Why this founder is the right person to build this specific company. Prior experience in the sector, prior insight into the customer, prior operating experience — the argument for why this founder has a differentiated view of what the customer wants and why others do not.
- **The insight.** The specific "why now" argument — a shift in technology, regulation, distribution, or customer behaviour that opens a window this company can walk through. The insight has to be specific and non-obvious.
- **The market.** A market large enough that a winning company in it is worth $1B+ in 10-15 years. Bottom-up more than top-down (chapter 4), but the framing has to be there.
- **The initial signal.** Enough proof — design partners, beta customers, waitlist, early product usage — to show the market is responsive to the insight. Not traction, but signal.
- **The team's ability to execute.** Small founding team, believable roadmap for the next 18-24 months.

The **proof-artifacts** at seed are correspondingly light. A pitch deck, a lightweight financial model (a driver-based 18-24 month forecast — mod-103), a light data room (corporate documents, cap table, a customer LOI or two, the pitch deck itself), and often a small number of early product screenshots or a demo video. Sometimes a set of design-partner or early-customer letters.

The **diligence process** at seed is fast, founder-relationship-heavy, and largely reference-based. A seed fund typically closes on 2-6 partner meetings across 2-4 weeks, and diligence consists of founder references (people who have worked with the founder), a handful of customer references (design partners), technical-diligence conversation with the CTO, and a light legal / cap-table check. There is no third-party audit. There is no operating-model deep-dive because there is no operating model of the required maturity to dive into.

The **round mechanics** at seed skew toward SAFEs and convertible notes for pre-seed and small-first-seed rounds (mod-105), and priced preferred for institutional-lead seeds ($3M-$10M range) (mod-108). The lead is often a specialised seed fund; the round often includes several smaller cheques from seed funds, angels, and strategic operators. Rounds close in 4-8 weeks from first-meeting to wire.

### Series-A — underwriting the traction and the wedge

At Series-A, the founder-and-insight bet is now table-stakes but not sufficient. The company has 18-30 months of operating history, real revenue (typically $1M-$3M ARR in B2B SaaS at current-vintage bars — chapter 1 and mod-106), a founding sales-motion proof, and a team of 15-40. The fund is underwriting not the founder's bet but the *evidence the bet is starting to work*. The traction is the specific artifact of that evidence.

The **specific things being underwritten** at Series-A:

- **The traction.** ARR, growth rate, cohort retention, NRR / GRR, gross margin, CAC payback (mod-102). Not just the point-in-time numbers, but the shape of the trajectory — the last 12-18 months of cohort behaviour, the sales-motion proof, the customer-concentration profile.
- **The wedge.** The specific customer segment and use case the product has landed in — the beachhead. A Series-A partner wants to see the wedge working before they underwrite the expansion.
- **The unit economics.** CAC payback under 24 months, LTV:CAC north of 3:1, gross margins in the sector-appropriate band (mod-102). Not just the numbers but the underlying cohort table that the numbers roll up from.
- **The team's ability to scale.** Founding team plus first-line hires (VP Eng, VP Product, first head of Sales / GTM leader). The founder has hired at least once above their previous ceiling.
- **The path to Series-B.** The plan for the next 18-24 months and the specific milestone bar the Series-B will underwrite against (chapter 1). Not just "we'll grow more" but "we'll clear the specific $8M-$10M ARR at 130% NRR bar the Series-B market is currently underwriting to."

The **proof-artifacts** at Series-A are substantially heavier than at seed. A pitch deck with the traction section as the load-bearing pages (chapter 4, slide 10). A driver-based three-statement model (mod-103) with the last 18-24 months of monthly actuals plus 24 months of forecast, scenarios, and cohort back-up. A full data room (chapter 5) with corporate documents, financials with monthly detail, cap table with 409A support, customer contracts with the top 10 customers, product roadmap, hiring plan, references list. A cohort table showing GRR / NRR by cohort. A CAC-payback and burn-multiple analysis. Sometimes an early-stage customer-satisfaction or NPS pack.

The **diligence process** at Series-A is deeper, more analytical, and often bifurcated between founder / commercial diligence (partner-led) and financial / operating diligence (associate-led). Typical Series-A diligence runs 6-10 weeks and includes:

- **Financial-model walk-through.** The partner or a diligence associate spends 60-90 minutes with the CFO walking through the model, understanding the drivers, and running sensitivities.
- **Customer references.** 5-15 customer calls with the fund's diligence team. The fund is triangulating the growth story against direct customer reads.
- **Product diligence.** For technical products, a technical partner or a hired technical adviser goes deep on the product architecture, defensibility, and roadmap.
- **Team references.** 5-10 back-channel references on the founder and the leadership team. This is more extensive than at seed.
- **Cap-table and legal review.** The fund's counsel reviews the corporate documents, cap table, prior-round docs, IP assignments, employment agreements, and outstanding convertibles / SAFEs (mod-105).
- **Sometimes third-party diligence.** For larger Series-A rounds or Series-A rounds with public-market comparables (e.g., strong SaaS traction), some funds engage third-party diligence firms (e.g., accounting-QoE firms) for a light QoE-style review. This is more common at Series-B.

The **round mechanics** at Series-A are firmly priced preferred, with a full NVCA-style term sheet (mod-108). The lead is typically a Series-A specialist fund (Benchmark, Accel, Founders Fund, First Round, USV, Bessemer, Craft, etc.) or a multi-stage fund's Series-A partner. The round frequently has a co-lead or a small syndicate of prior-round investors extending. Rounds close in 8-14 weeks from first-meeting to wire — materially longer than seed.

### Series-B — underwriting the operating model

At Series-B, the traction is now table-stakes and the founder bet is largely off the table (though founder-execution risk is not). The fund is underwriting the *operating model* — the specific machinery of a business that can scale to $50M-$100M ARR, unit economics that will hold at scale, and a management team that can build the operating rigour a scaling company requires.

The **specific things being underwritten** at Series-B:

- **The operating model.** How the company scales. The sales motion — is it a repeatable inbound + outbound + partnerships mix, or is it still founder-selling? The customer segmentation — is there a clear ideal customer profile (ICP) with defensible unit economics, or is it still opportunistic? The pricing and packaging — is it a mature packaging with tier-appropriate price points, or is it still bespoke enterprise pricing?
- **Cohort behaviour at scale.** Cohorts across 24-36 months, with GRR and NRR that hold or improve as cohorts age. The Series-B fund wants to see that the growth is not a coincidence of a good first-year cohort.
- **Efficiency at scale.** Rule of 40 in a defensible band (mod-106 chapter 4), burn multiple approaching investment-grade at the ARR band (mod-102 chapter 6), CAC payback holding under 24 months as the sales team scales, sales productivity per rep at industry norms.
- **The management team.** Not just the founder and the first head of Sales, but a full first-line: VP Eng, VP Product, CFO, VP Sales, VP Marketing, sometimes a COO. The fund is underwriting the team's ability to build a $50M-$100M ARR business, which requires 100-500 people across specialised functions.
- **The market position.** Competitive position within the sector — market-share estimates, win rates against specific competitors, differentiation defensibility.
- **The path to $100M ARR (or Series-C / late-stage).** A three-year operating plan with a specific machine that gets from current ARR to the next-round bar.

The **proof-artifacts** at Series-B are dramatically heavier than at Series-A. In addition to everything at Series-A:

- **Full operating model** with three-year forecast, monthly actuals for the last 24-36 months, scenarios and sensitivities, cohort-driven revenue build, and a bottom-up sales-productivity model.
- **Sales-productivity pack.** Rep-by-rep productivity, ramp curves, quota attainment, sales-cycle length, win rates by segment and by competitor. This is often what the diligence process actually deepens on.
- **Customer segmentation pack.** ICP definition, customer distribution across ICP tiers, retention and expansion by tier. Sometimes with third-party data (e.g., customer-count validation from a research firm).
- **Detailed cohort pack** with 24-36 months of cohort GRR / NRR, cohort ARPU expansion, cohort net-new-logo trends.
- **Full data room** including QoE-lite financials (or full third-party QoE for competitive rounds — see below).
- **Board pack** for the last 12-18 months. The Series-B lead often asks for the last 6-12 board packs as a diligence artifact — a window into how the CEO and CFO run the company.

The **diligence process** at Series-B is materially heavier than at Series-A and often includes:

- **Third-party diligence.** A specialised diligence firm (typically an accounting-QoE firm — see below) is often engaged by the lead investor to run a Quality of Earnings analysis on the financials. This is not a full audit, but it is a much deeper financial review than at Series-A. Cost typically $50K-$150K, paid by the company post-closing (or by the fund pre-signing).
- **Commercial diligence.** For competitive processes, a commercial-diligence firm (Bain, LEK, EY-Parthenon, McKinsey — the boutique commercial-diligence practices) may be engaged to independently validate market size, customer segmentation, and competitive position. This is common at growth-stage and increasingly at Series-B.
- **Extended customer references.** 15-30 customer calls, sometimes with specific segments called out (top 10 by ARR, top 5 at-risk, top 5 newest).
- **Technical-diligence deep dive.** For technical products, an outside CTO adviser reviews the architecture in 3-8 hours of engagement.
- **Model deep-dive.** 2-4 hours with the CFO on the operating model, plus days of independent model rebuild by the fund's diligence associate.
- **Reference calls on the management team.** 5-10 back-channels per senior hire, not just the founder.

The **round mechanics** at Series-B are priced preferred with a heavier term sheet, higher board-composition weight for the lead (often two board seats), and more protective provisions. The lead is often a Series-B specialist or the Series-A lead extending into Series-B (with a fresh lead alongside). Rounds close in 10-16 weeks from first-meeting to wire.

## The specific-stage motion for each round

### Seed motion — narrative-forward, founder-heavy, fast

**What the founder-CEO does.**

- **Runs the raise personally.** At seed the CFO role is either absent (there is no CFO yet) or is a fractional CFO helping build the model and the light data room. The founder-CEO owns the funnel, the pitch, the partner conversations, and the close (chapter 8's split is 90/10 CEO / CFO at seed vs. 50/50 or lighter at Series-A).
- **Optimises for narrative.** The pitch is a story about the founder's insight and the "why now." Numbers are the wrapper, not the substance. Every slide is about the founder's specific view of the market.
- **Warm-intro-heavy target list.** At seed the intro-quality-per-meeting is even more load-bearing than at Series-A. A cold-inbound seed process is high-friction; warm intros from specific-partner-network angels or from other founders in the partner's portfolio are the primary motion.
- **Fast close.** 4-8 weeks from process-start to term sheet. Seed rounds move fast because the diligence is light; the fund is betting on the founder, not diligencing the traction.
- **Tolerates smaller round sizes.** A $3M seed with a $10M pre-money is a real institutional round. The founder-CEO does not need to raise $10M at seed to have a valid raise.

**What the CFO (or fractional-CFO) does.**

- **Owns the model and the data room.** A lightweight but complete driver-based model, a lightweight data room with corporate documents, cap table, LOIs, the deck.
- **Owns the SAFE / preferred-round mechanics.** If the round is priced preferred, the CFO runs the term-sheet analysis (mod-108) and coordinates with company counsel. If SAFEs, the CFO runs the mechanics from mod-105 chapter 5.
- **Handles the outstanding convertibles.** Most seed-stage companies have prior SAFEs or notes on the cap table. Reconciling these into the round's conversion math is a CFO job (mod-105 chapter 6).

**The seed-motion pattern to recognise.** A fast, narrative-forward, founder-personal process with a small handful of high-conviction partner meetings, a compressed close, and a lightweight paper trail. When the seed process starts looking like a Series-A (heavy data-room requests, extended diligence, customer references at scale), either the founder is over-selling the traction or the fund is a Series-A-track fund pattern-matching the wrong stage.

### Series-A motion — traction-forward, CFO-and-CEO paired, disciplined

**What the founder-CEO does.**

- **Runs the founder-partner relationship stack.** The partner meetings, the "why now" story, the narrative for the wedge, the vision for the operating machine.
- **Owns the top-of-deck.** Company purpose, market, why now, team, and the traction narrative (slide 10 in the canonical structure). The founder writes and delivers these.
- **Anchors the customer references.** For 5-15 customer references, the founder-CEO has typically pre-briefed the customer on the call and stays close to the customer relationship. The reference calls are diligence-critical.
- **Delivers the partner meeting.** The partner meeting (chapter 3 funnel stage 4) is founder-led; CFO may attend for the model walk-through but the primary voice is the founder.

**What the CFO does.**

- **Owns the model, defends it, and runs the operating-plan discussion.** The model walk-through with the fund's associate or partner is CFO-led. The CFO defends the assumptions, runs sensitivities live, and demonstrates the model absorbs stress.
- **Owns the data room and the diligence process.** The data-room build (chapter 5), the sharing pattern (chapter 5 access-tiering), the diligence-request checklist, and the responsiveness to specific diligence requests are CFO-led.
- **Owns the funnel and the close choreography.** Weekly funnel review (chapter 3), momentum narrative preparation for the founder, close-week choreography (chapter 7), term-sheet analysis and red-line (mod-108).
- **Owns the term sheet.** Every incoming term sheet gets a same-day CFO-authored analysis against the NVCA reference and a specific red-line before the CEO responds.
- **Coordinates with board and counsel.** The CFO manages the board update on the process (through the CEO), coordinates with company counsel on term-sheet negotiation, and handles the closing mechanics.

**The Series-A-motion pattern to recognise.** A disciplined, traction-forward process with a well-tiered target list, a Monday-cadence funnel review, a professional data room, and a CFO-CEO paired execution. When the Series-A process looks like a seed (founder alone, no model deep-dive, no formal data room), the founder is under-preparing and the process will cost terms.

### Series-B motion — operating-model-forward, executive-team-heavy, extended

**What the founder-CEO does.**

- **Runs the founder-market-and-vision narrative, but now paired with the executive team.** The partner meetings often include the VP Sales, the VP Product, or the head of Customer Success as domain-authority participants. The founder is no longer the sole voice.
- **Owns the three-year vision and the operating-plan narrative.** The story of what the company becomes at $50M ARR and $100M ARR is founder-authored.
- **Manages the board through the process.** By Series-B, the board has 3-7 investors and outside directors (mod-110); the CEO manages the board update cadence on the raise.

**What the CFO does.**

- **Owns the full operating model.** Three-year monthly forecast, cohort-driven revenue, sales-productivity model, scenario stack, sensitivities, cap-table roll-forward. This is a materially larger model than at Series-A and often takes 2-4 weeks of pre-raise work to get to fund-diligence-ready state.
- **Owns the QoE and third-party diligence.** Coordinates with the lead's diligence firm on the QoE process. The CFO's pre-work — clean books, defensible revenue recognition (mod-101), reconciled cohort table — determines whether the QoE lands cleanly.
- **Owns the sales-productivity pack.** Rep-level productivity, quota attainment, ramp curves, sales-cycle data — this is often the operating-model artifact the Series-B fund cares about most.
- **Owns the customer-segmentation pack.** ICP definition, cohort-by-segment retention, expansion-by-segment — this is what defends the "we can 5x the ARR" story.
- **Runs the extended close.** Series-B closes take 10-16 weeks; the CFO manages the parallel-process choreography (chapter 7) over a much longer window with more diligence-firm coordination.

**The Series-B-motion pattern to recognise.** A months-long, operating-model-forward process with third-party diligence, executive-team participation in partner meetings, and a QoE-quality data room. When the Series-B process looks like a Series-A (no third-party diligence, no sales-productivity pack, founder-only in partner meetings), the founder is either raising the wrong round or has under-built the operating team the round is supposed to fund.

## Cross-stage diagnostic patterns

### "Am I raising the round I think I am?"

A useful diagnostic: describe what is being underwritten. If the answer is "our story and the founder" — that is a seed motion. If the answer is "our traction and the wedge" — that is a Series-A motion. If the answer is "our operating model and executive team" — that is a Series-B motion. If the answer is a mismatch — a founder describing traction as the underwriting but with $200K ARR to show, or a founder describing operating-model as the underwriting but without a VP Sales — the round is being raised for the wrong stage.

**A specific example.** A company at $600K ARR with a strong founder and a compelling insight is raising a "$8M Series-A." The traction bar for a Series-A is $1M-$2M ARR (chapter 1). The company is not clearing the Series-A traction bar. What the founder is really raising is either an over-sized seed (in which case, price it as a large seed at seed pre-money and market it as such) or an undersized Series-A that will be underwritten as a bet on the founder (in which case, accept that the round will price like a big seed, not like a Series-A). Trying to raise a $8M "Series-A" at a $30M pre when the traction is at a seed level is a specific mis-fit that most target funds will pass on quickly.

### "Am I running the right motion?"

A founder running a Series-A the way they ran their seed — no CFO, no formal data room, no funnel discipline, warm-intro-only outreach — is going to cost terms. A founder running a Series-B the way they ran their Series-A — no third-party diligence prep, no sales-productivity pack, no executive-team involvement — is going to cost terms.

**Diagnostic questions per stage.**

- **Am I underwriting-appropriate for the stage?** (Correct motion.)
- **Are my proof-artifacts stage-appropriate?** (Correct heavier data room and model at higher stages.)
- **Is my target list stage-appropriate?** (Seed funds do not lead Series-A; Series-A funds usually do not follow small seeds.)
- **Is my team involvement stage-appropriate?** (Series-B partner meetings often include the exec team.)
- **Is my timeline stage-appropriate?** (Seed 4-8 weeks; Series-A 8-14 weeks; Series-B 10-16 weeks. Compressing a Series-B into 8 weeks means diligence gets cut, which usually reads as founder-side pressure and costs terms.)

## What Series-A rewards that seed does not

Some specific mechanics that pay off at Series-A but do not at seed:

- **A driver-based three-statement model with cohort back-up.** At seed, a lightweight 24-month P&L forecast is enough. At Series-A, the model needs to survive a partner-led deep-dive with sensitivities and a scenario walk (mod-103 chapter 5).
- **A cohort table with GRR / NRR by cohort.** At seed, cohort data may not exist yet. At Series-A, the cohort table is the underlying artifact behind the traction slide (mod-102 chapter 4).
- **A formal data room with tier-2 access.** At seed, a Google Drive folder shared read-only works. At Series-A, a formal data room with staged access (chapter 5) is the professional bar.
- **CFO participation.** At seed, no CFO is fine. At Series-A, the CFO's model defence and data-room stewardship is a load-bearing input to the outcome.
- **Weekly Monday funnel reviews.** At seed, informal tracking works. At Series-A, the Monday review is the discipline that produces the momentum narrative.

## What Series-B rewards that Series-A does not

The Series-A-to-Series-B step-up requires new artifacts and new processes that do not exist at Series-A:

- **Third-party diligence readiness.** A QoE-ready set of books (clean revenue recognition per mod-101, reconciled cohort table per mod-102 chapter 4, reconciled ARR and MRR per mod-101 chapter 3) is a Series-B pre-requisite that Series-A can get away without.
- **Sales-productivity pack.** Rep-level data, ramp curves, quota attainment — this is a Series-B-specific artifact.
- **Executive-team participation in partner meetings.** VP Sales, VP Product, sometimes COO speaking to their functions. Series-A partner meetings are founder-focused; Series-B partner meetings often include the exec bench.
- **Board-pack diligence artifact.** The last 6-12 board packs. Series-A rarely asks; Series-B often does.
- **Longer timeline.** 10-16 weeks vs. Series-A's 8-14 weeks. The CFO's process-choreography discipline (chapter 7) has to hold across a longer window with more diligence-firm coordination.

## Bridge rounds and off-cycle rounds

A note on rounds that do not fit the seed / Series-A / Series-B stage taxonomy cleanly: bridge rounds (mod-109), extension seeds, pre-Series-A pre-emptive rounds, Series-A prime rounds. These rounds run their own motion — typically compressed, insider-led, narrower target list, and less diligence-heavy — and the "which motion to run" question does not fit the neat three-stage taxonomy. See mod-109 for the specific bridge / extension mechanics; the target-list, funnel, and close-choreography chapters (chapters 2, 3, 7) still apply but often with a materially compressed target list (existing investors first) and a shorter timeline (2-6 weeks).

## Common stage-mismatch traps

- **Raising a Series-A on seed proof-artifacts.** Deck without traction slide, model without cohort back-up, data room without contracts, founder-alone in partner meetings. Series-A funds pass because the proof they need is not there.
- **Raising a seed on Series-A proof-artifacts.** Heavy data room, formal QoE-ready books, extended diligence process — for a $2M seed. This over-invests founder time in process for a round that should close in 6 weeks on a lightweight motion.
- **Founder-only Series-A.** No CFO, no CFO analog, no fractional-CFO helper. The Series-A raise costs terms because the model defence and diligence responsiveness are under-resourced.
- **Series-B without a third-party diligence prep.** The lead engages a QoE firm and finds surprises (revenue-recognition inconsistencies, cohort-table mis-reconciliations, cap-table gaps) that would have been caught by a pre-raise cleanup. Terms compress or the round dies.
- **Series-A-track fund pattern-matched at seed.** A seed founder pitches a Series-A fund and gets partner interest that then stalls when the fund's Series-A diligence process runs against seed-stage proof. The founder has wasted 6-10 weeks. Chapter 2's fund-math (partnership-economics filter) is what prevents this.
- **Compressed Series-B timeline.** A founder trying to run a Series-B in 6-8 weeks the way they ran the Series-A. Diligence gets cut, the QoE lands mid-process with surprises, and the process either extends (embarrassingly, from the fund's side) or falls apart.
- **No executive-team involvement at Series-B.** The founder-only Series-B partner meeting reads as either "team is not ready" or "founder does not trust their team." Both hurt.
- **The stage-label mismatch.** Labelling the round as one thing (Series-A) when the underwriting frame is another (seed). The label is not what matters; the substance is.

## What good looks like

A stage-appropriate raise:

- **Correctly identifies what is being underwritten.** Founder-and-insight (seed), traction-and-wedge (Series-A), or operating-model-and-team (Series-B).
- **Prepares the specific proof-artifacts for the stage.** Lightweight for seed; traction pack for Series-A; operating-model pack and QoE readiness for Series-B.
- **Runs the specific motion for the stage.** Founder-heavy fast close for seed; CFO-CEO paired disciplined process for Series-A; executive-team-inclusive extended process for Series-B.
- **Times the process for the stage.** 4-8 weeks (seed), 8-14 weeks (Series-A), 10-16 weeks (Series-B).
- **Uses a stage-appropriate target list.** Seed funds for seed, Series-A specialists for Series-A, Series-B / growth funds for Series-B — filtered by chapter 2's fund-math.
- **Does not raise a round-labelled-as-X on proof-artifacts-that-are-Y.** The label matches the substance.
- **Adjusts the CFO / CEO split by stage.** ~90 / 10 CEO / CFO at seed; ~50 / 50 at Series-A; ~40 / 60 at Series-B (as the operating-model work weights heavier).

## Summary

- Seed, Series-A, and Series-B rounds are structurally different: seed sells the founder and the insight, Series-A sells the traction and the wedge, Series-B sells the operating model and the executive team.
- Each stage has its own proof-artifacts, diligence depth, target-list composition, timeline, CFO / CEO split, and specific motion that the fundraising process should execute.
- A raise's substance is set by what is being underwritten, not by the label on the term sheet. Raising a round labelled as one stage but with proof-artifacts of another is a common failure mode.
- Recognise the stage from the substance: what the fund is being asked to underwrite. Match the motion (target list, funnel, deck, data room, close choreography) to the stage.
- The Series-A step-up from seed requires a formal data room, a CFO / model defence, a cohort-driven traction pack, and Monday-cadence funnel discipline that seed can get away without.
- The Series-B step-up from Series-A requires third-party diligence prep (QoE, sometimes commercial diligence), a sales-productivity pack, an executive team in partner meetings, and a longer timeline.
- Bridge / extension / pre-emptive rounds run their own compressed motion; the standard three-stage taxonomy is a scaffold, not a taxonomy that covers every off-cycle round.
