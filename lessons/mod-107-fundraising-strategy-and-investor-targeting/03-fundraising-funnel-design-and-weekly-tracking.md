# Fundraising Funnel — Design, Weekly Tracking, and Honest Disqualification

## Why this matters

A fundraising round is a sales process. The target list from chapter 2 is the outbound universe; the term sheet is the closed-won outcome. In between sits a funnel with specific stages, specific per-stage conversion rates, and specific weekly management. Founders who run the raise without a funnel end up with the same problems that GTM leaders see in undisciplined pipelines: half-worked leads, invisible drop-offs, unfair prioritisation of the loudest voices, and a false sense of momentum. Founders who run the raise *as* a funnel produce the outcome that closes the round — parallel processes converging in a window, honest disqualification of dead conversations, and a defensible momentum narrative when a partner asks "who else is looking?"

This chapter installs the specific funnel structure, the per-stage conversion expectations, the weekly cadence, and the momentum-narrative mechanics.

## The seven funnel stages

The canonical fundraising funnel has seven stages, each with a specific definition and a specific "moves-to-next-stage" gate. Different founders and advisers name the stages slightly differently; the substance is stable.

1. **Target list.** Fund is on the constructed target list from chapter 2. No outreach yet.
2. **Warm intro sent.** A warm introduction to the target partner has been requested (or is in-flight) from a mutual connection.
3. **First meeting.** The founder-CEO has taken a first meeting with the sponsoring partner (or an associate).
4. **Partner meeting.** The full-partnership-track second (or third) meeting where the sponsoring partner brings the deal to a broader partner audience for pattern-check.
5. **Full partnership.** The formal full-partnership meeting (or Monday meeting, in the standard weekly cadence). The fund's collective decision-making body reviews the deal.
6. **Diligence / IOI.** The fund is running diligence (references, product diligence, financial diligence) with an indication of interest (IOI) or verbal commitment to a term sheet if diligence clears.
7. **Term sheet.** A written term sheet has been issued.

Each stage has a specific "moves-forward" gate. The gates matter because the CFO's weekly job is to know exactly which stage each fund is in and what the next-stage gate is.

## Per-stage conversion expectations

Every fundraising process is different. But the industry practitioner canon (Feld & Mendelson *Venture Deals*; Suster's *Both Sides of the Table*; Wilson's *AVC*; DocSend's annual startup fundraising reports) converges on the following rough ranges, sometimes summarised as the "10x funnel" pattern for early-stage rounds:

- **Target list → warm intro sent:** ~50-80% (most funds on the target list should be reachable through a warm intro chain).
- **Warm intro sent → first meeting:** ~40-70% (the intro-to-first-meeting conversion is the first major honest-signal gate — a fund that will not take a first meeting after a warm intro is a fund that has an early-stage pass baked in).
- **First meeting → partner meeting:** ~20-40% (the first-to-partner-meeting conversion is where most funds pass; the conversation determines whether the sponsoring partner will spend social capital bringing the deal to their partners).
- **Partner meeting → full partnership:** ~30-60% (partner meetings that advance to full-partnership almost always convert further; the pass rate at partner meeting is high because the sponsoring partner has already made a personal bet).
- **Full partnership → diligence / IOI:** ~40-70%.
- **Diligence / IOI → term sheet:** ~50-80%.

Multiplied through: a target list of 40 funds should produce approximately 20-30 warm-intro requests, 10-15 first meetings, 3-6 partner meetings, 2-4 full-partnership discussions, 1-3 diligence processes, and 1-2 term sheets. That is a healthy conversion path to a single-lead close (needing 1 term sheet) with room for competitive dynamics (2 term sheets in-window).

**These are ranges, not benchmarks.** Actual conversions vary heavily by fund, sector, market conditions, and the specific founder-market fit. What the CFO does with the ranges: use them as a *pattern-check* on the live funnel. A funnel where the first-meeting → partner-meeting conversion is 5% is a funnel where the deck or the founder's first-meeting delivery is off. A funnel where every first meeting advances to a partner meeting is a funnel where the founder is either extraordinarily well-fit or is failing to disqualify honestly (see below).

## The funnel tracker — structure and cadence

The concrete artifact for running the funnel is a spreadsheet (or CRM setup, but a spreadsheet works fine at this stage) with one row per fund and one column per stage-timestamp plus notes. The exact structure:

| Column | Content |
|---|---|
| Fund name | The fund. |
| Sponsoring partner | The specific target partner (chapter 2). |
| Tier | 1 / 2 / 3 from chapter 2. |
| Intro requested (date) | When the warm-intro request was made. |
| Intro received (date) | When the intro landed. |
| First meeting (date) | When the first meeting happened. |
| Partner meeting (date) | When the second / partner meeting happened. |
| Full partnership (date) | When the deal went to the full partnership. |
| Diligence started (date) | When the fund started diligence. |
| Term sheet received (date) | When the term sheet arrived. |
| Current stage | The current funnel stage. |
| Next action | The specific next thing the CFO or CEO is doing to move the fund forward. |
| Next action owner | Who owns it (CEO / CFO / intro-source). |
| Status | Active / Passed / Slow / Disqualified. |
| Signal notes | Free-text notes on the fund's current signal (partner's questions, expressed reservations, timing indicators). |

**Weekly cadence.** The CFO owns the tracker. Every Monday, the CFO reviews the tracker with the founder-CEO and produces a one-page funnel snapshot: how many funds at each stage, week-over-week movement, the top three "moving" processes, the top three "stalled" processes that need re-engagement, and the disqualification decisions for the week.

**A well-run raise has a Monday funnel review.** A raise that only reviews the funnel when something significant happens (a term sheet, a hot signal from a partner) is a raise where the CFO is not doing their job. The point of the weekly cadence is to catch stalled processes early and to force the founder-CEO to make honest disqualification calls before they clog the pipeline.

## The momentum narrative — what it is and why it matters

Every fundraising process eventually confronts the same partner question: "who else is looking?" The founder-CEO's answer to that question is the *momentum narrative*, and it is a load-bearing input to every subsequent partner conversation.

The right answer to "who else is looking" is a specific, honest, well-managed statement of the funnel. Not a list of funds (naming funds is bad manners and often signals desperation), and not a bluff, but a specific characterisation of the process. Something like:

> "We're running a focused process with about a dozen funds. We're in second meetings with three, in first meetings with four more, and we have three more first meetings on the calendar this week. We're targeting a term sheet in about three weeks."

That answer does three things: it establishes that the process is real, it establishes that the founder is running it professionally (a fund knows what a well-run process looks like), and it establishes a timeline that pressures the target partner to move.

**The wrong answer** to the same question is one of the three following:

- **Concealment.** "We're not talking to anyone else, we're focused on you." This reads as either untrue or as a founder who does not know how to run a process. Both are bad signals.
- **Overpromise.** "We're in term sheets with three funds." Every fund knows the other funds in the market at any given time. If the founder claims term sheets that do not exist, the target fund will find out (a call to their friend at the other fund) and the process is dead.
- **Excessive detail.** "We're in partner meetings with Fund A, Fund B, and Fund C." Naming specific funds — especially before term sheets are on the table — is a broken norm; the founder is now sharing signal-relevant information across funds who will compare notes.

**The momentum narrative is what the funnel tracker enables.** Without the tracker, the founder's answer to "who else is looking" is a guess. With the tracker, it is a defensible statement of the specific state of the process. The CFO's role includes making sure the founder has the narrative ready before every partner meeting.

## Honest disqualification

The single biggest disciplining function of the funnel is *disqualification*. Every raise has funds that will not close. The question is not whether — the question is when the CFO recognises it. Early disqualification frees the founder's time for funds that will close. Late disqualification wastes founder-hours on dead conversations and clogs the running-momentum-narrative math.

**The four disqualification signals.**

1. **Explicit pass.** The fund says no. Easy. Update the tracker, note the reason, move on. Sometimes an explicit pass leaves a door open for a follower cheque or a next-round conversation, which is worth noting.

2. **Slow no.** The fund does not say no but does not move forward. Signs of slow no: partner meetings not scheduled after a strong first meeting; associate handling the process rather than the sponsoring partner; specific diligence requests not being sent; two weeks between fund-side communications when other funds are moving weekly. The CFO's job is to recognise the pattern and either force the issue (a direct "we're targeting a term sheet by X — is that a timeline that works for you?" from the CEO) or reclassify the fund as disqualified.

3. **Silent no.** No response after a first meeting. Update the tracker, send a polite follow-up, if no response after 10 business days classify as passed. Do not chase.

4. **Fund passes, sponsoring partner is still interested.** The partner personally wants to invest but cannot get partnership alignment. Sometimes the partner will introduce to another fund, or make a personal follower angel cheque, or write into an SPV. Note the partner's continued interest but classify the fund as passed for round-participation purposes.

**The disqualification bar.** A fund should be classified as disqualified when the CFO can honestly say: "if we hit our best-case scenario over the next three weeks, this fund will still not lead a term sheet." Any fund still on the "active" list should have a specific pathway to a term sheet — a specific next meeting, a specific diligence-close event, or a specific partnership-alignment moment.

**Why disqualification matters for the momentum narrative.** The narrative depends on the fund's *actual* funnel state. Counting a slow-no fund as "in partner meetings" is a form of self-delusion that corrupts the running arithmetic. If the founder tells a Series-A partner "we're in three partner meetings" and one of the three has been slow-no for two weeks, the narrative is inflated. A partner who talks to the slow-no fund will find out. The narrative loses credibility, and worse — the founder loses credibility.

**Honest disqualification is a CFO-owned discipline.** The founder-CEO frequently over-weights fund interest ("she was really excited in the first meeting — she'll get back to us") and over-anchors on hopes. The CFO's job is to be the honest reader of the signal and to make the disqualification call before the founder is ready to. This is a specific-authority delegation the CEO should agree to at the start of the process: "the CFO calls disqualification, and once called, we do not re-engage without a new signal."

## Weekly cadence — a specific run pattern

A concrete run pattern for a 6-8 week seed raise or 8-12 week Series-A raise:

**Monday morning (30-45 minutes).**

- CFO reviews the tracker for the past week.
- Identifies stage transitions (funds that moved forward or backward).
- Identifies stalled funds and proposes disqualification for those with two-week no-signal.
- Produces the one-page snapshot: stage counts, week-over-week deltas, top-three moving, top-three stalled, disqualification decisions.
- Prepares the momentum-narrative sentence for the CEO's use in the week's partner meetings.

**Monday afternoon or Tuesday (60-90 minutes).**

- CFO and CEO review the snapshot together.
- CEO makes final disqualification calls (with CFO's recommendation).
- CEO and CFO align on the top-three priority processes for the week (who is closest to a term sheet).
- Any second-order signals to act on (a partner asks a specific diligence question, a co-lead is being explored, a signalling risk is emerging).

**Throughout the week.**

- CEO takes partner meetings, associate meetings, follow-up calls.
- CFO handles data-room requests, model walk-throughs, term-sheet analyses, follow-up questions.
- Both keep the tracker updated in real time as meetings happen, not retrospectively at the end of the week.

**Friday end-of-week.**

- CFO does a light-touch check: is the tracker current? Are any funds slipping that should be re-engaged before the weekend?
- Confirm next-week meetings.

## The "closing window" — when to compress

A well-run raise concentrates the closing activity into a compressed window (typically 1-2 weeks) so that term sheets converge in-window and the founder can run a proper competitive close (chapter 7). The funnel design has to work toward that compression.

**How to compress.**

- **Sequence outreach.** Tier-2 funds are pitched first (see chapter 2). By the time tier-1 funds are in first meetings, the founder has calibrated the pitch and has some existing partner meetings in-flight.
- **Time-box the process.** Announce the target close date internally at the start: "we want a term sheet by [date] and to close by [date + 4 weeks]." Every partner conversation is framed against that timeline.
- **Force the timing conversation.** In the second or third meeting with a serious fund, the CEO should explicitly say "we're targeting a term sheet decision by X — is that a timeline you can meet?" A fund that cannot meet the timeline is either genuinely constrained (in which case they can be given a specific delay concession) or is a slow-no in disguise.
- **Avoid over-serialisation.** Do not close with the first fund that offers a term sheet before checking the state of the other in-flight processes. A closed round with one term sheet is a closed round; a closed round with two or three converging term sheets is a closed round on better terms.

## Common funnel-management traps

- **No tracker.** Running the raise off memory or off the founder-CEO's Gmail inbox is how the funnel becomes invisible.
- **Tracker not updated in real time.** A tracker updated retrospectively on Monday for the past week loses the moment-to-moment signal. Update as meetings happen.
- **Founder-CEO owns disqualification.** Founders are optimists; disqualification requires realism. Delegate to CFO.
- **Optimistic stage classification.** A fund that has not scheduled a partner meeting after a "strong" first meeting is not in "partner meeting" stage — it is in "first meeting, awaiting partner meeting" stage. Classify honestly.
- **No cadence.** A weekly Monday review is the minimum. Missing weeks means missing signal.
- **No momentum narrative preparation.** Every partner meeting will surface the "who else is looking" question. Not preparing an answer is not-preparing to close.
- **Bluffing the narrative.** Saying term sheets exist that do not, or naming specific competitor funds, is a broken norm that ends processes. Do not do it.
- **Serialising the raise.** Meeting funds one-at-a-time — waiting for a first fund's answer before starting the second fund — extends the timeline and eliminates the compressed close. Parallelise from the start.
- **Not compressing to a window.** A term sheet in month 2 followed by a term sheet in month 4 does not produce competitive dynamics. Time the close.
- **Ignoring the low-tier calibration.** Pitching tier-1 funds first with an uncalibrated deck wastes the highest-value meetings. Calibrate on tier-2 first.

## What good looks like

A well-run funnel:

- Has a live tracker updated in real time.
- Has a weekly Monday review with a snapshot and disqualification decisions.
- Classifies funds honestly (Active / Passed / Slow / Disqualified) with a specific pathway defined for each Active.
- Has a specific closing-window target (a target-week for term sheets) and sequences activity to converge to it.
- Produces a defensible momentum narrative that the CEO uses in every partner meeting, updated weekly.
- Disqualifies aggressively — funds that will not close move off the active list quickly, freeing founder-time for funds that will.
- Runs multiple processes in parallel, not one-at-a-time.
- Uses the per-stage conversion pattern as a diagnostic — a stage where conversion is materially below the practitioner range is a stage where something (deck, first-meeting delivery, diligence responsiveness) needs to be diagnosed.

## Summary

- The raise is a sales process run through a seven-stage funnel from target list to term sheet.
- Per-stage conversion expectations from the practitioner canon give the CFO a diagnostic pattern-check on the live funnel.
- The funnel tracker (one row per fund, per-stage timestamps, next-action, status) is the central artifact and is updated in real time.
- Weekly Monday review with the CEO produces the momentum narrative, the disqualification decisions, and the priority processes for the week.
- Honest disqualification — recognising slow-no, silent-no, and partnership-alignment-failure patterns and reclassifying the fund quickly — is a CFO-owned discipline that keeps the funnel clean and the momentum narrative honest.
- The momentum narrative is what the CEO answers "who else is looking" with; it must be specific, honest, and driven by the tracker.
- The close is compressed into a 1-2 week window so that multiple term sheets converge; the funnel design has to serialise and parallelise activity to make that compression happen.
