# The CFO's Role in the Raise vs. the CEO's Narrative

## Why this matters

A well-run seed or Series-A raise has two distinct workstreams executed in parallel by two distinct roles. The **CEO owns the narrative-and-relationship half** — the vision, the partner conversations, the pitch delivery, the founder-market-fit story, the moment-of-conviction that gets a partner to write a term sheet. The **CFO owns the model-and-process half** — the operating plan and financial model that back the numbers on the deck, the data room the fund's diligence process reads, the term-sheet analysis and red-line, the choreography of parallel processes, and the board-and-legal coordination.

Neither role is dispensable. A raise run only by a founder-CEO who is also trying to manage the process ends up doing both jobs badly — the narrative work suffers because the founder is doing spreadsheets at midnight, and the process work suffers because the founder does not have the specific practice. A raise run only by a CFO without an anchoring CEO ends up as a well-run process for a story that no partner ever hears in the founder's own voice.

Series-A rounds specifically reward the CFO-CEO partnership. A first-time founder running a Series-A alone frequently costs terms — sometimes materially. This chapter walks the specific workstreams, the collaboration mechanics, and the specific reasons the founder-only raise is expensive.

## The two workstreams

**The CEO's workstream — narrative and relationship.**

- **Pitch delivery.** The founder-CEO delivers the pitch. The deck is a scaffold; the delivery is what closes a first meeting into a partner meeting.
- **Narrative authorship.** The specific story of the company — why it exists, why now, why this team, why this market — is founder-authored. The CFO can suggest structural corrections (chapter 4), but the story voice belongs to the CEO.
- **Partner relationships.** Every partner conversation is CEO-led. The CFO may sit in, but the primary relationship is founder-partner.
- **Vision and roadmap ownership.** The 5-year vision, the market thesis, the specific bet the company is making — all CEO-owned.
- **Team storytelling.** The team slide (chapter 4), the founder-market-fit story, the hires-to-date and hires-to-come — CEO-authored.
- **Reference calls with existing customers.** When a fund calls a customer reference, the CEO has typically prepared the customer for the call (with CFO's support on the specific technical detail). The relationship with the customer is CEO-owned.
- **Board-facing communication about the round.** Board updates on where the round stands are typically CEO-authored, with CFO providing the specific process detail.

**The CFO's workstream — model and process.**

- **Financial model authorship.** The driver-based three-statement model (mod-103) is CFO-owned. Every number on the deck comes out of it. Every diligence question about the numbers is answered from it.
- **Model defence.** Fund diligence will run sensitivities and stress tests on the model. The CFO defends the model live — in a conversation with the fund's associate or partner — and demonstrates it can absorb reasonable stress without breaking.
- **Data room ownership.** The data room (chapter 5) is CFO-authored, CFO-maintained, and CFO-audited.
- **Term-sheet analysis and red-line.** Every incoming term sheet is analysed against the NVCA reference by the CFO, with a same-day summary to the CEO and board (chapter 7). The CFO produces the red-line and the negotiation memo.
- **Board-and-legal choreography.** The board (if there is one), the informal seed-investor circle, and the company's counsel are coordinated by the CFO through the raise.
- **Funnel management.** The funnel tracker and the weekly-cadence review (chapter 3) are CFO-owned.
- **DocSend and deck-signal reading.** The engagement data (chapter 6) is CFO-monitored and CFO-interpreted; iteration recommendations to the CEO come from the CFO.
- **Close choreography.** The specific week-of-close mechanics (chapter 7) are CFO-managed.
- **Post-close mechanics.** The signing, the closing documents, the wire arrivals, the cap-table update, the 83(b) filings for new hires under the round — all CFO-owned.

## The specific handoff at each funnel stage

**Warm-intro stage.** CEO-owned. Founder identifies mutual connections and requests intros.

**First meeting.** CEO-owned. Founder delivers the deck. CFO does not attend first meetings unless specifically requested (which is rare at seed; more common at Series-A for a "meet the finance lead" purpose).

**Partner meeting.** CEO-owned; CFO may attend for the financial-model walk-through if the fund requests it. In practice the CFO attendance at partner meetings varies — some funds like it, some prefer founder-only.

**Full partnership.** CEO-owned; CFO usually does not attend full-partnership meetings. The full partnership discusses the deal internally after the CEO has left.

**Diligence.** CFO-heavy. The diligence process — model walk-throughs, cap-table reconciliation, customer-reference coordination — is CFO-led. CEO participates in specific diligence calls (customer references, technical-diligence calls if the CEO is the technical founder).

**Term sheet.** CFO-heavy for analysis; CEO-heavy for negotiation. The CFO analyses; the CEO negotiates. The negotiation reflects both workstreams.

**Closing.** Split. Legal drafting is CFO-led with company counsel. CEO relationship-maintenance with the lead continues.

## The specific reasons founder-only Series-A raises cost terms

At seed, a founder-only raise is often defensible. The seed deck is mostly about the founder, the story is mostly about the vision, and the specific process work is smaller in scope. Many successful seed rounds have been closed by first-time founder-CEOs running the process alone.

At Series-A, the pattern breaks. Series-A funds underwrite on traction, unit economics, and operating discipline — not on founder charisma alone. A founder-only raise costs terms in specific ways:

**Cost 1 — model quality suffers.** The Series-A financial model is a load-bearing artifact. A founder-CEO doing the model, the deck, the partner meetings, and the process management is doing four jobs. The model that comes out of that division of attention is usually the weakest — because the CEO is time-boxed and the model requires focused, uninterrupted work. A weak model in Series-A diligence produces the specific outcome: "we like the story but we'd want to see a more defensible model," which is either a delay or a pass.

**Cost 2 — diligence responsiveness suffers.** Fund diligence expects specific responses in specific timeframes. A CFO can turn around a sensitivity request in 24 hours. A CEO with no CFO can take a week. The fund reads the delay as a signal of operational discipline, and the term sheet — if it comes — reflects the lower conviction.

**Cost 3 — the term-sheet negotiation goes worse.** Term-sheet analysis (mod-108) is specific work with specific expertise. A first-time-founder CEO negotiating a term sheet with no CFO support is a founder who does not know which clauses are market-standard, which are stretch, and which are aggressive. Feld & Mendelson's *Venture Deals* is written specifically to arm founders in this scenario, but reading a book is not the same as having a CFO analyse the specific term sheet against 20 prior term sheets the CFO has seen. The result: preference structures that the founder did not fully understand, board-composition terms that the founder accepted because "everyone has that," and anti-dilution clauses that the founder discovers three years later cost them 10% of the company.

**Cost 4 — the parallel-process choreography is broken.** A founder-CEO doing the partner meetings AND managing the funnel is a founder-CEO who is losing track of which fund is at which stage. Term sheets do not converge in a window because the founder cannot see the pattern in real time.

**Cost 5 — the board and legal coordination is missing.** Existing investors expect proactive updates. Company counsel needs to be involved from term-sheet arrival. A CEO handling both is missing calls, missing emails, and disappointing the specific parties who need to be well-managed.

**None of these costs is theoretical.** Each is a specific pattern observed by advisors and investors across many Series-A raises. The empirical practitioner writing (Suster, Wilson, Feld) has been consistent for two decades: the founder-CEO needs an operating partner in the Series-A raise, and if the company does not have a full-time CFO, a fractional CFO or an experienced finance advisor is worth engaging.

**The counterargument.** Some founders — repeat-founders with prior successful raises, founders with strong finance backgrounds themselves, founders in specific hot sectors where the demand-side dynamics tilt in their favor — can and do run Series-A raises alone at defensible terms. The counterargument is legitimate but narrow. It does not apply to first-time-founder Series-A raises in normal markets, which is the mode.

## When to hire the CFO — or the interim CFO

**A full-time CFO at seed is often over-hire.** Seed companies at $250K-$1M ARR usually cannot justify a full-time CFO's compensation (~$250K-$400K cash for an experienced hire). The seed CEO frequently runs the raise with support from an operations lead or a fractional CFO.

**At Series-A, the CFO or CFO-equivalent becomes load-bearing.** Options:

- **Full-time CFO.** A hired CFO with prior CFO experience. Expensive ($300K-$500K+ cash + equity), but the load-bearing work of the Series-A raise justifies the hire in most cases.
- **Fractional CFO.** An experienced CFO working part-time (10-20 hours/week) across multiple companies. Costs $8K-$25K/month. A common choice for Series-A companies that don't yet need a full-time CFO but need the specific expertise. Firms like Kruze, Pilot, and Burkland offer fractional CFO services.
- **VP Finance or Head of Finance.** A senior finance lead who is not yet CFO-titled but has strong operating-finance experience. Common at pre-Series-B companies. Costs $200K-$300K cash. Handles the Series-A raise mechanics well; may not have the term-sheet-negotiation experience of a full CFO.
- **Trusted advisor.** A former operator or an experienced angel who acts as an informal CFO through the raise. Free or equity-only. Common at seed. Not a substitute for a full-time hire at Series-A.

**The specific triggers for hiring.** A CFO hire is typically made 3-9 months before the target Series-A close, so the CFO has time to:

- Build the driver-based three-statement model (mod-103).
- Author the data room (chapter 5).
- Get to know the business well enough to defend the model in diligence.
- Establish the operating-finance function that will scale into Series-A and beyond.

Waiting until the Series-A is in-flight to hire the CFO is a common mistake. The specific work the CFO does is 3-6 months of pre-work; hired at week 1 of the raise, the CFO cannot ramp fast enough.

## The specific collaboration mechanics

**Weekly (during the raise) — the Monday review.**

- CFO owns the funnel tracker.
- CFO prepares the one-page snapshot: stage counts, week-over-week movement, disqualification recommendations, priority processes for the week.
- CFO and CEO meet Monday morning for 30-60 minutes to align.
- CEO owns the final call on all disqualification and prioritisation decisions.
- CFO owns the follow-up on all diligence-related tasks; CEO owns the follow-up on all partner-relationship-related tasks.

**Ad-hoc (as term sheets arrive).**

- Term sheet arrives. CFO analyses within 24 hours.
- CFO produces the summary memo: key terms, comparison to NVCA reference, comparison to any other in-flight term sheets, pro-forma cap table, waterfall analysis, red-line recommendations.
- CFO and CEO meet to discuss the memo — usually within 24-48 hours of term-sheet arrival.
- CFO drafts the response to the term-sheeting fund (through counsel if legally required); CEO reviews.
- CEO has the direct partner conversation on the terms.

**Ad-hoc (as diligence requests arrive).**

- CFO owns diligence-request response for financial, cap-table, model, and legal items.
- CEO owns diligence-request response for customer references (introductions), product diligence (specific technical detail if the CEO is the technical founder), and any specific-story clarifications.

**Board-facing.**

- CFO owns the board pack for the raise-in-flight status update. Weekly during the raise; monthly otherwise.
- CEO owns the board discussion during board meetings.
- Both attend board meetings.

## The specific failure modes of the CFO-CEO partnership

**Failure mode 1 — CFO tries to be the CEO.** The CFO attempts to take the founder-relationship role in partner meetings, over-owns the story, or makes decisions that are the CEO's to make (which lead to accept, which specific pre-money to target). This is a role-boundary violation. The CEO's authority to make founder-decisions is not delegable.

**Failure mode 2 — CEO tries to be the CFO.** The CEO does not delegate the model, the data room, or the term-sheet analysis. The result is what the founder-only raise produces: bad terms because the specific expertise wasn't applied. The CEO's job is to delegate the specific work and to trust the CFO's work-product.

**Failure mode 3 — CFO and CEO disagree on the story but do not surface it.** The CFO thinks the market claim is unsupportable; the CEO thinks it is defensible. If the disagreement is not surfaced and resolved before the pitch is delivered, the CFO will not be able to defend the number in diligence, and the pitch will fall apart. Surface disagreements weekly.

**Failure mode 4 — CFO tries to work in a vacuum.** The CFO does not attend enough partner conversations to have context on what the CEO is saying to the fund. Diligence responses drift away from the pitch. Fund notices. Surface the pitch content to the CFO before it is delivered.

**Failure mode 5 — CEO fires the CFO mid-raise.** Very rare, but happens. Typically driven by CEO frustration with a CFO who is either genuinely underperforming or is doing the CFO job well but pushing the CEO in ways the CEO does not want to be pushed. A CFO fired mid-raise is a bad signal to the market. If the CFO is genuinely wrong for the role, address it before the raise starts or after it closes.

## The specific work-in-parallel example

A concrete example of the two workstreams running in parallel through a single week of the close:

**Monday.**

- CEO in a partner meeting with Fund A at 10am.
- CFO in a diligence call with Fund B's associate at 11am walking through the model.
- CEO takes a customer-reference intro call with Fund C at 2pm.
- CFO delivers the response to Fund B's follow-up diligence-request list by 5pm.

**Tuesday.**

- Term sheet arrives from Fund A at 9am.
- CFO drops other work, spends the day analysing the term sheet: NVCA red-line, pro-forma cap table, waterfall analysis at three exit scenarios, comparison to Fund B and Fund C's expected term-sheet terms.
- CEO takes a partner meeting with Fund D at 3pm — still running the funnel because Fund A's term sheet is not signed.
- CFO delivers the term-sheet analysis at 6pm.

**Wednesday.**

- CFO and CEO meet at 8am to discuss the analysis. Decision to negotiate specific terms with Fund A and to accelerate Fund B and Fund C to force competitive dynamics.
- CEO calls Fund A partner at 10am to negotiate specific terms.
- CFO calls Fund B and Fund C's associates to say "we have a term sheet in hand at market-rate terms — timeline for your decision?"
- Both attend a company board call at 3pm on the round status.

**And so on.** The workstreams are running in parallel, coordinated by the Monday review and by real-time updates as things happen. Neither the CEO nor the CFO could run this week alone.

## Common CFO-role traps

- **CFO not hired early enough.** Hired at week 1 of the raise, the CFO cannot ramp fast enough to be useful. Hire 3-9 months out.
- **Founder-CEO refusing to delegate the model.** Founder-CEO wants control of the numbers. Model quality suffers. Delegate.
- **CFO trying to run the founder-partner relationship.** Not the CFO's role.
- **CEO not looping the CFO into critical partner conversations.** CFO misses context, cannot align diligence responses to the pitch.
- **Weekly review skipped.** Momentum is lost. Disqualification is delayed. Signal is missed.
- **CFO not attending diligence calls.** Fund's diligence associate expects to talk to a finance lead. If the CEO is the only person on the calls, the fund questions the operating discipline.
- **CFO not producing the term-sheet analysis within 24 hours.** Every day of delay is a day the other in-flight funds are not being accelerated. Speed matters.
- **CFO not producing the pro-forma cap table.** Founder does not fully understand the dilution outcome. Accepts a term sheet on numbers they did not fully model.
- **Underestimating the load.** A Series-A raise plus the specific mechanics of chapter 5 (data room), chapter 6 (deck iteration), and chapter 7 (close) is a full-time job for one person. Two people is not too many.

## What good looks like

A well-run CFO-CEO partnership in a raise:

- The CFO is hired 3-9 months before the target close.
- The CEO owns narrative, delivery, and partner relationships; the CFO owns model, process, data room, term-sheet analysis, and choreography.
- Weekly Monday review is disciplined and produces the funnel snapshot, disqualification decisions, and priority processes for the week.
- Term sheets are analysed within 24 hours of arrival.
- Board and counsel are looped in from the moment the first term sheet lands.
- Disagreements between CFO and CEO are surfaced weekly, not stored up.
- Both attend the specific meetings appropriate to each role (CFO at diligence calls, CEO at partner meetings, both at board discussions).
- The CFO does not attempt to take the founder-relationship role; the CEO does not attempt to take the finance-analysis role.
- Post-close, the CFO manages the closing mechanics (signing, wires, cap-table update, 83(b) filings) and the CEO manages the transition of the newly-invested partners onto the board and into the ongoing governance rhythm (mod-110).

## Summary

- A raise has two parallel workstreams: the CEO owns narrative and relationships; the CFO owns model, process, data room, term-sheet analysis, and choreography.
- Both workstreams are load-bearing, especially at Series-A. Founder-only Series-A raises frequently cost terms in specific, quantifiable ways.
- The five specific costs of a founder-only Series-A: weaker model, slower diligence response, worse term-sheet negotiation, broken parallel-process choreography, and missing board-and-legal coordination.
- CFO hiring — full-time, fractional, or advisor-form — is typically 3-9 months before the target Series-A close so the CFO can build the model and the data room.
- The collaboration mechanics: weekly Monday review, ad-hoc term-sheet handling, specific division of meetings between CFO and CEO.
- Common failure modes: role-boundary violations in both directions, un-surfaced disagreements, and CFO working in a vacuum.
- The right partnership produces the specific-workstream productivity that closes at defensible terms.
