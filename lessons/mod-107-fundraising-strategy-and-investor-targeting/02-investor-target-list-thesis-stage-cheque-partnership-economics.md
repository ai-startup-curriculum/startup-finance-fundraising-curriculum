# Investor Target List — Thesis, Stage, Cheque Size, and Partnership Economics

## Why this matters

A well-sized round dies quickly if it is pitched to the wrong pool of investors. "Wrong" here is specific: the fund's stated thesis does not include the target company's sector, the fund's cheque-size band does not match the round's target lead cheque, the fund's most recent portfolio adds skew away from the target's stage, or the target partner at the fund does not have the internal authority — or the partnership-economics constraints — to lead the round. Every meeting the founder takes with such a fund is a founder-hour spent on a process that will not close, plus a signalling risk (chapter 7) if the fund passes and other funds notice.

The investor target list is the specific artifact that keeps the raise from that failure. It is not a list of "brand-name funds we should talk to." It is a specific, ranked, per-partner target list constructed against four filters — thesis, stage / cheque size, portfolio pattern, and partnership economics — and then further ranked by partner-level fit. The CFO's job is to build the list against the filters and defend the ranking; the founder-CEO's job is to bring the relationship intelligence, ultimately merging the two into an ordered execution list.

This chapter walks the four filters, the fund-math that the partnership-economics filter is running, and the practical mechanics of building the list from primary and secondary sources.

## The four filters

Every investor on the list should pass every one of the four filters:

1. **Thesis fit.** The fund's stated investment thesis includes the target's sector, technology, or market segment.
2. **Stage and cheque-size fit.** The fund's cheque-size band at the target stage matches the round's target lead-cheque size.
3. **Recent portfolio adds pattern.** The fund's *most recent* portfolio adds — not its founding portfolio — show they are actively deploying in the target's sector at the target stage.
4. **Partnership-economics fit.** The fund's fund-size × ownership-target × cheque-size math produces a partner-level economics case for leading the target's round.

A fund that passes fewer than all four is not eliminated from the world, but it is deprioritised. A fund that passes all four is a first-tier target. A fund that partially fits (thesis + stage but not cheque size, or cheque size + partnership economics but not recent-portfolio-pattern) is a second- or third-tier target that the CFO should be able to explain the specific mis-fit for.

## Filter 1 — thesis fit

**What "thesis" means.** A venture fund's thesis is the specific set of markets, technologies, and stages it invests in. It is published (in some form) on the fund's website, in the partners' blogs, in the fund's LP-facing materials, and in the public appearances of the fund's partners. Some funds have narrow, specific theses (deep-tech, or vertical SaaS, or B2B fintech, or open-source infrastructure). Some funds have broad "multi-stage" theses that state a preference without a specific narrow filter.

**Where the thesis is sourced from.**

- **The fund's website** — the "about" or "portfolio" or "focus" pages. Usually gives the sector-level focus and the stage-level focus.
- **The partners' blogs and content** — often more specific than the fund's website. Individual partners have specialised interests within the fund's broad thesis.
- **The fund's recent thesis publications** — many funds publish "the deep-tech thesis" or "the AI infra thesis" or similar. Read the specific-thesis writing.
- **The fund's podcast, if it has one** — many funds run a partner-hosted podcast. The topics of the podcast are a proxy for what the partners want to invest in.
- **The fund's most recent portfolio adds** — the operational proof of what the thesis actually funds, which is a much stronger signal than the stated thesis.

**Thesis fit is binary.** Either the target fits the fund's thesis or it does not. A fund whose stated thesis is "consumer marketplaces" is not going to lead a B2B fintech round no matter how good the pitch. A fund whose stated thesis is "AI infra + dev tools" is not going to lead a vertical healthcare SaaS round.

**The specific work.** For each fund on the initial list, write down the fund's stated thesis in one sentence and identify whether the target company is inside or outside it. If uncertain, look at the fund's most recent five to ten portfolio adds and identify the pattern. If those adds all sit in a sector the target is not in, thesis fit is a fail.

## Filter 2 — stage and cheque-size fit

**Stage.** The fund's declared investing stage — pre-seed, seed, Series-A, Series-B, growth, multi-stage — has to match the target round.

**Cheque-size band.** More specifically: what is the fund's typical *lead cheque* at the target stage? A fund that leads $2M seed rounds is not going to lead a $10M seed round. A fund that leads $15M Series-A rounds may not be interested in a $6M seed round (too small to matter to the fund's returns).

**Where the cheque-size band is sourced from.**

- **The fund's public materials** — some funds explicitly disclose cheque-size ranges. Many do not.
- **The fund's recent portfolio adds** — from Crunchbase, PitchBook, or the fund's own portfolio page, the announced round sizes give a proxy for the fund's cheque-size band. If the fund led a $12M Series-A last quarter and a $15M Series-A the quarter before, its typical Series-A lead cheque is in the $8M-$10M range (the fund does not lead the full round in most cases).
- **Direct conversations with founders in the fund's portfolio** — the most direct source. A founder whose round the fund led knows exactly what the fund put in and what its typical cheque was.
- **The Crunchbase / PitchBook fund profile page** — typically reports the fund's median cheque size and its median stage.

**The fund's mode.** Beyond the average cheque, does the fund *lead* or *follow*? A lead-first fund writes the term sheet and drives the partner meeting; a follow-first fund writes a smaller cheque into a round led by someone else. The distinction is load-bearing: a lead-first fund is a potential lead for the target round; a follow-first fund is not.

**The specific work.** For each fund on the list, note the typical lead cheque size and whether the fund is lead-first or follow-first at the target stage. Deprioritise the funds whose typical cheque is materially different from the target lead cheque. Split the list into "potential leads" and "potential followers" for use in the funnel design (chapter 3).

## Filter 3 — recent portfolio adds

**Why this matters.** A fund's stated thesis is what the fund says it invests in. Its most recent portfolio adds are what it actually invests in. The two frequently drift — a fund whose founding thesis was "consumer social" may in 2026 be adding almost entirely B2B SaaS deals because that is where its recent returns have been. The stated thesis is the marketing; the recent adds are the reality.

**Where recent adds are sourced from.**

- **The fund's own portfolio page** — sorted by date, if possible.
- **Crunchbase / PitchBook** — filter the fund's portfolio by year and by round-lead-status.
- **Signal / Signal-NFX / Harmonic** — some funds' recent activity is more visible through the third-party trackers.
- **The fund's LinkedIn** — many funds post "we invested in X" announcements. Sorted by date these are a good record.

**What to look for.**

- **Sector overlap with the target.** If the fund's last five adds are all in a sector the target is not in, the fund is probably not adding in the target's sector this vintage.
- **Stage overlap with the target round.** If the fund's last five adds are all Series-B (even though the fund's stated thesis says it does seed to Series-B), the fund is probably in Series-B mode for the current deployment cycle.
- **Cheque-size overlap with the target round.** If the fund's last five leads were $20M-$40M rounds and the target is a $5M seed, the fund's current deployment is not matching.
- **Time since last deploy.** A fund that has not led a deal in 6-9 months in the target's sector is either between funds or has moved off the sector. Both are useful signals.

**The specific work.** For each fund on the list, pull the last 8-12 rounds the fund has led (or been a meaningful participant in) and record the sector, stage, and cheque size. Note the pattern. Prioritise funds whose recent adds pattern-match the target round and deprioritise funds whose recent adds have drifted.

## Filter 4 — partnership economics

This is the filter most founder-CEOs skip and most CFOs recognise as load-bearing. Every VC fund is running specific partnership economics, and those economics dictate which rounds the fund can lead at which sizes. Understanding the math from the fund's side is the difference between pitching to a fund that can lead the round and pitching to a fund that structurally cannot.

**The fund-math.** A venture fund is a limited partnership with a target return (typically 3× net-to-LPs), a fund life (typically 10 years), a portfolio (typically 20-40 companies), and a cheque-size band per company. The fund's partners have committed to LPs to return the fund 3× (or better) over 10 years. That return is generated by a small number of "winners" in the portfolio that go 10-50× or more, offsetting the losses from the majority of the portfolio.

**The specific arithmetic** (worked at a canonical fund shape — verify the current-vintage numbers against Kupor *Secrets of Sand Hill Road* and current LP-side data):

- **Fund size:** $200M.
- **Target return:** 3× net-to-LPs → **~$600M gross returned** (adjusting for carry and fund expenses).
- **Portfolio construction:** ~25 companies.
- **Average cheque per company (initial + reserves for follow-on):** ~$8M (i.e. total $200M deployed across ~25 companies).
- **Expected winner distribution:** ~2-5 winners generating almost all of the fund return; the rest zero or partial return of capital. This is the "power-law" outcome distribution well-documented by Kupor and other GPs.
- **Required MOIC per winner:** If 3 winners are expected to return 80% of the fund's target gross return ($480M), each winner must return ~$160M on ~$8M invested — **~20× MOIC per winner.**

Now the CFO applies the math to the specific round.

**Question 1 — is this cheque size in the fund's band?** A $200M fund with a $8M average cheque cannot economically lead a $2M seed round. Leading a $2M round means writing a $1M-$1.5M cheque at seed, holding reserves for follow-on, and holding the position through the fund life. That cheque is too small to matter to a $200M fund; even a 30× MOIC ($30M returned) is 5% of the fund's target return. The fund is either not going to lead the round or, if it does, will assign it to a junior partner as a portfolio-building exercise rather than a real conviction bet.

**Question 2 — does the ownership target match?** Most institutional funds have a target ownership per portfolio company at the round they lead: 15-25% is common at Series-A, 10-20% at Series-B, sometimes higher. If the target round can only support a 5% ownership stake at the fund's typical cheque, the round is a bad fit for the fund's ownership target.

**Worked application.** Target company: a $6M seed round with an $18M pre-money ($24M post-money).

- **Small seed fund ($40M fund, $2M-$3M lead cheque, 20% ownership target at seed).** A $3M lead cheque at $24M post = 12.5% ownership. Below the fund's 20% target but within acceptable range. The fund can lead the round.
- **Mid-size seed fund ($150M fund, $5M-$7M lead cheque, 15-20% ownership target).** A $6M cheque at $24M post = 25% ownership. Above the fund's target range. The fund can lead the round but might prefer a smaller round (which the CFO should be prepared to say no to — see chapter 1 on over-raising) or a lower pre-money.
- **Multi-stage fund ($500M fund, $5M-$10M seed lead cheque, part of a longer-horizon strategy).** A $6M cheque at $24M post = 25% ownership. The fund's larger fund size means the seed cheque is a "portfolio-building exercise" — a way to get a seat at the table for the Series-A/B. Different underwriting than a pure-seed fund; frequently the pre-money is less contested because the fund is optimising for optionality on the follow-on.
- **Series-A fund ($400M fund, $12M-$18M lead cheque at Series-A).** A $6M seed round is too small for the fund to lead; the fund is not going to write a $6M cheque as a lead. It might write a $500K-$1M "get to know you" cheque as a follower, but that is not a lead-checking process. The fund is off the "potential leads" list; it may be on the "potential followers" list if follower cheques are being taken.

**The specific work.** For each fund on the list, run the arithmetic: fund size, typical lead cheque at this stage, ownership target, and the resulting ownership stake the target round produces. Note whether the fund is a natural fit, a stretch, or a mismatch. Deprioritise the mismatches; note the stretches so the founder-CEO can be intentional about how much time to spend.

## Partner-level fit — the second dimension

The four filters above evaluate the fund. The partner-level filter evaluates the specific partner inside the fund who would sponsor the deal. This matters because a fund does not invest — a partner does. A fund with perfect thesis / stage / cheque-size / partnership-economics fit that has no partner internally who wants to sponsor the deal is not going to lead the round.

**What "partner-level fit" means.**

- **Sector specialisation.** Which partner in the fund covers the target's sector? Some funds have explicit sector coverage; others have implicit sector coverage that emerges from the partner's portfolio.
- **Recent portfolio activity.** Which partner in the fund led the fund's most recent adds in the target's sector? That partner is the most likely sponsor.
- **Founder-market fit at the partner level.** Which partner has invested in similar-profile founders? A partner who invests in second-time B2B SaaS founders is a better fit for a second-time B2B SaaS founder than a partner who typically invests in first-time consumer founders.
- **Deal-flow bandwidth.** Which partners are actively taking meetings right now vs. which are heads-down on portfolio-support work? A partner who just closed three deals in the last month is typically slower to engage on a new one.

**Where partner-level fit is sourced from.**

- **The partner's blog / Twitter / public appearances** — often the strongest signal on what the partner is looking for.
- **Portfolio conversations** — a founder whose company was led by the target partner is the most direct source on how that partner underwrites, how quickly, and what they weight.
- **Warm-intro conversations** — before the founder pitches, the introducer (chapter 3) is a source on what the partner is currently focused on.

**The specific work.** For each first-tier fund, identify the target partner. Write down one sentence on the partner-level fit hypothesis: why this partner is likely to be the sponsor. If no specific partner is identifiable, deprioritise the fund — pitching to a fund without knowing who the sponsor is a lower-hit-rate motion.

## Building the list — mechanics

The concrete workflow for constructing the list:

1. **Initial universe.** Start from the current-quarter Crunchbase / PitchBook list of active VC funds at the target stage, filtered by geography (if location matters — some rounds still require in-region investors, though many now do not). Typical initial universe: 100-200 funds.
2. **Thesis filter.** Cut to funds whose stated thesis or recent portfolio adds pattern-match the target's sector. Typical output: 30-60 funds.
3. **Stage / cheque-size filter.** Cut to funds whose lead cheque at the target stage matches the round. Typical output: 20-40 funds.
4. **Recent-portfolio-adds filter.** Cut to funds whose most recent adds pattern-match the target stage and sector. Typical output: 15-30 funds.
5. **Partnership-economics filter.** Run the fund-math per fund. Deprioritise mismatches. Typical output: 12-25 funds.
6. **Partner-level fit.** Identify the specific target partner for each remaining fund. Note the fit hypothesis. Typical output: 10-20 funds with a specific partner mapping.
7. **Tier the list.** Tier 1 (5-8 funds) — first-tier fit on all filters, high partner-level confidence, warm-intro path available. Tier 2 (5-10 funds) — first-tier fit on most filters, moderate partner-level confidence. Tier 3 (5-10 funds) — partial fit, worth pitching if the top tiers stall.
8. **Sequence the outreach.** Tier 2 first (see chapter 3 on funnel design — pitching to lower-tier funds first calibrates the pitch before the top-tier meetings), tier 1 second, tier 3 as backfill.

**Sources for the initial universe:**

- **Crunchbase Pro / PitchBook** — most comprehensive fund databases.
- **NVCA Venture Monitor** — sector- and stage-specific fund activity.
- **Signal by NFX** — investor-search tool with public partner-level profiles.
- **Harmonic** — investor-search tool.
- **Founder-CEO's network** — most experienced founders have a running list of funds they've been in touch with, which is a valuable seed for the target list.

## Common target-list traps

- **Building a "brand names" list.** Sequoia, a16z, Benchmark, Founders Fund, Accel, Kleiner Perkins — the brand-name funds are on every founder's list. If the target company is not at the stage / sector / traction level the brand-name funds are underwriting, most of them are not going to lead the round, and the founder-hours spent pitching them are wasted. Build the list against the fit filters, not against brand.
- **Ignoring the ownership-target math.** A $500M multi-stage fund pitched a $2M cheque seed round will politely take the meeting and not lead. Ignoring the fund-math produces a list that is full of these meetings.
- **Skipping the partner-level mapping.** "We're pitching Sequoia" is not a strategy. "We're pitching partner X at Sequoia who covers B2B SaaS and led the last three seed rounds in the sector" is.
- **Using stale portfolio-adds data.** A fund's 2020 portfolio and its 2026 portfolio may look nothing alike. Use the most recent 12-18 months of adds.
- **Confusing the fund's stated thesis with what it actually funds.** The stated thesis is marketing. The recent adds are the reality. When they diverge, trust the adds.
- **Listing follower funds as potential leads.** A fund that has not led a round in 18 months is not going to lead the target round. It might follow. Mark it as a follower.
- **Not tiering the list.** An untiered list produces a random outreach order, which produces uncalibrated first meetings with the top-tier funds. Tier and sequence intentionally.

## Fund-math worked example — a specific target

**Target company.** Series-A B2B SaaS, $2M ARR, growing 2.5x YoY, 115% NRR. Raising $12M at $40M pre-money ($52M post-money).

**Candidate fund A.** $150M fund, seed-through-Series-A, typical Series-A lead cheque $6M-$10M, ownership target 15%.

- Cheque as % of round: $8M (mid-band) of $12M = 67% of the round. Reasonable lead cheque, would need a co-lead or follower for the balance.
- Ownership at $8M cheque, $52M post = 15.4%. Right at the fund's target.
- Fund-math: $150M fund, ~25 companies. $8M cheque needs to return 20-25× to meaningfully move the fund. That means an exit at $52M post-money × 20× MOIC = ~$1B exit, before subsequent dilution. Believable for a $2M ARR B2B SaaS if the story is strong.
- **Verdict: strong fit. Tier 1.**

**Candidate fund B.** $600M fund, multi-stage (Series-A through growth), typical Series-A lead cheque $12M-$20M, ownership target 15-20%.

- Cheque as % of round: $15M lead cheque > $12M round. Fund would either want to bring the round up to $15M (over-raise problem — see chapter 1) or lead a smaller cheque at 15% ownership ~ $7.8M, which is below fund's typical lead cheque.
- Fund-math: $600M fund. A $7.8M cheque at 15% ownership needing 20-25× MOIC is a $150M-$200M return. Small relative to the fund's target. This deal is at best a "get in early on a promising Series-A" bet from the fund; the underwriting is not going to have the same intensity as at Fund A.
- **Verdict: stretch fit. Tier 2. Founder should consider whether taking the fund's larger-round pressure is worth the trade — a $15M raise may be over-sized for the plan (chapter 1).**

**Candidate fund C.** $75M seed fund, typical seed lead cheque $2M-$4M, ownership target 15-20%.

- Cheque as % of round: $4M is 33% of the $12M round. Too small to lead as the sole participant; if leading, would need multiple co-leads.
- Fund-math: $75M fund, $3M average cheque, ~25 companies. Fund is in Series-A territory only by stretching outside its declared seed thesis. Recent adds show mostly seed rounds, no Series-A leads.
- **Verdict: mis-fit. Tier 3 or off-list. Not a lead. Might be a follower for a small cheque.**

## What good looks like

A well-constructed target list:

- Has 12-25 funds after all filters.
- Is tiered into 5-8 tier-1, 5-10 tier-2, and 5-10 tier-3 funds.
- Has a specific target partner mapped to each tier-1 and tier-2 fund.
- Documents the specific thesis / stage / cheque-size / recent-adds / partnership-economics fit hypothesis per fund.
- Cites the sources of the fit data (thesis-source, portfolio-adds-source, cheque-size-source).
- Separates potential leads from potential followers.
- Sequences outreach starting from tier-2 (to calibrate) and moving to tier-1.
- Is a living document updated as new signal comes in during the raise (a fund's partner leaves, a new portfolio-add reshapes the thesis, a warm-intro conversation reveals the partner is heads-down for a quarter).

## Summary

- The investor target list is constructed against four filters: thesis fit, stage / cheque-size fit, recent-portfolio-adds pattern, and partnership economics (fund-size × ownership × cheque-size math).
- Thesis fit is binary — the fund's stated thesis and recent adds either include the target sector or they do not.
- Cheque-size fit is a match between the fund's typical lead cheque at the target stage and the round's target lead cheque.
- Partnership economics — the fund-math — determines whether the round is a real bet for the fund or a portfolio-building side note; a fund whose fund-math cannot make the round meaningful is not going to lead it.
- Partner-level fit is a second dimension on top of fund-level fit: a fund does not invest, a partner does; identifying the sponsoring partner is a required step.
- The list is tiered (tier 1 / tier 2 / tier 3) and sequenced intentionally, with tier-2 typically pitched first to calibrate before tier-1.
- Common traps: pitching brand names without the fit filters, ignoring partnership economics, skipping the partner-level mapping, using stale portfolio data, and failing to separate potential leads from potential followers.
