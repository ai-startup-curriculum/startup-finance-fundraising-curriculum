# Close Choreography — Parallel Processes, Signalling, and the Term-Sheet Window

## Why this matters

The close is the specific two-to-four-week window at the end of the raise when term sheets converge, the lead is chosen, and the round closes. Everything before the close — funnel design, deck iteration, data-room preparation — is preparation for this window. Everything the CFO and CEO have built comes to bear here.

A well-choreographed close produces convergent term sheets in a compressed window, allows an honest competitive dynamic between leads, closes at defensible terms, and does not damage the relationships with the funds that pass. A badly-choreographed close produces one term sheet at a time in sequence (with no competition), signals distress to the funds still in the process, exposes confidential information across funds who compare notes, and closes at terms that are worse than the market would have supported.

The choreography is largely the CFO's job. The CEO drives the individual partner conversations; the CFO manages the process across those conversations. This chapter walks the mechanics: how to compress term sheets into a window, how to manage signalling risk from funds moving at different speeds, when to accept a term sheet vs. shop it, and the specific slow-no / fast-yes patterns to recognise.

## The core objective — parallel processes converging in a window

The single most important choreography objective: **term sheets should converge in a window of 1-2 weeks**. Not 8 weeks apart. Not 3 weeks apart. Ideally within 5 business days of each other, so the CEO can honestly say to Fund A "we have a term sheet in hand and expect another one this week" without lying.

**Why convergence matters.** The valuation, preference structure, and non-price terms on a term sheet are meaningfully more favorable when the fund knows the founder has an alternative. That is not manipulation — it is the market clearing at the price the market will support. A fund that has a monopoly on the round writes a term sheet that maximises the fund's outcome. A fund that has a competitor two days behind writes a term sheet that maximises the fund's *win probability*, which usually means better terms for the founder.

The empirical practitioner data (Feld & Mendelson *Venture Deals*; Suster *Both Sides of the Table*) has been consistent for two decades: rounds closed with competitive dynamics price 10-30% higher and land better non-price terms than rounds closed on a single term sheet, controlling for company quality. The convergence is worth engineering.

**How to engineer convergence.** Three specific mechanics:

1. **Sequence the funnel to converge.** Tier-2 funds are pitched first (chapter 2 / 3). By the time tier-1 funds are in serious diligence, tier-2 processes are 2-3 weeks ahead — meaning tier-2 term sheets may arrive first, giving the CEO leverage in the tier-1 partner meetings. Tier-1 term sheets arrive within a couple weeks of tier-2.

2. **Time-box every process.** In every serious partner conversation, the CEO establishes the target close window: "we are targeting a term sheet decision by [specific date] and to close by [specific date + 4 weeks]." A fund that cannot meet the timeline is either given a specific concession (a 1-week extension for a well-progressed fund) or classified as slow-no.

3. **Force the timing conversation.** In the third or fourth meeting with a fund, the CEO explicitly asks: "when can we expect to hear back on your partnership decision?" The fund's answer to that question is the schedule. If the answer is vague ("we're still working through it"), the process is not close to a term sheet.

## The close week — day-by-day

A well-choreographed close week for a seed or Series-A round has a specific day-by-day pattern. Names will vary; the substance is stable.

**Monday.**

- CFO and CEO review the state of every process.
- Confirm which funds are in the "term-sheet imminent" bucket, which are in the "partner meeting this week," which are in the "diligence closing this week," and which have already passed or are slow-no.
- Prepare the current-state momentum narrative for the week's conversations.

**Tuesday.**

- Convert-to-conversation: for the three top funds, CEO has direct conversations to force the timing question.
- CFO handles any diligence-close requests (references, model walk-throughs, contract review) that are on the critical path.

**Wednesday.**

- Term-sheet arrival day 1. Any fund that promised a term sheet by "end of the week" is delivering.
- CFO immediately analyses each term sheet against the NVCA reference and the mod-108 checklist. Same-day.
- CEO informs the other funds in the top-three of the term sheet's arrival — not the specific terms, but the fact of the arrival. This is a load-bearing action: it triggers the compression of the other funds' processes.

**Thursday.**

- The other top-three funds are now on the clock. Expect either accelerated commitments or specific asks (a phone call with the founder to align on non-price terms, a partner-meeting on Friday, a term sheet by Monday).
- CFO's term-sheet analysis is delivered to CEO and to the board.
- Board discussion (if the board is engaged) on the terms and the recommended lead.

**Friday.**

- Second term sheet arrives (ideally). Now there is real competition.
- CFO produces the side-by-side term-sheet comparison against the NVCA reference (mod-108 chapter 1).
- CEO and CFO discuss the leads and the choreography for the weekend.

**Weekend + following Monday.**

- Negotiation of specific terms with the preferred lead. Possibly a third term sheet arrives.
- Decision by end of Monday on which term sheet to accept.
- Signed term sheet by Tuesday.

**Following 2-4 weeks.**

- Confirmatory diligence, legal drafting, closing.

**This is the compressed pattern.** Not every raise runs this cleanly, but the mechanics are worth aiming for.

## Signalling risk

**Definition.** A fund passes on the deal, and the pass sends a signal — to other funds in the market, to future funds looking at future rounds, or to the fund's own portfolio — that the deal is worse than it actually is. That signal costs the founder.

**Specific mechanics.**

- **Fund A passes on the round after a partner meeting.** Fund A tells its friends at Fund B and Fund C that it passed. Fund B and Fund C, still in first meetings with the same company, now have a specific negative signal. Their conviction drops. The founder does not know this is happening.
- **Multiple funds pass in a compressed window.** The market (small, chatty, and networked) knows the round is not going well. New funds coming into first meetings arrive skeptical.
- **A fund passes after diligence.** The pass is a specific "the diligence uncovered something concerning" signal. Fund counsel talks to other fund counsels. Diligence-post pass is meaningfully worse signalling than early pass.

**Signalling from unengaged funds.** The specific failure mode: a fund on the target list is asked for a first meeting; the fund does not respond. The founder does not know why. If several funds do not respond, the market knows something. But the founder does not.

**Managing signalling risk.**

1. **Do not name-drop funds.** As covered in chapter 3, naming specific funds ("we're in a partner meeting with Fund X") is a broken norm. It also creates the specific vulnerability that if Fund X passes, the funds who heard the name now know Fund X passed.

2. **Compress the process.** A compressed process — 6-8 weeks for seed, 8-12 for Series-A — reduces the window in which signalling can accumulate. A raise that runs for 6 months is a raise where signalling metastasises.

3. **Manage the pass conversation.** When a fund passes, thank them, ask for two or three sentences on why, and offer to keep them in the loop on the round's outcome. Do not push. A fund that passes cleanly and courteously is less likely to gossip.

4. **Recognise when the signal has turned.** If several funds are unresponsive or slow-passing, the market may have soured on the deal. At that point, the CFO's advice to the CEO is often "pause, reset the pitch, re-approach in 3-6 months." Pushing through a soured market rarely produces a good outcome.

5. **Diversify the fund-network sources.** Different funds gossip in different networks. Silicon Valley funds gossip with Silicon Valley funds. East Coast funds gossip with East Coast funds. Multi-stage funds have their own network. A target list that spans networks reduces the concentrated-signalling risk.

## Slow-no vs. fast-yes patterns

Two specific patterns to recognise in the close window:

**Slow no.** The fund does not say no but does not move forward. The pattern was discussed in chapter 3 for the funnel-management context; in the close context, the specific manifestation is:

- Sponsoring partner is enthusiastic in one-on-one conversations but does not schedule the partner meeting.
- Diligence requests are not being sent.
- Partner meeting keeps getting rescheduled for scheduling reasons.
- Two weeks pass between fund-side communications when other funds are moving weekly.
- Sponsoring partner starts routing communications through the associate.

**How to force the resolution.** A specific CEO conversation: "we are targeting a term sheet decision by [specific date]. If that timeline does not work on your end, please tell us — we can adjust or we can proceed with the other funds." This forces the fund to either commit to the timeline or explicitly step back. Either outcome is better than continued ambiguity.

**Fast yes.** The fund moves quickly. Partner meeting scheduled within 5 business days of first meeting. Term sheet offered within 2-3 weeks of first meeting. Fast-yes is a strong signal — but sometimes a *trap*.

**The fast-yes trap.** A fund that moves before the founder has time to run the process is a fund that is trying to close the round before competitive dynamics can develop. The specific mechanics:

- Fast term sheet with an aggressive **exploding term** (a deadline for acceptance measured in days, not weeks).
- Fast term sheet with a **no-shop / exclusivity clause** that prevents the founder from continuing to run other processes.
- Fast term sheet with terms that are 10-20% below what a competitive dynamic would have produced.

**How to handle a fast yes.** Two specific tactics:

1. **Slow-play the acceptance while continuing the process.** Do not accept the term sheet immediately. Say "thank you, we need [specific time — typically 5-10 business days] to review with our advisors and board." Use that time to accelerate the other in-flight processes to converge.

2. **Explicitly discuss the exclusivity terms with the fund.** A well-intentioned lead will accept a modest exclusivity period (7-14 days) that allows the founder to complete confirmatory conversations with other funds in-flight. A lead that demands a longer exclusivity or refuses to give any is a lead that is trying to prevent competitive dynamics.

**Neither slow-no nor fast-yes is automatically bad.** A slow-no can be a fund with a specific legitimate constraint (partner on vacation, fund between closings). A fast-yes can be a fund with high conviction that has known the sector for years. The point is to recognise the pattern and respond to it — not to assume the pattern determines the outcome.

## When to accept a lead's term sheet vs. shop it

Once a term sheet is on the table, the CEO has a specific decision: accept the term sheet, negotiate specific terms and then accept, or "shop" the term sheet (share the terms with other in-flight funds to accelerate their processes).

**Accept immediately.** Rare. Only if the term sheet is materially better than what the other in-flight processes will produce and the exclusivity terms are reasonable. Almost never the right move on the first term sheet.

**Negotiate then accept.** Common. The first term sheet is rarely at its final terms. Specific items to negotiate — before accepting — include the pre-money, the option-pool sizing, the liquidation preference structure, the board composition, and the specific protective provisions (mod-108).

**Shop the term sheet.** Legitimate under specific conditions. Sharing the fact of the term sheet with other in-flight funds ("we have a term sheet in hand at [round-level terms]") is acceptable and expected. Sharing the specific fund's identity or the specific full term sheet is a broken norm. The right formulation: "we have a term sheet with market-rate terms from a well-regarded lead and are looking for a decision from you by [specific date]." Other funds will infer what they need to.

**Do not "shop" a signed term sheet during the exclusivity period.** Once a term sheet is signed and exclusivity is in effect (typically 30-45 days), the CEO cannot shop the terms. Doing so is a breach of the term sheet and can end the deal.

**Do not accept a term sheet you cannot live with.** A term sheet with a 3x participating liquidation preference or a founder-vesting acceleration clause the founder cannot survive is a term sheet to reject even if it is the only term sheet on the table. Better to close no round than close a round on terms that destroy the company's next-round optionality (mod-108).

## The CFO's role in the close

The CFO's specific work in the close window:

- **Term-sheet analysis.** Every term sheet is analysed against the NVCA reference and the mod-108 checklist same-day. The CFO produces a red-line and a memo to the CEO and board.
- **Pro-forma cap table.** Every term sheet's pre-money, post-money, option-pool, and preference structure produces a specific pro-forma cap table. The CFO builds it and shows the CEO the specific dilution and specific waterfall implications.
- **Model updates.** The operating plan may adjust based on the specific round size vs. the specific term sheet. The CFO updates the mod-103 model.
- **Board coordination.** The board (or the informal board of existing SAFE / seed investors) needs to be informed. The CFO manages the board-facing communication.
- **Legal coordination.** Company counsel needs to be looped in the moment the first term sheet arrives. The CFO handles the legal-side choreography.
- **Data-room access.** The lead's confirmatory-diligence access (tier 4 from chapter 5) is granted; other funds' access is revoked once a term sheet is signed.
- **Investor communication.** Every existing SAFE holder, note holder, and priced-round investor with information rights receives the notification the round is closing.

**The CFO does not drive the founder-lead relationship.** That is the CEO. The CFO does not choose the lead; the CEO does, with the CFO's input on terms and cap-table impact. The distinction is important: the CFO's job is to inform the decision, not to make it.

## Managing multiple term sheets

When the choreography works and two or three term sheets arrive in a window, the CFO's specific management work:

- **Side-by-side comparison.** A single sheet or single document that lays out the specific terms of every term sheet against a consistent set of dimensions (pre-money, option-pool, preference, board, protective provisions, anti-dilution, drag-along, exclusivity, closing timeline). The comparison makes the differences legible.
- **Pro-forma cap tables for each.** The specific dilution outcome under each term sheet.
- **Waterfall analyses for each.** The specific liquidation-preference outcome under each term sheet at several exit scenarios (mod-104 chapter 4).
- **Relationship / soft-factor commentary.** The CFO documents the qualitative factors — how the CEO experienced the sponsoring partner, the fund's reputation for follow-on, portfolio-value-add, specific board-composition preferences. These are not term-sheet-visible but are load-bearing to the decision.
- **Recommendation.** The CFO produces a specific recommendation on which term sheet to accept and specifically why. The CEO and board review; the CEO makes the final call.

## The passed-fund conversation

Every fund that made it deep into the process but was not chosen deserves a specific closing conversation. The CFO or CEO thanks the fund, explains the decision (in general terms — do not name the chosen lead's specifics), and where appropriate offers to keep the fund in the loop for the next round.

**Why this matters.** The funds that passed on this round are candidate leads for the next round. A fund that made it to a partner meeting and was not chosen has a lot of context on the company; if they are treated well in the pass conversation, they may lead the next round. A fund that is treated dismissively will not.

## The specific choreography for a soured process

Not every raise closes. Some raises reach a point where the market has said no and the founder has to reset. The specific choreography for that scenario:

1. **Recognise the pattern.** Multiple passes, unresponsive tier-1 funds, deck-engagement dropping. The CFO's honest funnel-management (chapter 3) is what surfaces this.
2. **Pause the process.** Stop initiating new outreach. Do not force additional first meetings.
3. **Assess the specific problem.** Is it the market (macro conditions)? The traction (below the bar)? The pitch (not landing)? The team (specific perception issues)? The diligence (a specific red flag being found)?
4. **Address the specific problem.** Depending on the diagnosis, options include: waiting 3-6 months for the market, buying more time with a bridge (mod-109), rebuilding the pitch and re-approaching, adding a specific hire, or fixing a specific data-room issue.
5. **Reset the target list.** The funds that passed this round are candidate leads for the next round, but only after a specific reset event (traction milestone, key hire, market change). Do not re-approach them without one.
6. **Communicate honestly with existing investors.** SAFE holders and existing seed investors need to know the round did not close, the plan going forward, and the runway implications.

**A paused raise is not a failed company.** It is a specific process outcome that needs specific management. Many successful companies have a paused raise in their history. The pause is a choice, not a defeat.

## Common close-choreography traps

- **Serialising the funnel.** Meeting funds one at a time so that term sheets arrive weeks apart. Eliminates competitive dynamics.
- **Not compressing the process.** A 6-month raise metastasises signalling risk.
- **Accepting a term sheet without competitive dynamics.** The first term sheet is rarely the best-terms term sheet.
- **Ignoring signalling.** Not noticing when multiple funds pass in a compressed window is missing the signal that the market has soured.
- **Name-dropping funds in one process to another.** Broken norm; leaks confidential information across funds who compare notes.
- **Sharing a signed term sheet.** During exclusivity, sharing the specific term sheet is a breach.
- **Not negotiating the first term sheet.** First term sheets are opening bids, not final offers.
- **Signing a term sheet with an aggressive exploding deadline.** Sometimes legitimate; often a lead trying to prevent competition.
- **Missing the fast-yes trap.** A fast term sheet with harsh exclusivity is a term sheet the CFO should flag before the CEO accepts.
- **Failing to coordinate with board and counsel.** Both need to be involved from the moment the first term sheet arrives.
- **Treating passed funds poorly.** They are candidate leads for the next round.
- **Not recognising the soured-process pattern.** Pushing through a market that has said no is expensive.

## What good looks like

A well-run close:

- Compresses term sheets into a 5-10 business day window through deliberate funnel sequencing and timing conversations.
- Produces 2-3 competitive term sheets rather than a single term sheet.
- Analyses every term sheet against the NVCA reference within 24 hours of arrival.
- Produces per-term-sheet pro-forma cap tables and waterfall analyses.
- Manages the exclusivity period to allow completion of in-flight processes without breaching signed terms.
- Handles signalling risk by not name-dropping, compressing the process, and diversifying the target-list network.
- Recognises fast-yes and slow-no patterns and responds to them intentionally.
- Preserves relationships with passed funds for the next round.
- Executes a clean pause if the market has soured, without forcing a bad close.

## Summary

- The close is a specific 2-4 week window where the funnel converges on term sheets and the round closes.
- The core objective is to compress multiple term sheets into a 5-10 business day window so competitive dynamics produce better terms.
- Compression is engineered through funnel sequencing (tier-2 first, tier-1 second), time-boxing every process, and forcing the timing conversation.
- Signalling risk — passes from one fund leaking to others — is managed by not name-dropping, compressing the process, handling passes courteously, and diversifying the target-list network.
- Slow-no and fast-yes patterns are both worth recognising: slow-no is forced-resolved with a specific timing conversation; fast-yes is protected against by slow-playing acceptance and negotiating exclusivity terms.
- Term sheets are analysed against the NVCA reference the day they arrive, pro-forma cap tables and waterfall analyses are built, and the CEO makes the final acceptance call with the CFO's recommendation.
- Multiple term sheets in a window are compared side-by-side across a consistent dimension set (pre-money, preference, board, protective provisions) plus qualitative relationship factors.
- Passed funds are treated with care — they are candidate leads for the next round.
- Sometimes the raise pauses; recognising a soured process and pausing intentionally is a better outcome than forcing a bad close.
