# Board Committees — Audit, Compensation, and Transaction

## Why this matters

At Series-A, most startup boards operate as a single unified body — every decision goes through the full board; there are no formal committees. That works because the board is small (five directors), the decisions are limited in category, and the CEO / CFO can drive the whole calendar themselves.

By Series-B or Series-C, that model stops scaling. The audit-and-financial-controls work grows too specialised for a general board discussion; the compensation work grows too sensitive to run in front of the CEO; the transactional M&A / financing work moves too fast for the quarterly cadence. In parallel, the board itself grows — to six, seven, eight directors — and full-board discussion becomes a coordination problem.

The mechanism the corporate governance canon has converged on for scaling the board is **committees**. A committee is a subset of directors, empowered by charter to make decisions in a specific scope (subject to full-board oversight and, for some decisions, full-board approval). Committees allow deep work to happen with the right people in the room, without consuming the full board's time or diluting the full board's discussion.

This chapter installs the three committees the CFO will most commonly encounter — audit, compensation, and transaction — with the charter templates, the cadence, the independent-director requirements, and the specific triggers for forming each one. The CFO is the audit committee's most-important operating counterpart, the compensation committee's data-and-benchmark provider, and the transaction committee's execution driver — the CFO's relationship with each committee is as important as the CFO's relationship with the full board.

## The audit committee

**Purpose.** Oversight of financial reporting, internal controls, and the outside auditor. In a public company, the audit committee is required by SEC and stock-exchange rules (see resources.md for the specific NYSE Corporate Governance Standards §303A.06-07 and Nasdaq Rule 5605(c)); in a private company, the audit committee is a governance-quality choice, but by the time the company is preparing for an IPO, the committee needs to be functioning at public-company standard for at least 12-18 months before the S-1.

**When to form.** Reference triggers:

- **Series-B for pre-IPO track companies.** If the company has a plausible IPO path within 3-5 years, form the audit committee at or shortly after Series-B.
- **Series-A for regulated-sector companies.** Fintech, healthtech, cybersecurity, and other regulated sectors typically face auditor or regulator pressure to form an audit committee earlier — sometimes at Series-A.
- **When the outside audit is engaged.** Whenever the first outside audit is engaged (typically Series-B for SaaS, Series-A for regulated), the audit committee should be functioning as of that engagement.
- **When investor covenants require it.** Some Series-B and later financings include audit-committee-formation covenants in the investor-rights agreement.

**Charter — the reference structure.** The audit committee charter is a formal document, adopted by the full board, that defines the committee's scope and authority. Reference sections:

1. **Purpose.** The committee's specific purpose in oversight terms.
2. **Composition.** The number of members (typically three); the requirement that all members be independent directors; the requirement that at least one member qualify as an "audit committee financial expert" (a specific definition drawn from SEC Regulation S-K Item 407(d)(5)); the process for appointment.
3. **Authority and responsibilities.** Selection and engagement of the outside auditor; approval of audit fees; approval of non-audit services above a threshold (typically $10K-$25K in aggregate per fiscal year); review of quarterly and annual financial statements; oversight of internal controls; oversight of financial-reporting risk; oversight of any whistleblower or complaint procedures.
4. **Meetings.** Frequency (typically quarterly, sometimes more often); requirement to meet privately with the outside auditor at each meeting; requirement to meet privately with the CFO at each meeting; requirement to meet in executive session (without any management present) at each meeting.
5. **Reporting.** The committee's reporting obligation to the full board.
6. **Committee resources.** Authority to engage independent legal, accounting, or other advisors at the company's expense.
7. **Annual charter review.** Requirement to review and reaffirm (or amend) the charter annually.

Public-company audit committee charters are more elaborate; NYSE and Nasdaq require specific charter contents. Pre-IPO private-company committees typically adopt a slightly-simplified version of the public-company charter to make the transition to public status smoother.

**Cadence.** Quarterly, coincident with the quarterly board pack cycle. Some audit committees also hold a session immediately before each quarterly full-board meeting (typically the day before) to review the quarter's financials in depth; then the committee chair reports out at the full-board meeting. Between-meeting activity: the committee chair maintains a running dialogue with the outside auditor and the CFO; between-meeting written consents are used for time-critical items (auditor engagement, non-audit-service approvals).

**Independent-director requirement.** All members must be independent (specific definition per the charter, matching the public-company definition — no employment relationship with the company or its subsidiaries in the last three years; no material consulting or advisory relationship; no immediate-family employment relationship; no material investment relationship other than a director's stock grant). Investor-affiliated directors (a Series-A lead's partner) are typically *not* considered independent for audit committee purposes — the audit committee is meant to be a check on management *and* on major stockholders.

**The financial expert requirement.** At least one member must qualify as an "audit committee financial expert" under SEC Regulation S-K Item 407(d)(5). Reference criteria: (a) understanding of GAAP, (b) experience preparing or auditing financial statements of comparable-sized companies, (c) understanding of internal controls, (d) understanding of audit committee functions, (e) education or experience obtaining the above. Typical qualifying backgrounds: a former CFO, a former audit partner at a Big Four firm, a former controller of a public company, occasionally a senior finance academic. The financial-expert designation is disclosed in the annual report; for private companies pre-IPO, the designation is a governance-quality mark, not a public disclosure.

**The CFO's operating relationship with the audit committee.** The CFO is not a member (management does not sit on the audit committee) but is the committee's primary operating counterpart. The CFO:

- Attends every audit committee meeting (unless the committee has explicitly asked management to step out for a specific portion).
- Presents the financials, the reconciliations, and any specific accounting-policy elections up for discussion.
- Works with the outside auditor on the audit plan and the audit results; the committee approves both, but the CFO drives the operational work.
- Meets privately with the audit committee chair between meetings on any material accounting, controls, or auditor-relationship matter.
- Ensures the audit committee has independent access to the outside auditor and to any other advisors it wants to engage.

The audit committee's meeting with the outside auditor without management present is a specific governance mechanism to allow the auditor to raise any concern the auditor did not feel it could raise with management in the room. The CFO's job is to *support* that mechanism, not to view it defensively. A CFO who is threatened by the auditor's private access to the audit committee is a CFO who will end up in a difficult conversation with the audit committee chair.

## The compensation committee

**Purpose.** Oversight of executive compensation, stock-plan grants above a threshold, and (increasingly) organisational compensation philosophy. In a public company, the compensation committee is required by SEC and stock-exchange rules; in a private company, the committee is a governance-quality choice and typically forms once the company has more than a small handful of employees with equity grants above the leadership-only cutoff.

**When to form.** Reference triggers:

- **Once ESOP grants exceed leadership-only.** The trigger commonly cited is when equity grants are being made to individual contributors and mid-managers, not just to the CEO's direct reports and the leadership team. The volume of grants makes case-by-case full-board approval impractical, and the standardisation-of-comp discipline benefits from a committee that can set frameworks.
- **Series-B or Series-C for most companies.** The trigger typically aligns with Series-B or early Series-C, when headcount grows past 100-150 and the equity grant workflow has to be systematized.
- **When a compensation policy or benchmark framework is being adopted.** Adoption of a specific benchmarking framework (Pave, Option Impact, Carta compensation data) benefits from committee oversight; the framework becomes the reference for the company's compensation actions.
- **Pre-IPO.** Public-company compensation committees are required and have specific Sarbanes-Oxley and stock-exchange requirements; the pre-IPO committee is set up to that standard 12-18 months before the S-1.

**Charter — the reference structure.** Reference sections:

1. **Purpose.** Oversight of executive compensation, equity-plan administration, compensation philosophy.
2. **Composition.** Number of members (typically three); requirement that members be independent directors (the specific compensation-committee independence definition is closely related to but not identical to the audit-committee definition — see SEC rules under Section 10C of the Exchange Act and the corresponding stock-exchange rules); process for appointment.
3. **Authority and responsibilities.** Approval of the CEO's compensation (base, bonus, equity, other); approval of other executive officers' compensation; approval of equity grants above a threshold (typically to VP-and-above hires); adoption or amendment of the equity plan; adoption of the annual compensation philosophy; oversight of executive-officer severance and change-of-control arrangements; oversight of any related-party compensation matters.
4. **Meetings.** Frequency (typically quarterly, plus ad-hoc for specific hires); requirement to meet in executive session (without CEO present) for CEO comp discussions.
5. **Reporting.** Reporting obligations to the full board.
6. **Committee resources.** Authority to engage compensation consultants (Aon, Compensia, FW Cook, Mercer, or comparable firms) at the company's expense.
7. **Annual review.** Charter review annually.

**Cadence.** Quarterly for the standard cycle, with ad-hoc meetings for specific senior hires or specific comp-related decisions (a promotion package, a severance package, a change-of-control triggered payment). Between-meeting activity: written consents for individual comp actions that meet the committee's approval threshold; written consents for standard equity grants that follow an approved framework.

**Independent-director requirement.** All members must be independent. The independence definition includes an additional prong specific to compensation committees under SEC Rule 10C-1 — consideration of the director's sources of income (specifically, consulting fees from the company or an affiliate) and any affiliation with the company through a specific security-holder relationship. Investor-affiliated directors are typically not considered independent for compensation-committee purposes.

**The CFO's operating relationship with the compensation committee.** The CFO is the committee's primary data provider:

- Presents comp-benchmark data (from Pave, Option Impact, or the equivalent) at each meeting.
- Presents specific compensation proposals for approval, with the underlying rationale.
- Manages the equity-grant workflow between meetings; the committee approves individual grants via written consent or in batch at the meeting.
- Runs the CFO's private meeting with the committee chair on any compensation matter that cannot go through the ordinary flow (e.g., an executive-team performance issue that requires a comp adjustment).

The CFO is *not* in the room for the CEO's compensation discussion — that discussion is a director-only conversation. The lead independent director (or the compensation committee chair, if different) manages that conversation.

## The transaction committee

**Purpose.** A committee formed on an ad-hoc or standing basis to handle detailed negotiation and execution of a specific transaction — an M&A opportunity (acquisition or sale), a specific financing (a Series-C, a venture-debt facility, a bridge), or occasionally a specific strategic partnership.

**When to form.** Two patterns:

- **Ad-hoc for a specific transaction.** When a specific M&A opportunity or a specific financing exceeds a materiality threshold and requires more attention than the full board can give at a quarterly cadence, the board delegates negotiation and execution to a transaction committee — typically two-to-four directors, empowered to negotiate within a specific range and to bring the final proposed transaction back to the full board for approval.
- **Standing at Series-C and later.** Some later-stage boards create a standing transaction committee for ongoing M&A / financing evaluation. Less common than the ad-hoc pattern.

**Charter — the reference structure.** The ad-hoc committee's charter is typically a *resolution* rather than a formal charter — the full board authorises the committee, defines its scope, sets its negotiating parameters, and requires final approval back at the full board level. Reference elements:

1. **Purpose.** The specific transaction the committee is being formed to handle.
2. **Composition.** Named directors (typically the CEO if a director, the lead investor's director, and one independent director; or the audit-committee chair for financing transactions).
3. **Authority.** Authority to negotiate specific terms within a specified range; authority to engage advisors; authority to expend up to a specific budget on transaction expenses.
4. **Limits.** Terms outside the specified range require full-board approval. Definitive-agreement signing typically requires full-board approval.
5. **Meetings.** Frequency (typically weekly or bi-weekly during active negotiation).
6. **Reporting.** Regular update to the full board (typically at each full-board meeting; interim written updates if between meetings).
7. **Sunset.** The committee dissolves upon completion of the transaction or upon determination that no transaction will proceed.

**Cadence.** During active negotiation, the committee meets weekly or more often; between negotiations, it does not meet.

**Independent-director requirement.** For M&A transactions involving related parties (e.g., an acquisition by an existing investor's other portfolio company), the transaction committee should be composed entirely of independent directors (the specific Delaware fiduciary-duty precedent, MFW and its progeny, requires that related-party transactions be approved by a fully-empowered independent-director committee acting on informed and un-coerced basis, or by a fully-informed majority-of-minority stockholder vote, or both, to receive business-judgment-rule protection).

**The CFO's operating relationship with the transaction committee.** The CFO is often the primary execution driver:

- Prepares the financial models, valuation analyses, and diligence materials.
- Coordinates with outside advisors (bankers, lawyers, accountants).
- Attends every transaction committee meeting, presents the numbers, drives the operational work.
- Manages the data room and diligence responses.
- Drafts the definitive-agreement financial and business schedules.

The CFO's role on the transaction committee is more operational and less oversight-focused than on the audit or compensation committees — the transaction committee is essentially a working group with board authority, and the CFO is often the closest thing to a full-time member from management.

## The CFO's coordination with committees

Managing three (or more) committees plus the full board is a coordination job in itself. The CFO's discipline:

- **A single calendar.** The full-board meeting, the audit committee meeting, the compensation committee meeting, any transaction committee meetings — all on one calendar, coordinated to the quarterly cycle where possible. The reference: audit committee the day before the full-board meeting; compensation committee the day before or two days before; transaction committee weekly during active negotiations.
- **A single-source-of-truth pack.** The same actuals, KPI definitions, and model produce every committee's materials. The audit committee's financials are the same financials as the full-board pack; the compensation committee's headcount and equity-grant data are the same as the full-board pack.
- **A cross-committee action-item register.** The register (chapter 4) covers all committee action items, not just full-board items. The CFO keeps the register unified.
- **Sequence of approvals.** Some decisions require sequential committee-then-board approval (e.g., a specific comp package: the compensation committee approves; the full board is notified). Others require parallel review by two committees (e.g., a debt facility that requires audit-committee oversight of the covenants and full-board consent for the transaction). The CFO sequences the approvals.
- **A single point of contact per committee.** Each committee has a chair; the CFO has a defined operating relationship with each chair.
- **Confidentiality management.** Compensation committee discussions (particularly around executive-team performance and CEO comp) are confidential in a way that some other board discussions are not. The CFO's role: know what is committee-confidential, and not surface it in full-board discussions.

## The lead independent director

By Series-B or Series-C, most boards designate a **lead independent director** — a director (not the CEO, not an investor-affiliated director) who serves as the board's coordinator for governance matters. Common responsibilities:

- Chairs the executive session (chapter 3).
- Debriefs the CEO after the executive session.
- Coordinates with the CFO on the full-board agenda.
- Chairs (or coordinates with the chair of) the compensation committee.
- Handles board-composition matters (particularly independent-director sourcing and evaluation).
- Serves as the CEO's board-side counterpart for governance-process matters that do not require full-board discussion.

The lead independent director is a specific role in the governance canon (see Delaware fiduciary-duty precedent and the corporate-governance guidelines published by ISS, Glass Lewis, and CII). Public companies typically require the role (or an equivalent "presiding director" for non-management board sessions); private companies increasingly adopt the practice pre-IPO.

The CFO's operating relationship with the lead independent director is one of the highest-signal relationships in the governance channel — the lead director is often the CFO's first-call escalation on any governance-process concern that cannot be handled through the CEO.

## Common CFO-side failure modes

- **Forming committees too late.** Waiting until the outside audit is already underway to form the audit committee, or until the compensation team has already made hundreds of equity grants to form the compensation committee, produces a backlog that the committee has to work through as its first order of business.
- **Adopting an off-the-shelf charter without review.** The public-company charter templates are a strong starting point but need to be tailored to the company's specific stage and structure.
- **Independent-director shortage.** The company forms an audit committee but does not have three independent directors, so an investor-affiliated director sits on the committee. This works legally in some structures but signals a governance shortcut that a professional investor or auditor will flag.
- **Missing the financial-expert designation.** The audit committee is formed but no member qualifies as a financial expert; the SEC-standard disclosure the pre-IPO company will need is not achievable.
- **The compensation committee approves grants without a comp philosophy.** Individual grants get evaluated in isolation; the pattern that emerges is inconsistent; comp precedent gets set haphazardly.
- **The transaction committee slides into full-board scope.** The full board expects to negotiate every detail; the transaction committee's authority is not respected; the CFO ends up running two parallel processes.
- **The CFO defensively guards the audit committee's access to the auditor.** The auditor's private meeting with the audit committee is a governance mechanism; a CFO who resists it undermines the committee's ability to do its job.
- **Compensation committee discussions leaking into full-board discussions.** CEO comp gets discussed in a full-board setting; the confidentiality convention breaks.
- **Cross-committee coordination failure.** The audit committee approves an audit fee; the compensation committee approves a comp package; both are correct in isolation but inconsistent when the full board sees them together.
- **Not designating a lead independent director.** The executive session lacks a formal chair; the CEO does not have a governance counterpart on the board.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Forms the audit committee at Series-B (or Series-A for regulated sectors), with a charter that is a reasonable pre-IPO-ready starting point.
- Forms the compensation committee once equity grants exceed leadership-only, with a charter and a defined compensation philosophy.
- Forms transaction committees ad-hoc for material M&A and financing transactions, with specific scope, authority, and sunset in the authorising resolution.
- Ensures every committee has the requisite number of independent directors and (for the audit committee) at least one financial-expert designee.
- Runs a single-calendar, single-pack, single-register coordination pattern across all committees and the full board.
- Supports (does not resist) the audit committee's private access to the outside auditor.
- Manages compensation-committee confidentiality; does not leak comp discussions into full-board settings.
- Sequences committee-then-board approvals cleanly; the pack cross-references committee actions.
- Coordinates with the lead independent director on governance-process matters; the lead director is the CFO's first-call escalation.
- Reviews and reaffirms (or amends) each committee's charter annually.

## Summary

- Board committees allow deep work to happen with the right people in the room, without consuming the full board's time. The three the CFO most commonly encounters are audit, compensation, and transaction.
- The audit committee forms at Series-B for pre-IPO track (Series-A for regulated sectors), with a charter modelled on the public-company standard (NYSE §303A.06-07, Nasdaq Rule 5605(c), SEC Regulation S-K Item 407). All members independent; at least one financial expert.
- The compensation committee forms once equity grants exceed leadership-only, typically Series-B or Series-C, with a charter defining its scope over executive compensation, equity-plan grants, and severance. All members independent under the specific comp-committee independence rules (SEC Rule 10C-1).
- The transaction committee forms ad-hoc for a specific M&A or financing, with a resolution defining scope, authority, and sunset. For related-party transactions, all-independent composition supports business-judgment-rule protection under Delaware precedent (MFW and its progeny).
- The CFO's operating relationship with each committee is different: primary operating counterpart to the audit committee, primary data provider to the compensation committee, primary execution driver to the transaction committee.
- Coordination across committees and the full board is itself a discipline: single calendar, single-source-of-truth pack, cross-committee action-item register, sequenced approvals, confidentiality respected.
- The lead independent director (typically formed at Series-B or Series-C) is the CFO's board-side governance-process counterpart.
- Committee formation too late, off-the-shelf charters, independent-director shortage, and cross-committee coordination failure are the most common CFO-side failure modes.
