# Decision Memo Authoring and CEO-CFO Pre-Alignment

## Why this matters

Every board decision that matters — a fundraise, a major hire above a threshold, a capital expenditure, an M&A opportunity, a restructuring — is preceded by (or should be preceded by) a decision memo. The memo is what the board reads before the meeting to form a view. The board's discussion at the meeting is a discussion of the memo, not a first exposure to the decision. The vote that closes the discussion is anchored to the specific option the memo recommended and the specific alternatives the memo considered.

A decision arriving at the board table *without* a memo is a decision the board will either defer, punt, or approve on limited information. Any of those three outcomes is worse for the company than a decision arriving *with* a memo — even if the board disagrees with the recommendation, the disagreement produces a better second-round conversation because the memo has framed the decision space.

The decision memo is one of the highest-leverage documents the CFO writes. This chapter installs the reference structure, the pre-alignment work with the CEO that precedes the memo, and the specific memo shapes for the five most common decision categories.

## The reference structure — context / decision-required / options / recommendation / risks

The reference decision memo has five sections. Every section is deliberate.

**1. Context (1 page).** What is the situation. What has led the company to face this decision now. What has already been done to prepare (analysis, external consultations, prior board discussions). What the specific timing pressure is. The reader should finish this section knowing why the decision is on this specific meeting's agenda and not deferred.

**2. Decision required (0.5 page).** The specific ask of the board. Not "we would like to discuss the fundraise strategy" — "we are seeking board approval to launch a Series-B fundraise with a target close in Q1, targeting $30-40M at $180-220M post-money, subject to the option analysis in Section 4." The decision-required statement is *the specific vote* the board is being asked to take. If the section is vague, the memo is not ready to circulate.

**3. Options (2-4 pages).** The specific alternatives considered, each presented in a comparable frame. Typical shape: three options (sometimes two, occasionally four). For each option, the memo presents: the option itself, the specific pros, the specific cons, the specific financial implication (dilution, cost, revenue impact), the specific risk profile, and the specific execution requirements. The options are directly comparable — same evaluation dimensions for each. The "do nothing" option is typically included as a baseline.

**4. Recommendation (0.5-1 page).** The specific option the memo recommends. The reasoning: why this option beats the alternatives on the evaluation dimensions that matter most. The specific conditions under which the recommendation would change (e.g., "if the KPI trajectory misses by more than 20%, the recommendation would shift to Option 2"). The recommendation is *specific* — not "Option 1 with some elements of Option 2" but "Option 1, as presented, with the following two specific adjustments."

**5. Risks (0.5-1 page).** The top three-to-five risks associated with the recommended option, and the specific mitigation plan for each. Not exhaustive — decision-relevant. A memo without a risks section reads as if the recommender has not thought through the failure modes; a memo with a page of risks reads as if the recommender is hedging.

**Appendix.** Supporting material: financial models, external analyses, benchmark data, term-sheet drafts, third-party proposals. The main memo body is readable without the appendix; the appendix is available for a director who wants to go deeper.

Total memo length: 5-8 pages plus appendix. Longer than 10 pages and the memo won't be read by every director; shorter than 4 and the memo is under-specified on the options.

## The CEO-CFO pre-alignment work

The single most consequential piece of work behind a good decision memo is the CEO-CFO pre-alignment. Before the memo circulates to the board, the CEO and CFO have to be *aligned* on the recommendation. Not necessarily identically-persuaded — sometimes the CFO holds a minority view and the memo reflects both — but they have to be aligned on what the memo *says*, on which option is recommended, and on how the CFO and CEO will each answer the questions that will come up in the meeting.

**Why pre-alignment matters.** A decision memo that surfaces a CEO-CFO disagreement at the board meeting produces one of the worst possible board dynamics. The board is watching the two most senior executives publicly disagree about a material decision, and the board's first instinct is to *defer* the decision until the internal disagreement is resolved. That deferral is a real cost — a lost quarter of momentum, an additional meeting to schedule, the erosion of board confidence in the leadership team's alignment. Pre-alignment prevents the deferral.

**The pre-alignment mechanic.** Two-to-four weeks before the memo circulates, the CFO drafts a **pre-alignment memo** that lays out the CFO's initial read: the decision, the option set, the initial recommendation, the initial risks. The CEO reads the pre-alignment memo. The two of them meet — sometimes once, sometimes multiple times — to work through disagreements. The output is either a shared recommendation or a shared understanding of the specific disagreement.

**When the CEO and CFO disagree.** Sometimes the disagreement is real and cannot be resolved without input from the board. In that case the memo *presents the disagreement* — "the CEO recommends Option 1; the CFO recommends Option 2; here is the specific point of disagreement and the specific board input the two of us are asking for." This is a rare but valid pattern. It works because the disagreement is *framed* rather than surprising, and because the board sees the two senior executives are aligned on the process even if not on the substance.

**When pre-alignment produces a shift.** Sometimes the CEO's read shifts the CFO's recommendation (or vice versa) during the pre-alignment discussion. The memo then reflects the aligned position, and neither the CEO nor the CFO says anything different at the meeting. The pre-alignment discussion is off the record; the memo is the record.

**The role of the lead investor's board director.** In many companies, the CFO also runs a pre-alignment with the lead investor's board director on high-consequence decisions. Not for approval (the director will vote at the meeting) but to surface the director's read early and to adjust the memo if the director's specific concern is not addressed. This is closer to the pre-read call practice (chapter 3) than to the CEO-CFO alignment, but functionally it is preventing surprise at the meeting.

## Memo shape — fundraise decision

The fundraise decision memo (usually preceding a Series-B, Series-C, or later financing) typically covers:

- **Context.** Current runway, current KPI trajectory relative to the plan, the specific milestone the company would raise against, the market conditions read (recent-comps, current-investor-behaviour signal from mod-107).
- **Decision required.** Whether to launch the raise now, in a specific target quarter, or on a specific KPI trigger. Target round size range. Target post-money range. Target investor set (with a specific lead-hunt strategy).
- **Options.** Common option-set: (a) raise now at conservative size and valuation, (b) raise in [X quarters] after hitting [KPI milestone], (c) extend runway via an insider bridge and defer the priced round by [X quarters], (d) hold off on the raise entirely and pursue [operational plan]. Each option carries a specific projected cash-out date, dilution profile, and probability-of-close estimate.
- **Recommendation.** The specific option, with the specific parameters (round size, valuation, timing, investor set).
- **Risks.** Fundraise-specific risks: market may shift, KPIs may miss, specific target investors may pass, dilution may exceed range. Mitigation plans for each.
- **Appendix.** Detailed model, specific target-investor list, timeline / calendar, comparable-company market data, draft management-presentation materials.

The fundraise memo is the most consequential single memo the CFO writes at Series-B and later. It also has the strongest CEO-CFO pre-alignment requirement — a CEO who launches a raise the CFO does not support (or vice versa) generates one of the most predictable execution disasters in the CFO's playbook.

## Memo shape — major hire decision

The major-hire decision memo covers senior hires (typically VP-and-above, and always for C-suite and direct-reports to the CEO) where the board's approval or input is being sought. Some companies delegate all hiring decisions to the CEO and use a compensation-committee approval flow for the comp package only; other companies bring the full hire decision to the board.

- **Context.** The specific role, why it's being hired now, how it fits the org design, what the current interim coverage is, the search process to date, the finalist candidate(s).
- **Decision required.** Board approval of the specific offer package (title, base, equity grant, sign-on, severance, other terms).
- **Options.** Typically: (a) approve the specific candidate at the specific package, (b) approve the candidate at a specific alternative package, (c) defer / re-open the search, (d) reallocate the role's responsibility to existing team without hiring. Each option carries specific implications.
- **Recommendation.** The specific candidate at the specific package.
- **Risks.** Fit risks, comp-precedent risks (this package sets a benchmark for future hires), start-date risks, competitive-offer risks.
- **Appendix.** Candidate resume, back-channel reference summaries (if permitted to share), full compensation analysis, comp benchmark data (Pave, Option Impact, or the specific benchmark used).

Some companies delegate this to the compensation committee entirely once formed (chapter 6). The full-board version is more common at Series-A and Series-B; the committee version is more common at Series-B onward.

## Memo shape — capital expenditure decision

The capex decision memo covers material one-time investments — a large facility lease, a significant hardware or infrastructure buildout, an acquisition of a specific asset (a customer list, a technology license), a large marketing commitment.

- **Context.** The specific investment, the strategic reason for it, the amount, the timing, the payback expectation.
- **Decision required.** Board approval of the specific commitment.
- **Options.** Typically: (a) approve the full ask, (b) approve a scaled-down version, (c) defer, (d) alternative approaches to the same outcome (build vs. buy vs. partner).
- **Recommendation.** The specific option and specific parameters.
- **Risks.** Execution risks, payback risks, opportunity-cost risks.
- **Appendix.** Detailed capex model, projected ROI, comparable-cost benchmarks, vendor / lease / contract drafts.

The capex memo is often shorter than the fundraise memo (a specific commitment is easier to frame than a fundraise strategy). It gets more scrutiny for the payback and opportunity-cost math than for the option set.

## Memo shape — M&A decision

The M&A memo covers a proposed acquisition (inbound target opportunity) or a proposed sale (inbound offer or outbound engagement). It is one of the most complex memo types and often multiple memos in sequence.

- **Context.** The specific opportunity, how it arose, the strategic rationale, the state of the current engagement (LOI, definitive agreement, informal discussion).
- **Decision required.** Depends on the stage. Early: authorisation to enter into a term sheet or letter of intent. Later: authorisation to sign definitive agreements. Latest: authorisation to close.
- **Options.** For an acquisition: (a) proceed at the proposed terms, (b) counter with revised terms, (c) walk. For a sale: same options in reverse. Each option carries specific strategic and financial implications.
- **Recommendation.** The specific option and specific negotiating parameters.
- **Risks.** Integration risks (for acquisition), strategic-alternative-value risks (for sale), diligence risks, regulatory risks (antitrust, tax, employment), fiduciary risks (particularly for related-party transactions).
- **Appendix.** Diligence summary, comparable-transaction data, financial models, term sheet or definitive agreement drafts, third-party advisor materials (banker's book, fairness opinion if applicable).

The M&A memo often triggers formation of a transaction committee (chapter 6) that handles the working-level detail while the full board sees the top-of-house memos.

## Memo shape — restructuring decision

The restructuring memo covers layoffs, org redesigns, product or market exits, or other decisions that reshape the company's cost or scope structure. These are typically difficult memos to write because they combine hard numbers with hard human consequences.

- **Context.** The specific pressure driving the restructuring (financial, strategic, competitive). The specific scope (headcount reduction, product line exit, market exit, all of the above).
- **Decision required.** Board approval of the specific plan.
- **Options.** Typically: (a) the recommended plan at the recommended scope, (b) a smaller-scope alternative, (c) a larger-scope alternative, (d) defer. Each option carries specific financial, operational, and human implications.
- **Recommendation.** The specific plan with specific scope, timing, and severance parameters.
- **Risks.** Execution risks, cultural risks, customer risks (for market or product exits), legal risks (WARN Act, severance liability), reputational risks.
- **Appendix.** Detailed financial model showing pre- and post-restructuring P&L, cash impact, runway impact. Detailed org-chart pre- and post-. Severance-package analysis. Legal-risk summary.

The restructuring memo is one of the most consequential memos the CFO writes and one of the memos most vulnerable to pre-alignment failure — a CFO and CEO who are not fully aligned on the specific scope of a restructuring produce a plan that has to be re-scoped after the board meeting, which erodes both trust and execution speed.

## What the memo is not

- **Not a status update.** The memo is a *decision request*. Status updates go in the pack; decision memos supplement the pack.
- **Not the analysis behind the recommendation.** The analysis is in the appendix. The memo is the recommendation and the framing.
- **Not a place to hedge.** "We recommend Option 1 unless the board prefers a different option" is not a recommendation; it is a punt. The memo takes a position.
- **Not the CEO's speech.** The memo is a written document that stands on its own. The CEO's oral introduction at the meeting is a short framing of the memo, not a re-presentation.
- **Not a marketing document.** The memo is written for a board that has read the pack and knows the company. It skips the introductory context that a marketing document would need.

## The distribution timing

The decision memo is usually distributed *with the board pack* — 72 hours before the meeting. For material decisions where the memo requires more preparation, the memo may go out earlier (5-7 days before the meeting) with an amended pack. For time-critical decisions that arise between meetings, the memo goes out with the interim-meeting invitation.

The memo does not arrive at the meeting for the first time. If a memo is not ready by the pack-send deadline, the decision it covers is deferred or handled by a subsequent-meeting or a written-consent process; it is not walked into an unprepared discussion.

## Post-decision documentation

Once the board decides, the CFO documents:

- **In the minutes.** The specific decision with the vote. The minutes reference the memo by name and appendix number ("the Board approved the recommendation in the [Series-B Fundraise Decision Memo], Appendix A of the [Q3 2026 Board Pack], with the following adjustments: [list]").
- **In the consent (if the decision requires formal consent, per chapter 4).** The specific resolution authorising the specific action.
- **In the action-item register.** The specific execution items assigned to specific owners.
- **In a follow-up memo (occasionally).** If the board's discussion produced material adjustments to the recommended option, the CFO drafts a short "decision-as-adopted" memo that documents the specific final decision as a reference for the execution team.

## Common CFO-side failure modes

- **Vague decision-required section.** The board doesn't know exactly what it's being asked to vote on.
- **Missing pre-alignment with the CEO.** The board sees the two senior executives disagree at the meeting; the decision defers.
- **Options section that is not comparable.** Each option evaluated on different dimensions; the board can't cleanly compare.
- **Do-nothing option omitted.** The baseline is not stated; the board can't calibrate the recommendation.
- **Recommendation hedged.** "Some blend of Option 1 and Option 2" is not a recommendation.
- **Risks section too long or too short.** Too long (a page of risks) reads as hedging; too short (one bulleted risk) reads as under-thought.
- **Memo distributed at the meeting.** The board reads it live; the discussion becomes exploratory rather than decisive.
- **Analysis in the memo body rather than the appendix.** The memo bloats to 15-20 pages; not read.
- **Follow-up memo not filed after adjustments.** The specific decision-as-adopted is lost; the execution team acts on the recommendation, not the adjusted decision.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Drafts a decision memo for every material board decision, using the reference context / decision-required / options / recommendation / risks structure.
- Runs the CEO-CFO pre-alignment discussion before every memo, documented in a short pre-alignment memo the CEO reads before the CFO circulates the board memo.
- Circulates the memo with the board pack, or 5-7 days ahead for material memos.
- Names the specific ask in the decision-required section; the section is unambiguous.
- Presents options comparably, with the do-nothing baseline included.
- Recommends a specific option with specific parameters; does not hedge.
- Documents the specific decision-as-adopted after the meeting, whether in the minutes, in a written consent, or in a follow-up memo.
- Files the memo in the corporate minute book alongside the meeting minutes and the associated consents.
- Runs pre-read calls with the lead investor's director on high-consequence memos in addition to the CEO-CFO pre-alignment.
- Coaches the CEO on the short-framing intro at the meeting rather than allowing the CEO to re-present the memo.

## Summary

- The decision memo is the highest-leverage document behind any material board decision. Every fundraise, major hire, capex commitment, M&A, and restructuring is preceded by (or should be preceded by) a memo.
- The reference structure is context / decision-required / options / recommendation / risks, with a supporting appendix. Memo length is 5-8 pages plus appendix.
- The decision-required section is unambiguous — it names the specific vote the board is being asked to take.
- The options section presents alternatives on comparable dimensions with a do-nothing baseline; the recommendation is specific and does not hedge.
- The CEO-CFO pre-alignment work is the highest-leverage single piece of work behind the memo. A disagreement at the board table without prior framing produces a deferred decision.
- The five most common memo shapes are fundraise, major hire, capex, M&A, and restructuring; each has specific structural conventions.
- The memo is distributed with the board pack (or earlier for high-consequence items); it never arrives at the meeting for the first time.
- Post-decision, the CFO documents the specific decision-as-adopted in the minutes, in the consent, in the action-item register, and (if the discussion produced adjustments) in a short follow-up memo.
