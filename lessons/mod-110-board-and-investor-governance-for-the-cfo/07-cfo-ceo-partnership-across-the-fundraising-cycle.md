# The CFO-CEO Partnership Across the Fundraising Cycle

## Why this matters

Every board and investor governance activity in the previous six chapters rests on one foundation: the CFO and the CEO are aligned. The monthly update reflects the CEO's voice on the wins and losses and the CFO's voice on the numbers, and both together on the ask. The board pack reconciles the CEO's narrative to the CFO's data. The decision memo is pre-aligned between the two before the board reads it. The executive session is honest because both know what the board will hear from the other.

The fundraising cycle is where the CFO-CEO partnership is under the most stress. During a Series-B, the CEO is running an 8-16 week road show while continuing to run the company; the CFO is running the model, the data room, the diligence responses, and the term-sheet negotiation, while continuing to close the books monthly. The two are pulled in different directions at different moments, and — done poorly — the partnership fractures and the raise absorbs the fracture as a discount on terms.

Done well, the partnership is one of the most consequential competitive advantages the company has. A CEO-with-CFO team that can walk into the same investor's Monday partner meeting with the same story, the same numbers, and the same read on the risks closes a raise on materially better terms than a CEO alone with an outsourced controller. This is not marginal — Series-A and later rounds are frequently 10-25% better on either valuation or size when the CFO is a real partner rather than a support function.

This chapter installs the pre-raise, during-raise, and post-raise disciplines of the partnership, and names the specific pattern where CEO-only raises fail.

## Why CEO-only raises cost terms

The most common pattern this chapter is here to correct: the founder-CEO who, at Series-A or Series-B, decides to lead the raise personally with the model in a spreadsheet, no CFO on staff, and outsourced-bookkeeper actuals. The CEO's reasoning is usually some combination of: "the story is my story, I'll tell it best"; "hiring a CFO costs $250K and I need to conserve cash for hiring engineers"; "our accounting is simple enough, I can handle the numbers myself"; "the last raise closed fine without a CFO."

The specific reasons this pattern costs terms:

**The model does not survive institutional diligence.** A CEO-drafted model in a spreadsheet is almost always internally inconsistent by the time an institutional Series-A or Series-B lead's associates work through it. The model that got the CEO to term-sheet doesn't reconcile to the actuals; the driver assumptions don't stress-test; the KPI definitions don't match the pitch deck; the historical financials are not in accrual form. The lead's investment committee sees these failures as either sloppiness (bad) or fabrication (worse). The term sheet that arrives is scoped for a company whose model doesn't fully hold up — smaller round size, lower valuation, tighter protective provisions.

**The data room is disorganised.** Without a CFO, the data room is a scramble in the two weeks between term-sheet and definitive-agreement signing. Missing documents, inconsistent versions, unlabelled files, financials that don't reconcile. The lead's counsel flags the disorganisation to the investment committee, which downgrades the "operational maturity" read. Some terms get tightened in the final documents that would not have been tightened against a well-run data room.

**The CEO cannot both run the raise and run the company.** A raise is 8-16 weeks of full-time work at the CEO level. Doing it alone means the company's operating momentum stalls during the raise; the KPIs the raise is being fundraised against soften just when they most need to hold. Lead investors watch the trajectory during the raise process itself; a softening trajectory produces price pressure.

**The CFO's specific work in the negotiation is not done.** Term-sheet negotiation on the preferred-stock economics (mod-108) — the liquidation preference, the anti-dilution formula, the protective provisions, the pro-rata mechanics — is CFO work. A CEO alone negotiates the headline number (valuation) and often gives up preferred-stock terms that materially affect the founder's ultimate proceeds in an exit. Institutional leads will accept the terms the CEO offers; they will not proactively negotiate against themselves.

**Diligence questions get the wrong answers.** Institutional diligence includes specific questions the CFO would answer differently than the CEO. "What is your fully-diluted cap table under a $200M exit at the current preference stack?" is a waterfall math question (mod-108 chapter 3); a CEO who has never modelled it either guesses wrong or defers to counsel, either of which reads as unpreparedness. "What is your gross retention vs. net retention broken out by cohort?" is a mod-102 question; a CEO without a CFO often does not have the cohort-level breakout.

**The board diligence on the CEO is worse.** Institutional lead diligence includes back-channel reference calls on the founder-CEO's ability to hire and lead an executive team. A founder-CEO who is running a raise without a CFO at Series-A produces a signal: "founder has not yet built a leadership team." The signal is not fatal but it costs.

The corrective: at Series-A onward, the CFO is a real partner in the raise. Fractional CFO if the company cannot afford a full-time hire; contract CFO plus a strong finance director if fractional does not fit; full-time CFO by Series-B at the latest. The specific investment in the CFO's involvement is one of the highest-return decisions the CEO makes in the year before the raise.

## Pre-raise alignment — the 3-6 month window

The 3-6 months before a formal raise opens is the highest-leverage window for CFO-CEO alignment. During this window, the two of them agree on the raise strategy and produce the artifacts that will carry the raise.

**The raise-strategy memo.** A CFO-drafted memo (with CEO input) that names the target round size, the target milestone bar the company will raise against, the target investor set (per mod-107 chapter 2), the target close timing, and the target dilution / valuation range. The memo goes to the board typically 3-6 months ahead of the raise as a decision item (chapter 5). The memo's key content:

- **Target round size and milestone bar.** Not a valuation target; a *milestone target*. "$25-30M at whatever valuation the market bears at $10M ARR and 100% NRR" is a defensible milestone-bar frame; "$25-30M at $150M post-money" is a valuation-target frame that will produce disappointment if the milestone bar isn't hit at the same time.
- **Target investor set.** Named leads, named co-investors, named backup lead options. The pipeline construction discipline from mod-107 chapter 3.
- **Target close timing.** The specific quarter the raise closes and the specific runway-remaining position at that quarter. This anchors the whole plan.
- **Fallback options.** If the raise doesn't close, what happens. Runway extension via cost actions? Insider bridge? The plan-B is documented in the memo (see mod-109 chapter 1).

**The model refresh.** The financial model gets a systematic refresh in the pre-raise window. Historical financials cleaned; KPI definitions locked; forecast rebuilt from the driver-based model with the current-year budget locked and the next 24 months rolling; scenario / sensitivity heatmap ready; cash / runway model complete. The CFO's work; the CEO reviews and challenges.

**The pitch-deck data.** The CEO drives the pitch narrative; the CFO drives the numbers on every slide. Every chart, every metric, every future projection has to be numerically consistent with the model. Version control on the deck is a CFO discipline — one master version, dated, with change log.

**The KPI dashboard as the shared reference.** In the pre-raise window, the KPI dashboard the board sees and the KPI dashboard the CFO uses for the model reconcile with what the pitch deck presents to investors. Same numbers, same definitions, same trajectory.

**The data room architecture.** The data room (mod-107 chapter 5) is built in the pre-raise window, not during the raise itself. Standard sections (corporate documents, financials, cap table, IP, contracts, HR, product) are populated; document naming conventions are enforced; the CFO owns the master.

**The specific alignment discipline.** The CFO and CEO have a standing 45-60 minute weekly meeting in the pre-raise window, on top of the monthly runway meeting. Agenda: model status, deck status, investor-list status, data room status, KPI trajectory, any surprises since last meeting. Outputs: a short weekly memo file that becomes the raise-preparation record.

## During-raise choreography — the 8-16 week active raise

The active raise begins when the CEO takes the first partner meeting. During the raise, the CFO-CEO choreography is intense and specific.

**Who takes which meeting.** Reference division:

- **First partner meeting: CEO alone (usually).** The initial pitch to a lead's investment partner is typically the CEO's meeting, occasionally with the CFO for specific investor types (fintech / financial-services-focused investors, later-stage / IPO-track-focused investors). The CFO joins the second meeting.
- **Second meeting: CEO + CFO together.** The follow-up meeting is typically joint — the CEO extends the story, the CFO takes the numerical deep-dive.
- **Diligence meetings: CFO leads, CEO joins for strategic topics.** The financial diligence sessions, the model walkthrough, the KPI reconciliation session are CFO-led with the CEO on hand for the strategic-context questions.
- **Reference calls: CEO and CFO both, in parallel.** The lead's reference-call process typically covers 5-10 references (customers, hires, prior investors); the CEO covers the customer and hire references, the CFO covers the prior-investor and finance-partner references.
- **Term sheet negotiation: CFO leads, CEO joins for the final valuation and non-financial-terms discussion.** The CFO handles the mechanic (per mod-108); the CEO handles the pricing and the qualitative-terms.
- **Investment-committee presentation: CEO leads, CFO joins.** The final IC presentation is typically the CEO's — the story, the vision, the team. The CFO is in the room for the numbers questions.

**Weekly investor-list tracking.** The CFO or CEO's chief of staff maintains the weekly investor-list dashboard (from mod-107 chapter 3) — every investor, current stage, next step, blocking issue, target close date. Both the CFO and CEO see the dashboard; the weekly Monday sync starts with it.

**Shift to weekly investor updates.** Per chapter 1, the monthly investor update shifts to weekly during the active raise, covering the raise-process status for the *existing* investor base. The CFO drafts; the CEO reviews and sends.

**KPI cadence tightens.** During the raise, the CFO refreshes the KPI dashboard weekly (not monthly). The lead investors will ask; the up-to-date dashboard is at hand.

**Standing daily 15-minute check.** Some CFOs and CEOs hold a standing 15-minute daily huddle during the active-raise weeks. Purpose: coordinate the day's meetings, flag any issue that came up in a partner meeting, adjust the deck or model if a specific question or objection has surfaced.

**Message discipline.** The CEO and CFO tell the same story. Not identical language — each has a personal voice — but the same specific answer to the same specific question. The pre-raise weekly meeting is where message discipline gets set; the daily huddle is where it gets refreshed.

**Handling different investor asks.** Different investors will ask for different variations of the same information — one wants the model in one format, another wants it in a different format, a third wants a specific breakout. The CFO's discipline is to produce every requested variation *from the same underlying model* — never a bespoke rebuild for a specific investor, always a re-presentation of the same underlying numbers.

## Post-raise operating alignment — the 4-8 week post-close window

The raise closes. The wire lands. The CEO takes a few days off. The CFO restarts the standing operating rhythm.

**The plan reset.** Within 4-8 weeks of close, the CFO drafts a **plan-reset memo** that updates the operating model for the closed capitalisation, the closed runway position, and the specific milestone bar the company will now execute against (typically the milestones for the *next* raise). The plan-reset memo goes to the board at the first post-close board meeting.

**The board-pack refresh.** The KPI dashboard, financials, and forecast in the next board pack reflect the post-close reality. Any specific commitments made to the new lead during the raise (specific hires, specific product ships, specific market moves) get called out as commitments and tracked in the action-item register (chapter 4).

**The new lead's board director.** The new lead's partner joins the board at the first post-close meeting. The CFO's onboarding for the new director: a 60-90 minute individual session covering the model, the KPI dashboard, the historical board packs, and the specific dynamics of the CEO-CFO relationship. This session pays back in every subsequent meeting.

**The updated capital plan.** Per mod-109 chapter 1, the capital plan memo updates for the closed round — new cash-out projection, new trigger points, new plan-B / plan-C options at the updated stage.

**The new comp benchmarking.** Post-raise, executive compensation and grant-level equity often need to be adjusted (a promotion round for the team that carried the raise, adjusted comp benchmarks for the new stage). The compensation committee (chapter 6) drives this if formed; the full board handles it otherwise. The CFO drives the benchmark analysis.

**Recovery.** The CEO and CFO are tired after a raise. The recovery is real — 2-3 weeks of lower-intensity work after close is appropriate. Immediately restarting at raise-intensity produces burnout that damages the following quarters.

## The specific pattern for a first CFO hire

The CEO-only raise pattern is often broken by hiring a first CFO in the 6-12 months before a Series-A or Series-B raise. The reference pattern:

**Fractional CFO first.** For a Series-A company that is not yet at scale for a full-time CFO, a fractional CFO (Kruze, Burkland, Aprio, or an independent fractional CFO) covers 60-80% of the CFO scope at 20-30% of the cost. The fractional CFO delivers: month-end close, financial model, board-pack authoring, cap-table management, and support in the fundraise.

**Full-time CFO by Series-B.** By the Series-B raise, most companies want a full-time CFO. The specific reasons: the deeper diligence at Series-B, the audit-committee formation requirement, the more complex cap-table and preferred-stock mechanics, the more elaborate board-governance channel, the higher stakes on the term-sheet negotiation.

**The CFO hire itself is a board-approved decision** (per chapter 5 major-hire memo). The CFO comes in with a specific mandate from the CEO and the board; the mandate should include ownership of the standing communication channel (updates + board packs) and full ownership of the model and cap table.

**The CFO's first 90 days.** Rapidly build a working operating model, close the current-month books cleanly, refresh the KPI dashboard, ship the next monthly update, prepare the next board pack. The first-90-day artifacts are the CFO's proof-of-work to the CEO and the board.

**The CEO's first 90 days with the new CFO.** Grant the CFO the operating authority the mandate specifies. Do not micromanage the model. Do not run parallel numbers in the CEO's own spreadsheet. Do not surprise the CFO in a board meeting with a KPI number the CEO computed differently. The CEO's discipline in the first 90 days sets the partnership pattern for the multi-year relationship.

## Specific failure modes in the partnership

- **The CEO carries a "shadow model" in a personal spreadsheet.** The CFO's model and the CEO's model diverge; each uses different numbers with the board. Trust erodes.
- **The CFO gets excluded from partner meetings during the raise.** The CEO takes every meeting alone; the CFO is used only for post-hoc diligence responses. The CEO's story and the model don't reconcile because the CFO didn't shape the story.
- **The CEO commits to term-sheet numbers without CFO input.** A liquidation-preference structure or an anti-dilution formula gets committed at the term-sheet handshake, and the CFO finds out reading the term sheet after signing. Recovery is often impossible.
- **The CFO negotiates against the CEO in front of the board.** A CFO who publicly disagrees with the CEO's specific decision at the board meeting without prior alignment produces the deferred-decision pattern (chapter 5) and erodes both trust and board confidence.
- **The CFO takes a bad-cop role permanently.** The CFO becomes "the one who says no"; the CEO becomes "the one who says yes." The board reads the partnership as unbalanced; the operating team reads the CFO as an obstacle rather than a partner.
- **The pre-raise weekly meeting drifts.** The two miss weeks; alignment slips; the raise opens without the model and deck being in sync.
- **The post-raise plan reset doesn't happen.** The old plan is executed against the new capitalisation; the trigger points from mod-109 chapter 1 are wrong; the next raise opens against a stale plan.
- **The CEO withholds difficult information from the CFO.** A senior-departure conversation, a customer loss, a legal issue — anything the CEO doesn't share with the CFO in real time is something the CFO will surface with the board later, which erodes the CEO's control of the narrative.
- **The CFO manages upward through the board rather than through the CEO.** The CFO who cultivates the lead investor's director as a channel around the CEO is a CFO whose partnership with the CEO will eventually fracture publicly. Escalation through the board is a specific tool for genuine governance failures, not a shortcut to workaround an inconvenient CEO.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Runs a standing weekly meeting with the CEO in every operating quarter, tightened to weekly-plus-daily during an active raise.
- Owns the model, the KPI dashboard, and the cap table; the CEO does not maintain a shadow model.
- Attends the second partner meeting and every subsequent diligence meeting during a raise.
- Leads term-sheet negotiation with the CEO on hand for pricing and non-financial-terms discussion.
- Drafts the raise-strategy memo 3-6 months ahead of the raise; presents it to the board as a decision item.
- Drafts the plan-reset memo within 4-8 weeks of close; presents it to the board at the first post-close meeting.
- Onboards new board directors post-raise in a dedicated individual session.
- Manages message discipline with the CEO during the active raise; both tell the same specific story to the same specific question.
- Escalates specific disagreements with the CEO to the CEO privately in the pre-alignment discussion, not to the board publicly at the meeting.
- Recognises the difference between a strong partnership and a "yes-CFO" pattern; brings specific dissents to the CEO honestly.
- Recovers 2-3 weeks post-close; does not restart at raise-intensity.
- Coaches the CEO out of the "shadow model" pattern; produces a shared reference the CEO trusts enough to use as the sole source.

## Summary

- The CFO-CEO partnership is the foundation of every governance and investor-communication artifact in this module. Aligned partners produce aligned artifacts; misaligned partners produce artifacts that surface the misalignment.
- A CEO-only raise at Series-A onward frequently costs terms because the model does not survive institutional diligence, the data room is disorganised, the CEO cannot simultaneously run the raise and the company, and the term-sheet negotiation on preferred-stock economics is CFO work that does not happen.
- The pre-raise window (3-6 months ahead of the raise) is when the raise-strategy memo, the model refresh, the pitch-deck data, the KPI dashboard reconciliation, and the data room architecture get built. Standing weekly CFO-CEO meeting.
- The during-raise choreography splits meeting attendance by type: CEO alone for first partner meetings, joint for second meetings and IC presentations, CFO-led for financial diligence and term-sheet negotiation. Message discipline is set weekly, refreshed daily.
- The post-raise plan-reset memo lands within 4-8 weeks of close, resetting the operating model against the closed capitalisation and the next raise's milestone bar. New board directors onboarded individually.
- The first-CFO hire pattern is fractional at Series-A, full-time by Series-B. The CEO's discipline in the first 90 days with the new CFO sets the multi-year partnership pattern.
- The most damaging failure modes are the shadow-model pattern, the CEO-alone raise, the public CFO-CEO disagreement at the board meeting, and the CFO who manages upward through the board around the CEO.

Chapter 8 turns to the specific hard cases the partnership handles: a miss to plan, a departure of a senior hire, a customer concentration risk, a founder disagreement — and the honest-signal frame that distinguishes a CFO the board trusts from one the board manages.
