# Board Consents, Action Items, and the CFO-Owned Register

## Why this matters

The board meeting produces decisions. The decisions have to be *recorded*, *authorised*, and *followed through*. The mechanic for recording is the meeting minutes (chapter 3). The mechanic for authorising is the **board consent** — a document with legal force that captures a board decision either in a meeting or between meetings by written signature. The mechanic for following through is the **action-item register** — the running list of open items the CFO maintains between meetings.

None of these three artifacts is glamorous. All of them are load-bearing. A board decision that lives only in the CEO's head, without a written consent authorising it, is a decision the corporation cannot rely on in a legal or regulatory context. An action item that "everyone agreed to" but that no one owns and no one is tracking is an action item that will surface at the next board meeting as an unresolved risk. A CFO who does not own these three mechanics ends up with a board that does not trust the corporation's own record of its decisions — which is a slow but total governance failure.

This chapter installs the mechanics: how consent works legally, when it applies, how to draft it, how to run the running register, and how to keep the whole system honest against the actual corporate-formality requirements of Delaware (and the equivalent in other common states of incorporation).

## The legal frame — what "board consent" means

Under Delaware General Corporation Law §141(f), a board of directors may take an action either by holding a meeting and voting at the meeting, or by unanimous written consent in lieu of a meeting. The written-consent-in-lieu-of-a-meeting mechanism is the specific legal tool most startup boards use for the vast majority of their consent activity. The full text of §141(f) is available at the Delaware Code site (see resources.md); the key points:

- **Unanimous.** Every director entitled to vote on the matter must sign the consent. If even one director does not sign, the consent fails as a §141(f) action, and the matter must be taken to a meeting.
- **Written.** The consent is a written document. Electronic signature (DocuSign, HelloSign, and similar) is accepted under DGCL amendments; the practical convention is to circulate a PDF with signature blocks and collect electronic signatures.
- **Filed with the minutes.** The signed consent is retained in the corporate minute book alongside the meeting minutes. It has the same legal weight as a decision voted on at a meeting.
- **Effective when the last director signs, unless otherwise stated.** The consent can specify an effective date; absent that, the effective date is when the last signature comes in.

Under DGCL §228, stockholders may similarly take action by written consent — a distinct mechanic often used for stockholder-level approvals (charter amendments, stock plan approvals, some financing consents), and one the CFO increasingly manages as the cap table complexifies. Most companies incorporated in Delaware default to allowing action by written consent at both board and stockholder levels; the certificate of incorporation may modify this default (some post-IPO companies eliminate the stockholder-written-consent option, which is why some public-company charters differ from private-company charters on this point).

Non-Delaware companies (usually incorporated in the state of the founders' residence or of an operating headquarters) operate under the equivalent statute of that state. The mechanics are broadly similar; the CFO should confirm the state-specific mechanic with counsel and cite the specific statute in the consent's preamble.

**None of the above is legal advice.** The CFO's role is to run the consent process cleanly and to escalate to counsel any consent whose specific legal status is ambiguous.

## What actions require board consent

Not every board discussion produces a consent. The reference categorisation:

**Actions that legally require board consent (formal resolution).** These vary by state and by the corporation's charter, but the reference list for a Delaware C-corporation includes:

- **Issuance of new equity** (stock issuances to employees, advisors, investors; convertible securities like SAFEs and notes; any authorised stock plan grants). Board resolution required.
- **409A valuation refresh** (adoption of a new common-stock fair-market-value for option-strike-price purposes). Formal board approval required; usually done concurrently with the 409A refresh.
- **Compensation actions** (executive compensation, severance packages, promotions or new hires above a specified threshold). Often delegated to the compensation committee once formed (chapter 6); requires committee or board approval.
- **Adoption of the annual budget and business plan.** Board approval required at the Q1 (or Q4-of-prior-year) meeting.
- **Adoption or material amendment of the stock plan.** Board (and often stockholder) approval.
- **Authorisation of debt facilities** (venture debt, lines of credit, convertible notes). Board approval required; specific terms often require committee approval.
- **Material contracts above a threshold specified in the charter or bylaws.** Common thresholds: leases above a specified dollar amount, contracts with terms longer than a specified duration.
- **M&A activity** (any letter of intent, definitive agreement, or transaction that would result in change of control). Board approval required at multiple points.
- **Bankruptcy, assignment for the benefit of creditors, or dissolution.** Board approval required.
- **Amendments to the certificate of incorporation, bylaws, or investor-rights documents.** Board (and stockholder) approval typically required.
- **Related-party transactions.** Actions involving directors, officers, or major stockholders in a capacity beyond their director / officer / stockholder role. Specific process (independent-director approval, or stockholder approval, or both) required per the charter and per Delaware fiduciary duty precedent.
- **Officer appointments and removals.** Formal action to elect or remove the CEO, CFO, COO, and other officer positions defined in the bylaws.

**Actions that require committee approval (in lieu of, or in advance of, the full board).** Once committees are formed:

- **Audit committee.** Selection and engagement of the outside auditor; approval of audit-services scope and fees; approval of non-audit services above a threshold; review of quarterly and annual financial statements; approval of specific accounting-policy elections.
- **Compensation committee.** All executive-officer compensation actions; all stock-plan grants above a threshold (often, grants to VP-and-above); annual comp-benchmark review.
- **Transaction committee.** Ad-hoc, for a specific M&A or financing transaction where the full board delegates a working group to handle detailed negotiation.

**Actions that do not require formal consent but appear on the pack.** Informational updates to the board — customer wins, product ships, hiring updates, financial reporting between formal approvals — are on the pack but do not require a vote. The pack labels these as "informational."

The CFO's job: for every action on the pack, know whether it requires consent, and by whom (full board, committee, board plus stockholders). A pack that requests a consent action without knowing which body needs to approve it is a pack that will produce the wrong authorisation.

## The two consent modes — at-the-meeting vote and between-meetings written consent

**At-the-meeting vote.** The most common mode for the ordinary quarterly consent items. The pack's risks / decisions / consents section names the specific resolutions. At the meeting, the chair puts the resolution to a vote. The minutes record the vote (typically "unanimously approved" or the specific vote count if not unanimous). The minutes carry the legal force of the consent.

**Between-meetings written consent.** Used for items that arise between meetings and cannot wait for the next quarterly meeting. Common examples: an option grant for a new hire whose start date is between meetings; a routine 409A refresh; authorisation of a specific contract or lease; a bridge financing that closes between meetings. The CFO drafts the consent as a written resolution, circulates for electronic signature, and files the signed consent in the minute book. If the consent is not unanimous (one director objects or does not sign), the matter cannot be closed by written consent and must go to a meeting.

**Ratification.** Occasionally an action is taken (usually inadvertently) before the required consent is obtained. The subsequent board can *ratify* the action retroactively via a consent that references the specific prior action. Ratification is legally effective but is a signal of process breakdown; the CFO should treat frequent ratifications as an indicator the consent process is not being run tightly.

## Drafting a consent

A written consent is a short, structured document. The reference format:

```
UNANIMOUS WRITTEN CONSENT OF THE BOARD OF DIRECTORS
OF [COMPANY LEGAL NAME]

In lieu of a meeting, the undersigned, being all of the members of
the Board of Directors of [COMPANY LEGAL NAME], a [STATE] corporation
(the "Company"), acting pursuant to Section 141(f) of the General
Corporation Law of the State of Delaware, do hereby consent to,
approve, and adopt the following resolutions with the same force
and effect as if such resolutions had been adopted at a duly
convened meeting of the Board of Directors:

WHEREAS, [context / recital];

WHEREAS, [additional context if needed];

NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT RESOLVED, that [the specific action being
authorised, stated with specific parties, amounts, dates];

FURTHER RESOLVED, that [any officer authorisation to execute
specific documents];

FURTHER RESOLVED, that [any additional related actions];

FURTHER RESOLVED, that any officer of the Company is hereby
authorised and directed to take any and all such actions and to
execute and deliver any and all such documents as such officer may
deem necessary or appropriate to effectuate the purposes and
intent of the foregoing resolutions.

This Consent may be executed in counterparts and by electronic
signature, each of which shall be deemed an original, but all of
which taken together shall constitute one and the same instrument.

IN WITNESS WHEREOF, the undersigned have executed this Consent as
of the date set forth beside their signatures below.

_____________________________     Date: ____________
[DIRECTOR NAME]

_____________________________     Date: ____________
[DIRECTOR NAME]

[etc., one signature block per director]
```

Key drafting points:

- **The recitals name the context.** The "whereas" clauses are the plain-English backstory: what the board is being asked to approve, why now, what the underlying transaction or event is. A consent without recitals is a consent that a reader three years later cannot understand.
- **The resolutions are specific.** Not "the Company is authorised to enter into a debt facility" but "the Company is authorised to enter into that certain Loan and Security Agreement dated [date] with [Lender] in a principal amount of up to $[amount], on the terms set forth in the term sheet attached hereto as Exhibit A." Specificity means a future reader (auditor, litigant, acquirer) can determine exactly what was approved.
- **Attached exhibits.** Any specific document being approved (a term sheet, an offer letter, a lease, a definitive agreement) is attached as an exhibit and referenced in the resolution.
- **Officer authorisation.** The final "any officer is authorised" clause gives officers the authority to execute the necessary follow-up documents without requiring another board consent for each step.
- **The signature-block convention.** Every director's name is printed; the signature line and date line are alongside. Electronic signature captures the same information. The signed consent is retained; unsigned pages are not retained (or are retained as evidence of the incomplete consent, if that becomes relevant).

The template can be adapted for each specific action. Company counsel typically owns a library of standard resolutions for common actions (option grants, 409A refresh, standard financing consents); the CFO's job is to know the library exists and to use it rather than drafting from scratch every time.

## Ordinary vs. non-ordinary consents

The pack calls out which consents are ordinary and which are not.

**Ordinary consents.** The recurring quarterly items that require formal action but that are not decisions in the substantive sense — approval of the prior meeting's minutes, approval of the 409A refresh (if quarterly), approval of specific option grants that have been through the compensation committee, adoption of any standard employee-agreement forms. These are typically voted en bloc at the meeting (one vote covers the ordinary-consent block).

**Non-ordinary consents.** Consents for material decisions — a debt facility authorisation, an M&A letter-of-intent authorisation, a fundraising authorisation, a compensation package for a new executive, a specific budget adoption or amendment. Each non-ordinary consent gets its own vote. Each is preceded by a decision memo or a specific pack section that gives the board the substance to vote on.

The ordinary / non-ordinary distinction is a signal of the meeting's discipline. A meeting with a dozen "non-ordinary" consents is a meeting where too many decisions are being made without adequate pre-work. A meeting with zero non-ordinary consents is either a quiet quarter or a meeting where meaningful decisions are being deferred.

## The action-item register — what it is and who owns it

The action-item register is the running document that tracks every commitment made at a board meeting or an interim board conversation.

**Format.** A simple table: item ID, meeting date item was created, description, owner, target close date, status (open / in-progress / closed / deferred), notes, closed date.

**Owner.** The CFO. Some companies split this role with a chief-of-staff or a corporate-secretary function; the CFO retains the responsibility for the register even when execution is delegated.

**Sources of items.** Board meetings (the specific commitments made in the meeting, whether by management to the board or by a director to another director), interim board communications (a director asks the CFO a question via email; the CFO's response is an action item), the CFO's own commitments made in the pack (a promised follow-up), and any decision item that was deferred to a specific future date with a named process.

**Lifecycle.** An item enters the register with a target close date. As the date approaches, the CFO checks in with the owner. Closed items are marked closed with the specific outcome (a link to the deliverable, a summary of the resolution). Deferred items are re-dated with the new target and a note explaining the deferral.

**Reporting.** The register is included in every board pack (typically as an appendix, sometimes as a section). Items closed since the last meeting are called out; items with target dates that have slipped are called out; items opened since the last meeting are called out. The board's read on the register is a signal of the CFO's operating discipline.

**Aging.** An item that has been open for two consecutive meetings without progress is a signal of a stuck decision or a broken commitment. The CFO's job: escalate to the item's owner (or to the CEO) and either drive the item to resolution or explicitly close it as "no longer pursued."

**Confidential vs. public register.** Some registers include items that should not be visible to the full board or to the observer set — for example, a board conversation about a specific management-team member that produced a follow-up commitment. These items live in a confidential register maintained by the lead independent director or the compensation committee chair, not in the standard register. The CFO's job is to know which mode a given item belongs in.

## The interaction between consents and the register

A written consent is an action item once the consent is drafted; it closes when the last signature is received. The register tracks the drafting-and-collection cycle:

- Item opens: "Draft written consent for [X] and circulate for signature. Target signature-collection close: [date]."
- Item in-progress: consent drafted, exhibits attached, circulated for signature.
- Item closed: last signature received; signed consent filed in the minute book; the corresponding corporate action (the option grants execute, the lease is signed, the debt facility is drawn) proceeds.

A written consent that sits at partial signature for weeks is an operational failure of the register discipline, not a legal problem per se, but often signals that one director has an issue with the consent (which needs to be surfaced) or that the CFO is not chasing the signatures. Either way, the register makes the delay visible.

## The corporate minute book

The minute book is the physical (or, more commonly, digital) repository of all corporate governance records. Contents:

- Meeting minutes for every board meeting.
- Signed written consents for every action taken by written consent.
- Committee meeting minutes and written consents.
- Stockholder-action records (written consents and meeting minutes).
- The certificate of incorporation and all amendments.
- The bylaws and all amendments.
- The stock-plan documents and any amendments.
- Investor-rights documents from each financing.
- 409A valuation reports.
- Cap-table snapshots at each meeting.

The minute book is maintained by the CFO or by outside counsel (for most Series-A/B companies, outside counsel — typically the same firm that handles corporate matters generally — maintains a digital minute book, and the CFO ensures the firm receives every signed consent and every set of meeting minutes for filing).

The minute book gets audited during any material transaction: the diligence for a fundraise, a debt facility, an M&A transaction, or an IPO. A minute book with gaps (missing consents for actions that were taken, minutes not signed and approved, ratifications never processed) is a diligence red flag and produces cleanup work at the worst possible time. The steady discipline of keeping the minute book clean at each meeting is worth an order of magnitude more than the emergency cleanup during diligence.

## Common CFO-side failure modes

- **Taking a board action without a consent.** The board discusses a decision, "agrees" it, and management proceeds — but no written consent is drafted or signed. Six months later during fundraise diligence, the acquirer's counsel asks "where is the consent for [action]," and the answer is a scramble to ratify.
- **Consent language that is too vague.** "The Company is authorised to enter into a debt facility on customary terms" is not specific enough for an auditor or a diligence lawyer. Specificity is the discipline.
- **Missing exhibits.** A consent authorising a specific term sheet or agreement without attaching the actual term sheet or agreement is a consent that cannot be evaluated by a future reader.
- **The action-item register that lives in the CFO's head.** Untracked items become surprises.
- **Chronic-open items.** Items that stay open for three consecutive meetings without progress signal a stuck decision or a broken commitment; either resolve them or explicitly close them as no-longer-pursued.
- **The minute book with gaps.** Undiagnosed until a material transaction, at which point it becomes a diligence problem.
- **Not distinguishing ordinary from non-ordinary consents.** Everything voted individually creates a laborious meeting; everything voted en bloc creates ambiguity about which specific approvals were given.
- **Delegating consent tracking to outside counsel entirely.** Outside counsel maintains the minute book but does not know what the board actually approved unless the CFO tells them. The CFO's role is the primary source of truth; outside counsel is the repository.
- **Frequent ratifications.** Ratifying prior actions retroactively works legally but signals a broken consent process; investigate and fix the root cause.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Knows which board and committee approvals every material corporate action requires, and drafts (or engages counsel to draft) the specific consent for each.
- Uses a standard resolution library for recurring actions (option grants, 409A refresh, minutes approval) and drafts bespoke resolutions for material actions (financings, M&A).
- Ships every consent with the underlying decision memo or reference document as an exhibit.
- Distinguishes ordinary from non-ordinary consents in the pack and at the meeting.
- Maintains a running action-item register with owner, target date, status, and notes; includes the register in every board pack.
- Chases signature collection on written consents; a consent open for more than 5-10 business days at partial signature is a signal to escalate.
- Ensures the minute book is up to date after every meeting and every between-meetings consent, coordinating with outside counsel as the filing repository.
- Escalates the aging or repeatedly-stuck action item to the CEO or the specific owner; explicitly closes items no longer being pursued.
- Coordinates with the committee chairs (audit, comp) on the register items that live in the committees' scope rather than the full-board scope.
- Treats a frequent ratification pattern as a symptom of process breakdown and fixes the process rather than normalising the ratification.

## Summary

- Board consent is the legal mechanism (DGCL §141(f) for Delaware corporations) by which the board authorises corporate actions, either at a meeting or between meetings by unanimous written consent. Stockholder consent (DGCL §228) is the analogous mechanism at the stockholder level.
- Not every board discussion produces a consent. The CFO knows which specific actions require formal consent (equity issuances, 409A refreshes, compensation, budgets, debt facilities, M&A, charter amendments, related-party transactions, officer appointments) and by whom (full board, committee, stockholders).
- Written consents are drafted in a structured format with recitals, specific resolutions, attached exhibits for the underlying documents, and officer-authorisation clauses. Specificity is the discipline; vagueness creates diligence problems downstream.
- The ordinary / non-ordinary distinction is a discipline signal. Ordinary consents are voted en bloc; non-ordinary consents get individual votes preceded by decision memos.
- The action-item register is the CFO-owned running document that tracks every commitment made at or between board meetings. It appears in every pack. Aged and stuck items get escalated.
- The corporate minute book is the repository of all governance records. Kept clean at each meeting, it produces no cleanup work during transactions; kept sloppy, it becomes an urgent diligence problem at the worst moment.
- Frequent ratifications are a symptom of a broken consent process; investigate the root cause.
- The CFO retains ownership of the whole system even when execution is delegated to a chief-of-staff, corporate secretary, or outside counsel.
