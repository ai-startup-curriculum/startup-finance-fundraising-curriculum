# Board Composition and Independent-Director Selection

## Why this matters

Board composition is the single most consequential governance term in a term sheet. Every other governance term (protective provisions, drag-along, voting agreement) modulates or constrains the board's authority; the board itself is where the day-to-day decisions of the company live. Who sits on the board — how many founder-elected directors, how many investor-elected directors, how many independent directors, and by what mechanic each is selected — defines the company's governance shape across the years between rounds when nothing else in the corporate charter changes.

A CFO reading a Series-A term sheet has to understand:

- **What the seed board looked like** before this round (typically founder-heavy or founder-only) and what will change at the Series-A close.
- **What the market-standard Series-A board is** in the current market (typically five seats: two founder, one Series-A investor, two independents) and where the received term sheet deviates.
- **How independent-director selection works mechanically** — who nominates, who approves, how a director is removed, how a vacancy is filled — because "independent" without a nomination-and-removal mechanic is a term with no operative meaning.
- **How the board evolves through Series-B, Series-C, and beyond** — the founder seats tend to compress, the investor seats accumulate, the independent seats grow — and how term sheets at each round negotiate the transition.
- **How the composition interacts with the Voting Agreement** — the actual binding-in-practice mechanic by which the founder, the preferred, and the common commit to vote for the specific slate.

This chapter walks the pattern from seed through Series-B, the specific mechanics of independent-director selection, and the drafting-and-negotiation posture the CFO takes at each round.

## The seed board

At the seed stage, most companies have either no board (a founder-only director) or a small board (one or two founders plus one seed investor). The specific patterns:

- **Founder-only board (one director).** Common at pre-seed and immediately post-seed for SAFE-funded companies with no priced round. The founder-CEO is the sole director; corporate actions require board consent from the CEO alone.
- **Two-director board (two founders).** Common when there are two co-founders and both want a board seat.
- **Three-director board (two founders + one seed investor).** Common after a priced seed round with a lead. The seed lead gets one seat; the founders keep two.
- **Three-director board (one founder + one investor + one independent).** Less common at seed but seen when the seed round includes an experienced institutional lead who insists on an independent director.

The seed board is a low-formality body. Board meetings are often quarterly, sometimes coordinated with the seed lead's board-observation schedule. Board consents (rather than formal meetings) handle most decisions.

The Series-A term sheet will reshape this board substantially. The CFO's task at Series-A is to negotiate the reshape.

## The current-market Series-A board

The modal current-market Series-A board is five directors:

- **Two founder directors** — typically the CEO and a designated co-founder or COO. Elected by the common stockholders (per the Voting Agreement).
- **One Series-A director** — elected by the Series-A preferred stockholders. The Series-A lead's partner typically takes this seat.
- **Two independent directors** — elected by the common and Series-A voting together (or by a specific joint mechanic under the Voting Agreement). "Independent" means not affiliated with the company, the founders, or the preferred holders.

The "2 founder + 1 investor + 2 independent" pattern has become the reference at Series-A over the last decade. Some variations exist:

- **Three-director board (one founder + one investor + one independent).** Some smaller Series-A financings (particularly at the lower end of the cheque-size range) run a three-director board.
- **Four-director board (two founder + one investor + one independent).** A common pattern when only one independent has been sourced at the time of Series-A close, with a second independent added later.
- **Five-director board with a swing independent.** A specific pattern where the fifth board seat is filled by "an independent director mutually acceptable to the founders and the Series-A lead." This puts the swing-vote power in a person who requires joint agreement to seat.
- **Six or seven directors.** Larger boards are unusual at Series-A but appear in some later-stage-anchor Series-A rounds or when a strategic co-investor adds a seat.

The two-founder-plus-two-independent framing preserves a founder-favourable board dynamic at Series-A: founders and independents together (four of five seats) can pass most actions, and the investor director's role is constructive rather than controlling. This is the market-standard the CFO should negotiate against.

## The Series-B and later evolution

At Series-B, the board changes again. The specific patterns:

- **Series-B seat is added.** The Series-B lead typically gets a director seat, either as a new seat (expanding the board from five to six/seven) or as a replacement of one of the existing seats (typically an independent or one of the founder seats, though the latter is more contentious).
- **Founder seats sometimes compress.** By Series-B, a common pattern is to move from two founder seats to one (the CEO). The second founder (if there was one) may keep a seat as an "observer" or step off the board entirely.
- **Independents remain or grow.** The number of independents typically stays constant or grows. Some Series-B financings add a second or third independent director as the board grows.
- **Observer seats emerge.** Non-voting observer seats become common, given to strategic investors, secondary Series-A holders, or in some cases to holders below the "Major Investor" threshold. Observers attend meetings but don't vote.

Typical Series-B board patterns:

- **Six directors: 1 founder + 1 Series-A + 1 Series-B + 3 independents.** Founder-favourable Series-B pattern; the founder-plus-independents block has a majority.
- **Six directors: 2 founder + 1 Series-A + 1 Series-B + 2 independents.** Preserves both founders on the board; adds the Series-B seat by expansion.
- **Seven directors: 1 founder + 1 Series-A + 1 Series-B + 4 independents.** An expanded board that dilutes founder control but adds governance depth.
- **Five directors: 1 founder + 1 Series-A + 1 Series-B + 2 independents.** A compact board that reduces the founder to one seat. Investor-friendly Series-B pattern.

At Series-C and beyond, the pattern typically continues: additional investor seat (or seat re-allocation), independent seat additions, occasional founder-seat compression, and additional observer seats. The board approaches its late-stage / pre-IPO shape (typically 7-9 directors with a strong independent majority and an audit-committee-eligible independent set) by Series-D or Series-E.

## Independent-director selection — the mechanic

"Independent director" without a nomination-and-removal mechanic is not a meaningful term. The Voting Agreement drafts the specific mechanic. The common patterns:

**Common-elected independent.** The common stockholders vote to elect one or more independents. In practice this means the founders (who typically control the common vote) select. Investor-friendly variations may require the independent to be reasonably acceptable to the preferred.

**Preferred-elected independent.** The preferred stockholders vote to elect. Less common at Series-A for the "primary" independent seat; more common for a second or later independent.

**Jointly-elected independent.** The common and preferred voting together (as a single class) elect. Requires alignment; if the common and preferred disagree, the seat can go vacant.

**Mutually-agreed independent.** Both the common (via a founder-elected process) and the preferred (via the Series-A lead) must jointly approve. This is the "swing independent" pattern: an independent director acceptable to both sides. Requires active negotiation to fill; a common source of stalemate if the founders and the lead have different preferences.

**Search-committee-nominated independent.** A specific search committee (typically the founder-CEO, the Series-A lead, and one existing independent if any) leads a search, presents candidates, and the board (or the electing class) selects. Formalises the process.

**Existing-director-appointed independent.** The existing independent directors (once there are more than one) nominate additional independents. A common pattern for filling vacancies in later-stage boards.

The specific mechanic matters because it defines who has veto power over the independent slate. A "mutually acceptable" independent gives the founder-CEO and the Series-A lead a joint veto — either can block. A common-elected independent gives the founders control. A preferred-elected independent gives the Series-A lead control.

The market-standard pattern at Series-A is typically:

- **One founder-elected (common-elected) independent** — the founders' pick, subject to reasonableness.
- **One mutually-agreed independent** — the "swing" seat requiring both sides' agreement.

This produces a five-seat board where founders control 3 of 5 (two founder + one common-elected independent) if the swing seat is unfilled, and controls or splits depending on the swing seat's leanings if filled.

## The Voting Agreement mechanics

The Voting Agreement is the binding-in-practice document that implements the board-composition negotiation. Key mechanics:

- **Board election obligations.** Each party (common, preferred, specific series) agrees to vote its shares for the designated slate. Founders vote their common for the founder-elected seats and the common-elected independent; the Series-A lead votes its preferred for the Series-A seat.
- **Vacancy-filling mechanics.** If a founder-elected director resigns or is removed, the founders (or their designated proxy) nominate a replacement, and all parties vote for that replacement. Similar for the investor-elected and independent seats.
- **Removal mechanics.** A director may be removed with-or-without-cause by the class that elected them. A founder-elected director is removed by the common; an investor-elected director is removed by the preferred. Independent directors are typically removable by the class that elected them.
- **Change-of-CEO mechanic.** In some drafts, the "CEO seat" (typically one of the founder seats) is defined as "the person then serving as CEO." If the CEO is replaced, the seat automatically transfers to the new CEO. Founder-CEO transitions often trigger complex renegotiation of this drafting.
- **Term of the agreement.** The Voting Agreement typically terminates at IPO, on a change of control, or by mutual agreement. Some drafts have shorter or longer terms.

The CFO's coordination role at Voting Agreement drafting: ensure the mechanics implement the term sheet, ensure the vacancy-filling and removal mechanics are workable, and ensure the CEO-seat drafting handles the founder-CEO-transition case cleanly.

## The role of observers

Board observers attend board meetings, receive materials, participate in discussions, but don't vote. Observer seats are common in modern venture financings. Typical patterns:

- **Series-A lead observer.** Sometimes the Series-A lead takes an observer seat in addition to (or instead of) a director seat, particularly if the fund's investment committee prefers a lower-visibility posture.
- **Strategic co-investor observer.** Strategic investors (corporate VCs, some financial sponsors) often take observer seats rather than director seats to avoid conflict-of-interest complications.
- **Secondary Series-A investor observer.** Preferred holders who don't meet the Major Investor threshold but who invested significant amounts sometimes get observer seats as a courtesy.
- **Board-approved observer for specific purposes.** In some cases the board grants observer status to a specific individual (an advisor, a search-committee member, an interim executive) for a defined term.

Observer rights typically live in the IRA or in a specific side letter. The rights typically include: attend board meetings, receive board materials on the same schedule as directors, participate in discussions with the board's approval, and (optionally) attend executive sessions. Confidentiality and non-disclosure obligations attach.

Observer expansion is a modest ask that founders often grant without much friction, but the CFO should track the observer roster because a board meeting with three directors and five observers has a very different dynamic than one with three directors alone.

## Selecting the specific independents

The mechanic gets the mechanism right; the *specific individuals* selected as independents are the material decision.

Sourcing patterns for the first independent at Series-A:

- **Founder network.** A former CEO, founder, or executive the CEO knows and trusts. Common; the risk is a "friend of the founder" that isn't sufficiently independent to add real governance value.
- **Investor network.** The Series-A lead's introduction to a candidate the fund has worked with. Common; the risk is a "friend of the investor" that's more aligned with the preferred than nominally independent.
- **Board-advisor firms.** BoardList, theBoardlist, Him For Her, and similar firms curate slates of independent-director candidates. Emerging as a formal source, particularly for diversity-conscious searches.
- **Executive search.** Formal search firms (True, Daversa, Heidrick, Spencer Stuart, and specialised smaller firms) run director searches. Common at Series-B and later; less common at Series-A due to cost.
- **CEO peer network.** Introductions from other founder-CEOs to their independent directors, or to candidates who have served on similar-stage boards.

Selection criteria to consider for each independent seat:

- **Domain expertise.** Sector knowledge, functional expertise (finance, operations, sales, engineering).
- **Prior board experience.** Independents with prior director experience are typically more effective than first-time directors.
- **Independence dimensions.** Financial independence (no material investment in the company or the investors), operational independence (not a customer, supplier, or partner), governance independence (not affiliated with the founders or the investors).
- **Diversity of perspective.** Gender, race, background, geography. Board diversity is both a governance-quality issue and increasingly an IPO-readiness issue as some exchanges and states have imposed board-diversity requirements or disclosure requirements.
- **Availability.** Independent directors who serve on many boards may not have the bandwidth for active governance.
- **Fit with the founder-CEO and the Series-A lead.** The independent has to work productively with both.

Compensation for independent directors at Series-A is typically equity-based:

- **0.25%-0.75% of the fully-diluted cap table** as a stock grant, vesting over 2-4 years.
- **Annual cash retainer** in some cases ($25K-$75K per year); more common at Series-B and later.
- **Meeting fees** occasionally; increasingly rare.
- **Expense reimbursement** universal.

The specific comp for the first independent at Series-A is a live negotiation with the individual director. The board (or the compensation committee once formed) sets it.

## Board vs. Board Observer vs. Advisor

A useful mental separation:

- **Board director.** Voting member; fiduciary duties to the corporation; participates in all board decisions.
- **Board observer.** Non-voting attendee at board meetings; may have information rights but does not have fiduciary duties.
- **Advisor.** Not part of the board structure. Advises the CEO or specific executives on specific topics; may have a compensation arrangement (equity, cash, or both); does not have fiduciary duties or automatic information access.

Founders frequently confuse these — an "advisor" and an "observer" and a "director" are three different things with three different roles and three different compensation ranges. The Voting Agreement and IRA draft the board director and observer roles; advisor arrangements are typically documented in separate advisor agreements outside the financing documents.

## The negotiation posture on board composition

For a Series-A term sheet:

- **Push for a five-seat board with 2 founder + 1 Series-A + 2 independents.** This is the market-standard and the founder-favourable position.
- **Accept 3-seat boards only when the Series-A cheque is small** and the seat expansion doesn't make sense. Even then, negotiate for the third seat to be an independent (not the CEO by default).
- **Push for at least one founder-elected (common-elected) independent.** Preserves founder influence over the swing vote.
- **Push for a mutually-agreed independent as the second independent seat** rather than a preferred-elected independent. The joint-approval mechanic preserves the swing.
- **Refuse or negotiate hard against boards that give the Series-A lead a "swing vote"** — e.g., a three-seat board of 1 founder + 1 Series-A + 1 preferred-elected independent gives the preferred 2 of 3 seats and effective control.
- **Refuse "consent to CEO change" clauses in the Voting Agreement.** These aren't strictly about board composition but affect it; they give the preferred a veto on the founder-CEO succession.
- **Coordinate with counsel on the Voting Agreement drafting** — specifically the vacancy-filling and removal mechanics for the "mutually-agreed" independent, which is the most complex mechanic to draft.
- **Have candidate independents in mind before term-sheet signing.** The CFO who arrives at Series-A signing without a shortlist of independent candidates ends up in a scramble in the weeks after closing.

For Series-B and later:

- **Preserve at least one founder seat.** If the negotiation compresses to one founder director (typically the CEO), so be it — but don't accept a board with zero founder seats.
- **Negotiate the specific structure of the Series-B seat.** New seat (expanding the board) is preferable to seat re-allocation (replacing a founder seat or an independent).
- **Grow the independent-director count** as the board grows. A board with more independents than investors is founder-favourable.
- **Consider committee formation** (audit committee, compensation committee) at Series-B or Series-C. Committees are typically composed of independent directors and provide governance depth.

## Common founder traps

- **Accepting a 3-seat board with 1 founder + 1 investor + 1 CEO-position independent without thinking through the veto structure.** If the third seat is preferred-elected, the founder is a 1-of-3 vote and can be overruled on any board matter.
- **Accepting "mutually-agreed independent" seats without a sourcing plan.** The seat can sit vacant for months or years if the two sides can't agree on a candidate.
- **Not defining "independent" clearly.** An independent director who has a consulting arrangement with the Series-A lead is not independent in substance. Draft to the specific independence definition.
- **Accepting a "preferred-elected" second independent.** Effectively gives the preferred two board seats.
- **Not planning for the Series-B board evolution.** Founders sometimes negotiate the Series-A board to a specific balance and then are surprised when Series-B reshapes it.
- **Missing observer-seat expansion.** Observers are non-voting but attend meetings; a board with too many observers becomes unwieldy and loses the executive-session function.
- **Not coordinating with the Voting Agreement drafting.** Board composition on the term sheet is one thing; the specific vote-obligation and vacancy-filling mechanics in the Voting Agreement matter as much.
- **Accepting "consent to CEO change" in the Voting Agreement.** Gives the preferred an effective veto on the founder-CEO succession.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Negotiates the Series-A board to the 2 founder + 1 investor + 2 independent pattern (or the closest available shape).
- Ensures the second independent is either common-elected or mutually-agreed rather than preferred-elected.
- Has a sourcing plan for the independent director(s) before term-sheet signing.
- Coordinates the Voting Agreement drafting to implement the negotiated composition with workable vacancy-filling and removal mechanics.
- Refuses "consent to CEO change" and similar veto clauses that would compromise the founder-CEO succession.
- Plans for the Series-B board evolution and negotiates the transition rather than being surprised by it.
- Tracks observers separately from directors and manages the observer roster to preserve board meeting effectiveness.
- Coordinates independent-director compensation with the compensation committee (or the board) once formed.

## Summary

- Board composition is the single most consequential governance term in a term sheet. It defines who has voting authority on the corporation's day-to-day decisions and is difficult to change once agreed.
- The current-market Series-A board is typically five seats: 2 founder + 1 Series-A + 2 independents. Three-seat boards are common at smaller Series-A financings; larger boards appear at Series-B and beyond.
- Independent-director selection is drafted in the Voting Agreement. The mechanic (common-elected, preferred-elected, jointly-elected, mutually-agreed) defines who has veto power over the independent slate.
- The Voting Agreement implements the composition with binding vote-for obligations, vacancy-filling mechanics, and removal mechanics.
- Board observers attend meetings without voting and are common for strategic investors, secondary Series-A holders, and specific-purpose attendees.
- The CFO's negotiation posture at Series-A: 5-seat 2/1/2 board, at least one common-elected independent, second independent mutually-agreed, no "consent to CEO change" clause.
- Series-B and later board evolution compresses founder seats, adds investor seats, and grows the independent count. Negotiate the transition at each round.
- Independent-director sourcing is a real work stream that starts before term-sheet signing. Founder network, investor network, board-advisor firms, and executive search all serve; sourcing planning avoids post-close scrambles.

Chapter 8 turns to the "quiet" clauses — drag-along, tag-along, redemption, dividends, MFN — that get less attention in a CEO's line-by-line review but have real consequences at exit, in a down round, or in a bridge financing.
