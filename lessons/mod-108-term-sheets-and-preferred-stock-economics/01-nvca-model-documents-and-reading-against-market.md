# The NVCA Model Documents — Reading a Term Sheet Against the Industry Reference

## Why this matters

A Series-A term sheet is not drafted from scratch. It is drafted from an industry reference — the National Venture Capital Association's Model Legal Documents — that most US venture-financing counsel keep open in another tab. Every experienced deal lawyer starts from the same base and marks up the specific deal against it. If the CFO doesn't have the same reference open, the CFO is negotiating against a document whose default position they can't see and whose deviations they can't name.

The NVCA reference has two consequences for the CFO's reading discipline:

- **Every clause has a default position.** When counsel writes "1x non-participating preferred," "broad-based weighted-average anti-dilution," "board of five: two founder, one investor, two independents," they are not inventing — they are copying the NVCA model with specific parameter values. A CFO who knows the model can read a term sheet in ten minutes and identify which clauses match the model, which deviate slightly, and which are custom drafting.
- **Every deviation has a name.** A term sheet with "1x participating with a 3x cap" is not just "aggressive." It is a specific deviation from the NVCA-default 1x non-participating structure, has a known cost profile, appears with a known frequency in the current-quarter deal-terms data, and has a known negotiation response. The CFO's job is to identify the deviation, price it, and hand counsel a targeted red-line rather than a wholesale rewrite.

This chapter walks the seven NVCA model documents, what each governs, how counsel uses them, and the "market-standard vs. negotiated vs. aggressive vs. unusual" classification that structures every subsequent chapter in the module.

## The seven NVCA model documents

The NVCA publishes its Model Legal Documents freely at [nvca.org/model-legal-documents/](https://nvca.org/model-legal-documents/). The set is maintained by a working group of NVCA member-firm counsel and is refreshed periodically as market practice moves. The current set comprises seven documents that together produce a full Series-A preferred-stock financing.

**1. Term Sheet.** The non-binding summary of the economic and governance terms of the round. Two to four pages of numbered clauses covering: security type (preferred class, class name), amount raised, pre-money valuation, price per share, capitalisation table before and after, dividend, liquidation preference, voting, protective provisions, mandatory conversion, anti-dilution, redemption, drag-along, pro-rata rights, right of first refusal, co-sale, information rights, registration rights, board composition, employee vesting, D&O insurance, no-shop, expenses, conditions to close, and confidentiality. This is the document the CFO negotiates. Every one of the remaining six documents implements a subset of the terms agreed in the term sheet.

**2. Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA).** The definitive document under which the preferred stock is actually issued and paid for. Covers: purchase and sale mechanics (price, closing, subsequent closings), representations and warranties of the company (organisation, capitalisation, IP, litigation, employees, tax, financial statements, environmental — a long list), representations of the investors (accredited-investor status, investment intent), covenants (pre-closing and post-closing), conditions to closing (opinions, consents, board approval), indemnification, and miscellaneous. The SPA is where the company's rep-and-warranty exposure lives; the CFO owns coordinating the disclosure schedules that qualify the reps.

**3. Investors' Rights Agreement (IRA).** The bundle of ongoing rights the preferred investors have after the closing that are not built into the corporate charter or the shareholder-vote mechanics. Covers: registration rights (demand, S-3, piggyback — the ability to force the company to register the investors' shares for public sale), information rights (annual audited, quarterly and monthly unaudited, budget, inspection rights, standard investor updates), pro-rata / preemptive rights (the right to purchase a proportional share of future securities), management-rights letter provisions where needed for VCOC compliance, "confidentiality" and standstill provisions in some drafts, "market stand-off" (the lock-up around IPO), and specific IPO-cooperation covenants.

**4. Right of First Refusal / Co-Sale Agreement (ROFR/Co-Sale).** The mechanics that govern founder and key-holder secondary transfers. If a founder wants to sell their shares to a third party, the company has a first right to buy them (the ROFR) and, if the company declines, the preferred investors have a first right to buy them (the secondary ROFR); if the preferred investors also decline, they have a right to sell alongside the founder proportionally (co-sale) so that the founder cannot exit unilaterally while the investors are locked in. Also typically governs transfers to permitted transferees (family members, estate planning) that are exempt from the ROFR/co-sale.

**5. Voting Agreement.** The contract by which the shareholders agree to vote for the board composition and certain other matters agreed in the term sheet. Covers: board election (which parties nominate which board seats, how independent directors are selected), drag-along (the obligation of the minority to vote for and sell into an approved sale of the company), and, in some drafts, specific "vote-for" obligations on other matters (e.g., votes to elect the CEO's designee, votes on charter amendments implementing later-round terms). This is where the board-composition mechanics of chapter 7 live in binding form.

**6. Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation (Model Certificate of Incorporation).** The corporate charter that creates the preferred class and encodes its rights: dividend rate and mechanics, liquidation preference (amount and participation), voting rights (per-share and class), conversion rights (optional and mandatory), anti-dilution (the formula), redemption, protective provisions (the class-vote list), and the mechanics of how future series are stacked (senior, pari-passu, or junior). This is the load-bearing document — if the charter doesn't say it, the investor doesn't have it. Everything in the term sheet under "liquidation," "anti-dilution," "voting," "conversion," "protective provisions," and "dividends" ends up implemented in the charter.

**7. Management Rights Letter.** A short letter granted to funds that need it for Venture Capital Operating Company (VCOC) compliance under ERISA. The fund gets the right to consult with management, receive information, and (in some drafts) inspect facilities, in order to qualify the fund's investment as a "venture capital investment" under the Department of Labor regulations and avoid the fund's assets being treated as "plan assets." Most institutional venture funds request this letter; it is short and rarely negotiated substantively, but the CFO should know why it is being asked for and should not accept overreach language beyond the minimum VCOC threshold.

## How counsel uses the reference

The mechanical workflow inside a well-run deal is:

- **Investor counsel drafts first.** The lead investor's counsel typically produces the first draft of the term sheet, sometimes based on a template the lead firm maintains but marked with the specific deal parameters. The term sheet is deliberately kept short (2-4 pages) and non-binding so that the substantive negotiation happens quickly and the definitive-document drafting proceeds only after the material terms are locked.
- **Company counsel marks up against the NVCA model.** Company counsel receives the draft, opens the NVCA term sheet and Model Certificate of Incorporation on the second screen, and marks up the specific clauses where the received draft deviates from the model. The mark-up is not "reject everything that's not NVCA" — the NVCA is the starting point, not the finish line — but "identify deviations, understand them, and negotiate the ones that matter."
- **The CFO does the same read in parallel.** The CFO produces an internal red-line memo (chapter-by-chapter classification of clauses against the model) and negotiates the economic and governance clauses with the lead investor while counsel handles the legal drafting mechanics. The CFO does not need to draft the charter; the CFO needs to know what the charter *will say* if a given term-sheet clause is agreed.
- **The term sheet is negotiated and signed.** Non-binding except for the "confidentiality," "no-shop," and "expenses" clauses; but the executed term sheet is the binding-in-practice document for all subsequent drafting. Deviating from an executed term sheet during definitive-document drafting is a serious signalling event; counsel drafts to the term sheet, not to their own preferences.
- **Definitive documents drafted, negotiated, signed, closed.** The SPA, IRA, ROFR/Co-Sale, Voting Agreement, restated Certificate of Incorporation, and Management Rights Letter are drafted to implement the executed term sheet. Most of the definitive-document work is drafting mechanics that don't require the CFO's attention; the CFO reviews the definitive documents against the term sheet for consistency and against the disclosure schedules for accuracy of the reps.

The load-bearing point: the executed term sheet is 90%+ of the negotiation. Every subsequent document implements it. A CFO who negotiates the term sheet against the NVCA reference does most of the substantive work up front.

## The four-tier classification

Reading a received term sheet against the NVCA reference produces a natural four-tier classification for every clause:

**Tier 1 — Market-standard.** The clause matches the NVCA model with parameters at or very near the current-market defaults. A "1x non-participating liquidation preference" with senior-to-common (but pari-passu with any prior series if this is not the first round) at Series-A is market-standard. A "broad-based weighted-average" anti-dilution provision is market-standard. Most of a well-drafted Series-A term sheet from an experienced institutional lead falls in this tier. The CFO's response: accept.

**Tier 2 — Negotiated but market.** The clause is within the range of things one sees in the current market, but the specific parameters are up for negotiation. The board composition (five vs. seven seats, one independent vs. two, timing of the second independent), the protective-provisions list (which specific items are on the class-consent list), pro-rata rights (which investors get them, in what form), dividend rate (0%, non-cumulative 6%, non-cumulative 8%), and mandatory-conversion threshold (IPO price × N and IPO gross of $Y). These are the clauses where the CFO's read of the current-quarter data and the specific deal dynamics produces the counter.

**Tier 3 — Aggressive.** The clause is inside the market envelope but at the investor-favourable end, and it appears with materially lower frequency than the market-standard version. A 1x participating preferred (increasingly rare at Series-A in founder-favourable markets; more common in stressed markets and later stages). A narrow-based weighted-average anti-dilution. A protective-provisions list that includes many operating decisions (annual budget, individual hires below the officer level, sub-$500K contracts). The CFO's response: identify, price the difference from market, and push back with specific counter-language.

**Tier 4 — Unusual.** The clause is outside typical market practice. A full-ratchet anti-dilution. A redemption right that is short-fuse (five years) and unqualified. A "sitting-out-a-round means loss of protective-provision vote" clause. A drag-along that fires at a low threshold (>50% of preferred rather than >50% of preferred and majority of common). These are the clauses that a founder cannot accept as-is without a specific and documented reason. The CFO's response: push hard for removal or wholesale restructure; escalate to the lead partner if company counsel is not making progress.

The classification is not fixed forever — market drifts. A clause that was "aggressive" in a founder-favourable 2021 market becomes "negotiated but market" in a stressed 2023 market. Chapter 9 handles reading the current-quarter position; every classification in chapters 2-8 assumes a "typical" post-vintage market and points out where the classification shifts under stress.

## The specific NVCA-vs.-drafted difference

To make the classification concrete, here are the deviation patterns the CFO most often sees:

**Liquidation preference (charter § on liquidation).** NVCA model: 1x non-participating, senior to junior series and to common. Common deviations: participating; capped participation; different multiples (2x, 3x); explicit senior-vs.-pari-passu language across series. Chapter 2.

**Anti-dilution (charter § on conversion price adjustments).** NVCA model: broad-based weighted-average. Common deviations: narrow-based weighted-average; full ratchet; pay-to-play carve-outs; specific inclusions/exclusions from the anti-dilution trigger (option grants at fair market value, warrants issued in connection with debt, etc.). Chapter 4.

**Protective provisions (charter § on class vote).** NVCA model: a specific list of about 10-14 items requiring class vote. Common deviations: additional items (annual budget, incurrence of debt above a threshold, hiring of any officer at or above VP level); different-percentage-required class votes (majority of preferred vs. supermajority); protective provisions that survive conversion to common at a lower valuation. Chapter 6.

**Board composition (voting agreement).** NVCA-typical Series-A pattern: five directors, comprising two founder directors, one Series-A director, and two independents (one chosen by common, one by joint agreement of common and Series-A). Common deviations: three-director boards (founder + investor + CEO); different independent-director selection mechanics; observer rights without director seats; class-vote requirements for future board changes. Chapter 7.

**Registration rights (IRA § on registration).** NVCA model: two demand registrations after a certain lock-up, S-3 registrations after S-3 eligibility, piggyback rights subject to underwriter cutbacks. Common deviations: more or fewer demands, specific "shelf-take-down" mechanics, expense allocation. This is a clause the CFO glances at; it matters at IPO, not now, and is typically drafted very close to the NVCA form.

**Redemption (charter § on redemption).** NVCA model: optional redemption after five years at the original purchase price plus accrued dividends. Common deviations: shorter fuses, mandatory redemption, redemption at a premium to purchase price, "put right" on change-of-control-adjacent events. Chapter 8.

**Drag-along, ROFR/co-sale, dividends.** All have NVCA defaults; deviations are covered in chapters 6 and 8.

## What the CFO produces after reading the term sheet

The output of the read-against-NVCA discipline is an internal red-line memo, typically 2-3 pages, that has the following structure:

- **Bottom-line summary.** One-paragraph read: "The received term sheet is largely market-standard with three specific deviations to negotiate: (1) 1x participating preferred → push to 1x non-participating; (2) protective-provisions list includes 21 items → target 12-14; (3) board composition specifies three seats (founder + investor + CEO) → target five with two independents."
- **Clause-by-clause table.** Rows: each numbered term-sheet clause. Columns: (a) NVCA-model default; (b) received-draft language; (c) classification (market / negotiated / aggressive / unusual); (d) proposed counter-language; (e) priority tier for negotiation (1 = must-have, 2 = strong preference, 3 = worth surfacing but concede if needed).
- **The three-to-five negotiation asks.** The specific handful of clauses where the counter matters. A term-sheet negotiation that tries to red-line every clause loses momentum and signals inexperience; a negotiation that surfaces three-to-five real asks and concedes cleanly on the rest gets closed.
- **The market-conditions context.** One paragraph anchoring the negotiation posture in the current-quarter data — "Fenwick's most recent survey shows participating preferred at X% incidence, up from Y% four quarters ago; this deviation reflects the market drift but is still on the investor-favourable side of the median" — with the specific data cited so counsel and the CEO can defend the counter in real time.

The memo goes to the CEO for alignment before the negotiation opens, to counsel as the input to the term-sheet red-line, and to the board (or the lead-investor CEO peer group, if the founder-CEO has one) for reality-checking.

## Common founder traps

- **Not reading the NVCA model before receiving the term sheet.** By the time the term sheet arrives, the negotiation is already time-pressured; the founder who has not read the NVCA reference beforehand can't classify the clauses on the timeline available.
- **Treating counsel's mark-up as the negotiation.** Counsel red-lines against legal precision; the CFO negotiates against economic and governance consequence. These are different jobs, and the CFO's absence from the negotiation frequently produces a legally-tight but economically-costly term sheet.
- **Trying to red-line every clause.** A term-sheet negotiation is a signalling event. Founders who send back a document with every clause marked up look inexperienced and lose the lead's respect. Pick the three-to-five that matter.
- **Accepting a term sheet without the market-conditions context.** A clause that is "aggressive" in a founder-favourable market may be "market-standard" in a stressed market. The negotiation posture depends on the current-quarter position, not on the NVCA-default in the abstract.
- **Ignoring the definitive-document drafting stage.** The executed term sheet drives 90% of the negotiation, but the remaining 10% happens in the SPA, IRA, ROFR/Co-Sale, and Voting Agreement drafting. Founders who "hand it to counsel and disengage" sometimes discover surprises in the charter or the IRA that weren't apparent from the term sheet.
- **Not obtaining Management Rights Letters from funds that need them.** Some funds cannot close without the MRL; the CFO who does not surface this early can delay the closing.
- **Assuming the NVCA form is neutral.** It is a *reference*, not a *neutral*. Historically the NVCA model has leaned slightly investor-favourable on some clauses (protective provisions in particular). "Market-standard against the NVCA" is not the same as "founder-optimal"; it is a starting point for negotiation, not the finish line.

## What good looks like

A CFO or fundraising-finance lead who has this material installed:

- Keeps the current-vintage NVCA model documents open when reading any received term sheet.
- Produces the clause-by-clause classification memo within 48 hours of receipt.
- Surfaces three-to-five negotiation asks to the CEO and counsel and concedes cleanly on the rest.
- References current-quarter Fenwick / Wilson Sonsini / Carta data (chapter 9) in the counter.
- Coordinates the disclosure-schedule drafting with counsel and the ops team so that the SPA reps are qualified accurately.
- Reviews the definitive documents against the executed term sheet and flags any drift.
- Knows why each fund is asking for its Management Rights Letter and does not accept overreach language.

## Summary

- The NVCA Model Legal Documents are the industry-reference stack for a US Series-A preferred-stock financing: the term sheet, the SPA, the IRA, the ROFR / Co-Sale Agreement, the Voting Agreement, the Model Certificate of Incorporation, and the Management Rights Letter. Every experienced deal counsel drafts against them.
- The executed term sheet is 90% of the negotiation; the definitive documents implement it. The CFO's leverage is at the term-sheet stage.
- Every clause in a received term sheet can be classified against the NVCA reference into four tiers — market-standard, negotiated but market, aggressive, unusual — and the classification structures the negotiation posture.
- The CFO's output is a 2-3 page internal red-line memo with a bottom-line summary, a clause-by-clause table, three-to-five priority asks, and the current-quarter market-conditions context.
- The classification is not fixed; market drifts. Aggressive-in-2021 clauses can become market-in-2023 stressed environments. Chapter 9 handles the current-quarter read that keeps the classification honest.

Chapter 2 turns to the specific mechanics of the liquidation-preference clause — the largest single economic term in the charter, the clause with the widest possible spread between "market-standard" and "aggressive," and the clause whose consequences are hardest for the founder to see without the waterfall math that chapter 3 introduces.
