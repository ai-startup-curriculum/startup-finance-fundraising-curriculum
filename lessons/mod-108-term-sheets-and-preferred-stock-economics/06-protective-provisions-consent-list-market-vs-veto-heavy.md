# Protective Provisions — The Consent List, Market vs. Veto-Heavy

## Why this matters

Protective provisions are the enumerated list of corporate actions that require the affirmative consent of a defined preferred class (or majority of preferred) before the company may take them. They live in the Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation, and they create a class vote — separate from the board vote and the shareholder vote — that gives the preferred a specific veto over specific decisions.

Two things make the clause structurally important to the CFO:

- **The list defines the scope of the preferred's veto.** A short, market-standard list of ~10-14 items (sale of the company, changes to the preferred's rights, big debt, new senior securities, redemption, dividends, dissolution) gives the preferred protection over decisions that materially affect their investment without giving them a role in day-to-day operations. A long, veto-heavy list of 20-30 items (annual budget, individual officer hires, sub-$500K contracts, any hire at the VP level, changes to the option plan) gives the preferred effective operational control.
- **Protective provisions bind the company via class vote, not board vote.** Even if the board (which the founder-CEO or a company-friendly majority may control) votes to approve an action, the class vote can veto it. Protective provisions therefore give the preferred a direct check on the board that survives changes in board composition. This is the reason the specific list matters so much: it defines the scope of a governance right that cannot be diluted by adding more independent directors or otherwise reshaping the board.

This chapter walks the NVCA-standard protective-provisions list, the incremental items that expand the list, the drafting patterns that shift the balance of power, and the CFO's response to an over-broad list.

## The NVCA-standard protective-provisions list

The Model Certificate of Incorporation provides a bracketed list of approximately 10-14 items requiring class-vote consent. The exact list varies slightly by NVCA vintage, but the current-market cluster comprises the following:

**Corporate-existence and structural actions:**

1. **Amend, alter, or repeal any provision of the Certificate of Incorporation or Bylaws** in a way that adversely affects the preferred (either that specific series or preferred generally).
2. **Liquidate, dissolve, or wind up the affairs of the corporation** or effect any Deemed Liquidation Event (which includes many sales / mergers / asset sales).
3. **Effect any merger, acquisition, sale of substantially all assets, or other change of control transaction** — the "sale of the company" veto.
4. **Reclassify, alter, or amend any existing security** of the corporation in a way that senior-ranks it or otherwise adversely affects the preferred.

**Class-related actions:**

5. **Create, authorise, or issue any new class or series of stock ranking senior to or pari passu with the preferred** with respect to liquidation preference, dividends, or voting.
6. **Increase or decrease the authorised number of shares of Preferred Stock** (or of the specific series).
7. **Redeem, repurchase, or otherwise acquire shares of the corporation** other than pursuant to standard equity-plan repurchase mechanics.
8. **Pay or declare any dividend or distribution** on any securities (with defined exceptions).

**Financial and structural actions:**

9. **Incur indebtedness in excess of a defined threshold** (typically $500K to a few million dollars, depending on stage).
10. **Enter into or amend any transaction with a founder, officer, director, or affiliate** (the related-party-transaction check).
11. **Change the size of the board of directors.**

**Optional additions in some drafts (the tier-2 "sometimes-market" cluster):**

12. **Change the principal business of the corporation** in a material way.
13. **Sell, lease, license, or otherwise dispose of a material asset** outside the ordinary course.
14. **Hire or terminate officers at or above a defined level** (typically CEO, CFO, or all C-level).

That is broadly the NVCA-standard list. Every item is defensible as protecting the preferred's investment against dilution of rights, unauthorised risk-taking, or self-dealing. None of the items give the preferred day-to-day operational control.

## The scope of the class vote

Two drafting parameters govern how the class vote is exercised:

**Whose vote is required?** Options in the drafting:

- **Majority of the preferred (all series voting together).** Most preferred-friendly for the company because a single class vote spans series.
- **Separate class vote by series.** Each series (Series A, Series B, Series C) has its own veto. More restrictive; a Series C investor with a small stake can veto a decision the majority of preferred supports.
- **Supermajority (66.7% or 75%) of preferred.** Rare, and typically only for specific items like "change the size of the board" — the drafting sometimes distinguishes different thresholds for different items.

**The specific-series-vote is a real economic ask.** A Series B lead who negotiates a separate Series-B class vote (in addition to the pari-passu class vote of all preferred) has veto power over specific items even when the majority of preferred (Series A + Series B combined) doesn't oppose. This is a common ask by growth-stage lead investors and should be treated as tier-3 aggressive at Series-A but tier-2 negotiated at Series-B and later.

## The veto-heavy overreach patterns

An aggressive term sheet may propose additions beyond the NVCA-standard list. The specific patterns:

**Operational-decision additions.** Items like:

- Approve the annual budget or business plan.
- Approve any hire above a defined base-salary level (e.g., >$200K salary).
- Approve any expenditure or capital commitment above a low threshold (e.g., $250K).
- Approve any contract with a customer or vendor above a defined size.
- Approve or modify the equity incentive plan (individual grants or plan-level).
- Approve marketing spend, R&D spend, or any specific line item of the operating budget.
- Approve any change to the corporate structure (subsidiaries, foreign subsidiaries, joint ventures).

These items should be tier-3 or tier-4 by default. They cross the line from "protect the investment" to "control the operation." The CFO's counter is to strip them or to move them to a *board-level* approval requirement (rather than class-vote), where the founder-controlled or independent-heavy board can approve without a class-consent step.

**Structural additions that shift board leverage.**

- **Consent to changes in the CEO** — either termination or appointment of the CEO. Very aggressive; effectively gives the preferred a veto on the CEO decision. Sometimes appears as a "consent to appointment of any officer" clause.
- **Consent to changes in the CFO or key executives** — similar to CEO consent.
- **Consent to changes in the corporate books and records** — the "we can veto changes to how we monitor you" clause.
- **Consent to any action outside the ordinary course** — undefined "ordinary course" gives the preferred wide discretion. Push for specific enumerated items rather than a catch-all.

**Structural additions that expand class-vote scope.**

- **A separate class vote for each new series** rather than a combined preferred class vote. Discussed above.
- **A separate class vote for each holder above a threshold** — extreme; effectively individual-veto power. Almost never appears in institutional Series-A but has been seen in one-off strategic-investor deals.
- **A "veto by any preferred holder representing more than 10%"** — similarly extreme, hands veto power to any large minority holder.

## The "specific-series vote" pattern in later rounds

By Series B and beyond, the drafting question of "combined preferred vs. separate class vote by series" becomes actively negotiated. The Series B lead often wants a separate Series-B class vote (in addition to the pari-passu combined vote) to protect its specific investment against actions supported by Series A + Series B combined majority but that specifically disadvantage Series B.

Some specific-series-vote items that appear:

- **Amendments to the Certificate that affect the Series B specifically** — nearly universal, and defensible.
- **Sale of the company at a price below a defined threshold** — the "no-forced-exit-below-my-preference" veto. Series B lead's tier-2 ask; not universally granted.
- **Issuance of a Series C ranking senior to Series B** — defensible; the Series B doesn't want its position subordinated.
- **All the standard NVCA items, but voted separately** — aggressive; effectively doubles the veto surface.

The CFO's job at Series-B is to negotiate the specific-series items down to the ones that materially affect the Series B specifically, rather than accepting a duplicative full-list class vote.

## Interaction with pay-to-play

If the round has a pay-to-play clause (chapter 4), non-participating preferred that gets converted to common under hard pay-to-play loses its protective-provision votes along with its anti-dilution and other rights. This is a mechanism for shaking loose the class vote of investors who don't participate in the down round.

Under soft pay-to-play, the specific interaction depends on drafting — most soft pay-to-play retains the protective-provision votes for the non-participating preferred, but strips only the anti-dilution. Some hybrid drafts strip both. The CFO who has a pay-to-play clause on the table should read the interaction with protective provisions carefully with counsel.

## The consequences of an over-broad list

An over-broad protective-provisions list has second-order consequences that the CFO should anticipate:

- **Operational drag.** Every decision on the list requires class-vote coordination, which means notifying and getting consent from a class of investors distributed across multiple funds. Even for straightforward decisions, the class-vote process adds days to weeks of process. If the list includes items like "annual budget" or "hiring above X salary," the drag accumulates.
- **De-facto board control.** With a broad protective-provisions list, the board becomes advisory rather than decisional on the items the preferred vetoes. The founder's ability to build a founder-friendly board is undermined because the preferred has parallel authority.
- **Blocking coalitions at exit.** A protective-provisions veto on "sale of the company" is universal and defensible, but if the drafting is broad — e.g., "sale at any price" or "sale requiring supermajority preferred consent" — a small dissenting fund can block an otherwise-desirable exit and force a negotiation with the dissenting fund that costs value.
- **Difficulty raising subsequent rounds.** The next-round lead sees the protective provisions and may demand equivalent or expanded rights, compounding the veto surface.
- **Down-round pain.** Combined with pay-to-play mechanics, a broad protective-provisions list gives sitting-out investors extensive leverage in the down-round negotiation.

## The CFO's negotiation approach

For a received term sheet with a protective-provisions list:

- **Read the list against the NVCA-standard cluster.** Every item in the received list should be identifiable as market (in the NVCA-standard cluster), negotiated (in the tier-2 optional additions), aggressive (an operational-decision addition), or unusual (a structural overreach).
- **Push all operational-decision items to the board.** "We agree the board should approve the annual budget; we don't agree the class of preferred should have a separate class vote on it. Delete from the class-vote list; add to the board-approval mechanic if it's not already there."
- **Refuse individual-veto structures.** No single holder should have a veto; the class-vote should require a majority (or supermajority) of the class.
- **Negotiate the class-vote threshold.** Majority of preferred is standard; supermajority is a real ask that should be limited to specific items.
- **Coordinate the specific-series vote at Series-B.** Limit specific-Series-B items to those that materially affect Series B specifically.
- **Coordinate the interaction with the board composition** (chapter 7) — if the board is founder-controlled or independent-heavy, the protective provisions matter more; if the board is investor-controlled, they matter less.
- **Cross-reference the "Deemed Liquidation Event" definition** in the charter. The sale-of-company veto covers whatever the charter defines as a Deemed Liquidation Event, so a broad definition (asset sales, licences, exclusive distribution deals, majority-share sales) means a broader veto than a narrow one.

## Where protective provisions live in the definitive documents

Protective provisions are drafted in the **Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation** (specifically in the section defining the rights of the preferred class, usually §5 or §6 depending on drafting convention). They are *not* in the Investors' Rights Agreement (which handles registration rights, information rights, and pro-rata), and they are not in the Voting Agreement (which handles board-election obligations and drag-along).

This charter-level placement matters because:

- Protective provisions are enforceable against the corporation directly; they don't depend on any specific shareholder's separate agreement.
- Amendments to the protective-provisions list themselves require a class vote (via item 1 in the NVCA-standard list — "amend the Certificate in a way that adversely affects the preferred"). Once agreed, the list is sticky.
- The list runs with the preferred class rather than any specific holder; a secondary purchaser of preferred inherits the same rights.

## Documenting the CFO's counter

The CFO's negotiation memo on protective provisions should:

- **List each proposed item** in the received term sheet's list.
- **Classify each item** (market / tier-2 / aggressive / unusual).
- **For aggressive and unusual items, propose specific counter-language** — either strike the item or move it to the board-approval mechanic.
- **Cite the NVCA-standard cluster** as the reference for market classification.
- **Provide the market-conditions anchor** from the current-quarter Fenwick / Wilson Sonsini data on protective-provisions list length.
- **Bundle the negotiation** — protective provisions are typically negotiated as a package rather than item-by-item, so the counter should propose the full desired list rather than one-off strikes.

## Common founder traps

- **Not reading the specific list.** Founders sometimes accept protective provisions as a category ("standard investor protections") without reading the specific list. The list length and specific items matter enormously.
- **Missing operational-decision additions.** Items like "approve the annual budget" or "approve hires above X salary" are common overreach that founders don't push back on because they sound reasonable in the abstract.
- **Not coordinating with board composition.** A broad protective-provisions list plus a founder-controlled board is manageable; a broad list plus an investor-controlled board leaves the founder with limited operational latitude.
- **Accepting supermajority thresholds without cause.** Supermajority (66.7% or 75%) is stricter than majority. It should be limited to specific items rather than applied across the list.
- **Not negotiating the specific-series vote at Series-B.** Series-B leads often ask for full separate-class-vote on all items; the founder-favourable response is to limit specific-series items to those that materially affect Series-B specifically.
- **Missing the interaction with the Deemed Liquidation Event definition.** A broad DLE definition expands the sale-of-company veto's scope; a narrow one contains it. Draft carefully.
- **Not tracking cumulative accumulation across rounds.** By Series-D, if each series added a few specific items to the list, the aggregate protective-provisions list can be very broad even if each individual round's additions felt reasonable at the time.

## What good looks like

A CFO who has this material installed:

- Reads the protective-provisions list against the NVCA-standard cluster on every term sheet.
- Classifies each item into the four tiers and proposes specific counter-language for aggressive and unusual items.
- Coordinates with counsel on the class-vote threshold (majority vs. supermajority) and the combined-vs.-separate-series-vote structure.
- Maintains a live protective-provisions inventory across rounds and flags accumulation before Series-C and later.
- Cross-references the Deemed Liquidation Event definition and the pay-to-play interaction.
- Frames the negotiation as a package with a defensible target list rather than as one-off strikes.

## Summary

- Protective provisions are the enumerated list of decisions requiring preferred class-vote consent. They live in the Certificate of Incorporation and create a class-vote check separate from the board vote and shareholder vote.
- The NVCA-standard list is approximately 10-14 items covering corporate-existence changes, changes to preferred rights, senior-securities issuance, redemption, dividends, major debt, related-party transactions, sale of the company, and dissolution.
- The tier-2 "sometimes-market" additions include changes to the principal business, disposal of material assets outside the ordinary course, and hiring or terminating officers at defined levels.
- Aggressive additions include operational-decision items (annual budget, individual hires below the officer level, sub-threshold contracts, marketing spend, equity plan modifications).
- Unusual additions include structural overreach (CEO consent, individual-holder veto, catch-all "outside ordinary course" clauses, single-holder veto).
- The class-vote threshold (majority vs. supermajority) and the combined-vs.-separate-series-vote structure are actively negotiated at Series-B and beyond.
- The CFO's counter is a package: strike aggressive and unusual items, move operational-decision items to the board-approval mechanic, and limit specific-series votes to items that materially affect that series specifically.
- Broad protective-provisions lists produce operational drag, de-facto board control by the preferred, blocking-coalition risk at exit, and difficulty in subsequent-round negotiation.

Chapter 7 turns to board composition — the seed board, the Series-A board, the Series-B evolution, and the mechanics of independent-director selection under the Voting Agreement — which sits alongside protective provisions as the second half of the governance package a term sheet negotiates.
